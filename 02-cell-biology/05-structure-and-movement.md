# Structure and Movement: Cytoskeleton, Centrosome, Vacuoles, Cilia, Flagella

## Why it matters

Everything covered so far is chemistry in compartments. This chapter adds the **mechanics**: the filaments that hold a cell's shape, the tracks along which cargo is dragged, the motors that walk, and the appendages that move a cell or move fluid past it.

Three ideas run through the whole chapter:

1. **Structure is active.** The cytoskeleton is not scaffolding — it is assembled, disassembled, and reorganised on demand. A cell changes shape by changing which filaments exist where.
2. **Movement needs tracks.** Vesicle traffic, organelle positioning, and cell division all run on filament highways with motor proteins as the trucks.
3. **Motion is conserved, machinery differs.** Bacteria, protists, sperm, and the cells lining your airway all move — by three unrelated solutions: molecular motors, cilia/flagella, and cytoskeletal polymerisation.

## The three filament systems

| | **Microfilaments (actin)** | **Intermediate filaments** | **Microtubules** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Diameter** | ~7 nm | ~10 nm | ~25 nm |
| **Subunit** | G-actin (globular) | Various (keratin, vimentin, lamins, neurofilaments) | α/β-tubulin dimer |
| **Structure** | Two helical strands | Rope-like coiled coils | Hollow tube, 13 protofilaments |
| **Polarity** | Yes (+/− ends) | **No** | Yes (+/− ends) |
| **Dynamic instability** | Treadmilling | Stable | **Rapid growth and catastrophe** |
| **Polymerisation energy** | ATP | None (covalent crosslinks) | GTP |
| **Main role** | Shape, contraction, crawling | Mechanical strength | Tracks, chromosome movement, cilia |
| **Motor proteins** | **Myosin** | None | **Kinesin, dynein** |
| **Drugs** | Cytochalasin, phalloidin | — | **Colchicine, vincristine, taxol** |

The pairing is worth memorising as a rule: **each filament has exactly one motor family, and no filament shares a motor.** Actin goes with myosin; microtubules go with kinesin and dynein; intermediate filaments have no motors and are passive by design — their job is to bear tension, not to generate movement.

### Microfilaments (actin)

- **Two-ended and polar.** The plus end grows fast; the minus end is anchored, often by **Arp2/3** branching networks or **formin** nucleation.
- **Treadmilling:** monomers added at one end while lost at the other — the filament can stay the same length while material flows through it.
- **Function by location:**

| Where | Structure | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Just under plasma membrane | **Cell cortex** | Cell shape, membrane stability |
| Leading edge of a crawling cell | **Lamellipodia** (branched network), **filopodia** (bundles) | Probing and moving forward |
| Muscle | **Thin filaments** in sarcomeres | Contraction with myosin |
| Cell–cell junctions | Adherens junctions, microvilli | Attachment; absorption surface |
| Cleavage furrow | Contractile ring | Cytokinesis |

**Microvilli** are worth a note: the intestinal absorptive surface is covered in actin-based fingers, each a bundle of parallel microfilaments with myosin and crosslinkers. They increase surface area ~20-fold without increasing cell number — again surface area as a transport requirement. **Brush border = actin.**

**Cell crawling is a three-step cycle**, and it is how white cells patrol and how a fibroblast closes a wound:

```
1. EXTEND   actin polymerises at the front → membrane pushed forward
      ↓
2. ADHESION  integrins grip the substrate ahead
      ↓
3. TRANSLATE  rear detaches; myosin contracts and pulls the cell body forward
      ↓
   repeat — the network behind disassembles, monomers recycle to the front
```

**Cancer metastasis reuses this machinery.** A cell that invades does so by polymerising actin into invadopodia that push through matrix, coupled with secretion of matrix-degrading enzymes. The motility programme is a normal wound-healing programme running in the wrong place.

### Intermediate filaments

**Ropes, not tracks.** They are the cell's tension-bearing elements — stretchable, tough, and unlike the other two they have **no polarity and no motors**.

| Type | Where | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Keratins** | Epithelia, hair, nails | Resist shear; link to desmosomes |
| **Vimentin** | Connective tissue cells | Mechanical resilience |
| **Neurofilaments** | Neurons (axons) | Axon calibre → conduction velocity |
| **Lamins** | Nuclear lamina | Nuclear shape, chromosome attachment (chapter 02) |

Because they anchor at desmosomes and hemidesmosomes, keratin networks **span from cell to cell**, so a sheet of epithelium distributes mechanical stress across the tissue rather than concentrating it at one cell. A tear in one cell's filaments is braced by its neighbours'.

**Clinical anchors:**

- **Epidermolysis bullosa simplex** — keratin mutations → skin blisters from trivial friction: the mechanical system fails at the filament.
- **Progeria** — lamin A mutations → nuclear deformation (chapter 02).
- **Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease** — neurofilament/structural defects → axon degeneration.

### Microtubules

- **Hollow tubes** of αβ-tubulin dimers; plus ends at the cell periphery, minus ends anchored at the **centrosome**.
- **Dynamic instability** — rapid growth followed by sudden collapse (**catastrophe**). This is not noise; it is a search mechanism. A growing plus end probes the cytoplasm until it captures a chromosome, a cargo, or a membrane. Tubulin bound to GTP polymerises; GTP hydrolysis to GDP makes the lattice prone to depolymerisation, so the tube carries a built-in timer.
- **Tracks for motors:**

| Motor | Direction | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Kinesin** | Toward **plus end** (periphery) | Carries vesicles and organelles outward |
| **Cytoplasmic dynein** | Toward **minus end** (centrosome) | Inward traffic; positions organelles; pulls on chromosomes |

Both are **hand-over-hand** walkers powered by ATP — literal molecular feet striding along a tube.

**Positioning, not just transport:** the Golgi sits near the centrosome because dynein pulls its vesicles inward; the ER spreads outward along microtubules. Disrupt the tracks with colchicine and the whole internal geography of the cell collapses — the Golgi fragments and redistributes, which is a standard demonstration that **organelle location is actively maintained.**

**Drugs that act here are anticancer staples**, because microtubules are also the mitotic spindle:

| Drug | Effect | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Colchicine** | Prevents polymerisation; binds tubulin | Gout — stops neutrophil migration and inflammation |
| **Vincristine, vinblastine** | Prevent polymerisation | Leukaemias, lymphomas |
| **Taxol (paclitaxel)** | **Stabilises** microtubules, blocks disassembly | Ovarian, breast, lung cancer |
| **Griseofulvin** | Disrupts fungal mitotic spindle | Dermatophyte infections |

**The logic is counterintuitive but consistent:** a spindle must *assemble and disassemble* to work. Blocking either direction freezes it — a cell that cannot depolymerise cannot segregate chromosomes, and a cell that cannot polymerise cannot form a spindle at all. Both stop mitosis; the drugs differ only in which step they jam.

## The centrosome and centrioles

| Feature | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Structure** | Two **centrioles** at right angles, each a 9-triplet ring (**9×0**, no central pair) |
| **Surrounded by** | Pericentriolar material (PCM) — the **microtubule-organising centre (MTOC)** |
| **Role in interphase** | Nucleates microtubules; sets cell polarity — minus ends at centrosome, plus ends outward |
| **Role in mitosis** | The two centrosomes separate and nucleate the **mitotic spindle** |
| **Number** | One centrosome duplicates once per cell cycle → two poles → bipolar spindle |

**Bipolarity is the safeguard.** A bipolar spindle pulls sister chromatids to opposite poles; multipolar spindles from extra centrosomes (common in cancer) give daughters the wrong chromosome number — **aneuploidy**, which both results from and drives genomic instability.

**Centrioles also build cilia and flagella.** A **basal body** is structurally a centriole (9×0) sitting at the base of an axoneme (9×2). Same template, two products: spindle poles or motile appendages. That relationship is why **centrosome and ciliogenesis defects cluster together clinically.**

## Vacuoles and the containment of bulk

A vacuole is a membrane sac for storage, digestion, or turgor — the generalist term; specialised versions are named by what they hold.

| Type | Contents | Function | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Food vacuole** | Engulfed particle | Digestion after phagocytosis | Amoebae, macrophages |
| **Contractile vacuole** | Water | **Osmoregulation** — pumps water out | Freshwater protists |
| **Central vacuole** | Water, ions, pigments, waste | **Turgor pressure**, storage, colour | Plant cells |
| **Lysosome** | Hydrolases | Recycling and defence | Animal cells (chapter 03) |
| **Secretory vesicle** | Cargo | Regulated release | Endocrine, exocrine cells |
| **Lipid droplet** | Triacylglycerol | Energy storage | Adipocytes, liver |

**Turgor is the plant's structural strategy.** The central vacuole, occupying up to 90% of a plant cell's volume, pushes the plasma membrane against the rigid wall; the resulting pressure keeps the tissue turgid. **Wilting is loss of turgor, not loss of wall.** Water potential, osmosis, and the mechanism of wilting belong to [03 — Cellular Processes](../03-cellular-processes/); here the point is that the vacuole is the organelle that makes pressure into a structural element.

**The contractile vacuole is an osmotic pump that solves a physical problem:** a freshwater protist constantly gains water by osmosis and would burst without active expulsion — its vacuole fills, fuses with the membrane, and contracts. It is the clearest single example of *an organelle whose entire job is countering a property of water* from [05 — Water](../00-foundations/05-water.md).

**Vacuole vs lysosome — common confusion.** Plant cells use the **vacuole** for the storage and hydrolytic roles that the animal **lysosome** performs, and the two are homologous compartments. Textbooks that say "plants have no lysosomes" mean the enzyme set is delivered to the vacuole; the chemistry is the same and the M6P-equivalent targeting logic applies.

## Cilia and flagella

### The axoneme: 9+2

```
        outer doublet pair
             ╱╲
     ┌─────  │  ─────┐
     │   ╱ ┌─┴─┐ ╲   │      9 OUTER DOUBLETS
     │  │  │ + │  │  │      (each a complete microtubule
     │  │  │ P │  │  │       fused to a partial one)
     │  │  └─┬─┘  │  │
     └──┤    │    ├──┘
        │  2 │    │           2 CENTRAL SINGLET PAIRS
        └────┴────┘           + radial spokes + nexin links
                    → dynein arms on each outer doublet
```

| Component | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **9 outer doublets** | The rails |
| **2 central singlets** | Regulatory; the "9+2" signature |
| **Nexin links** | Elastic connections between doublets — **convert sliding into bending** |
| **Radial spokes** | Signal from central pair to outer doublets |
| **Dynein arms** | Motor proteins that make one doublet try to slide past its neighbour |

**The mechanism is the important part:**

```
dynein on doublet A walks toward the PLUS END of doublet B
        ↓
doublets try to SLIDE past each other
        ↓
nexin links + basal anchoring PREVENT sliding
        ↓
the constrained sliding is converted into BENDING
        ↓
coordinated, rhythmic beating
```

**Sliding becomes bending because the structure will not let it slide.** This is the same motor-on-track logic as vesicle transport, except the track is constrained so that motor action produces a curve rather than translation.

- **Cilia** beat in a two-phase cycle: a stiff **power stroke** and a flexible **recovery stroke** — like an oar. Metachronal waves (neighbours beating out of phase) move fluid across a sheet of cells.
- **Flagella** (eukaryotic) are longer and beat in an undulatory, wave-like pattern — sperm propulsion is the standard example.

**Prokaryotic flagella are not the same structure** — a rotating filament built of **flagellin**, driven by a proton motive force in the membrane, with no dynein and no 9+2. Convergent function, unrelated machinery. The distinction is a favourite exam item.

### Non-motile (primary) cilia

Most vertebrate cells carry **one unmotile 9+0 cilium** — a sensory antenna rather than an oar. It concentrates receptors (e.g. polycystin) and monitors flow and chemical signals.

**Renal cystic disease follows from that.** In **polycystic kidney disease**, defective sensory cilia in the kidney tubule misread fluid flow, the cell reads "no flow" as a signal to proliferate, and cysts expand. **A sensory defect becomes a proliferative disease** — the connection between chapter 01 (growth signals) and this chapter (the antenna).

### Ciliary diseases (ciliopathies)

Because so many tissues use cilia, one structural defect has wide reach:

| Condition | Defect | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener syndrome)** | Dynein arms absent → no beating | Chronic respiratory infection, **situs inversus** (~50%), male infertility (immotile sperm) |
| **Polycystic kidney disease** | Defective primary cilia | Bilateral renal cysts |
| **Bardet–Biedl syndrome** | Intraflagellar transport defects | Retinal degeneration, obesity, polydactyly, renal anomalies |

**Kartagener syndrome ties three systems together with one motor protein.** Embryonic nodal cilia normally direct left–right asymmetry by sweeping morphogens to the left side; without beating, organ placement is randomised — so a patient can have reversed heart position *and* infected sinuses *and* immotile sperm from one dynein defect. **When you see chronic sinusitis with situs inversus, think cilia.**

**Intraflagellar transport (IFT)** is worth one line: cilia cannot import proteins by vesicle traffic once assembled (the transition zone gates entry), so components are carried along the axoneme by kinesin-2 (outward) and dynein-2 (inward) on the doublet tracks. Cilia are therefore **built and maintained by the same motor-on-track logic that positions organelles.**

## Putting movement together: three solutions

| Solution | Machinery | Used by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Molecular motors on tracks** | Actin–myosin; microtubule–kinesin/dynein | All eukaryotic cells | Vesicle transport, muscle, cell crawling |
| **Cilia / flagella** | 9+2 axoneme, dynein-driven sliding | Eukaryotic cells | Airway clearance, sperm, nodal flow |
| **Rotary motor** | Bacterial flagellum, proton-driven | Bacteria | Chemotaxis |

Everything else in the cell's behaviour — division, crawling, beating, transport — is one of these three with different cargo.

## Cell division: where the cytoskeleton does its most visible job

Mechanisms are covered properly in [03 — Cellular Processes](../03-cellular-processes/); the parts belong to this chapter:

```
microtubules → MITOTIC SPINDLE → captures and segregates chromosomes
actin + myosin → CONTRACTILE RING → pinches the cell in two (cytokinesis)
intermediate filaments → reassemble around the nucleus after division
```

A drug that jams microtubules (chapter's drug table) arrests cells in mitosis — which is precisely why antimitotic drugs act preferentially on **rapidly dividing cells**, and why their toxicities hit gut epithelium and marrow (the labile cells of chapter 01).

## Medical relevance

**Motility as a disease marker.** Loss of normal cytoskeletal control appears in cancer as changed shape, loss of contact inhibition, and invasive movement. The **epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition** — where a stationary epithelial cell downregulates keratins and desmosomes, upregulates vimentin, and becomes motile — is a deliberate reprogramming of the very systems in this chapter, and it is what a cell must do to metastasise.

**Neurofilament calibre sets conduction speed.** Axon diameter determines how fast action potentials travel; neurofilament density sets diameter. Demyelinating and axonal neuropathies are therefore structural diseases of the cytoskeleton's downstream consequences, covered in 10 — Nervous.

**Wound healing is this chapter in action.** Fibroblasts crawl in on actin, contract the wound using actin–myosin, and secrete matrix anchored at hemidesmosomes. Poor healing in diabetes reflects impaired motility and signalling — the same machinery, running slowly.

**Antimicrobials and antiparasitics.** The cytoskeleton difference between fungal tubulin/actin and human versions is exploited by antifungals; colchicine's effect on neutrophil actin is why it works in gout — it stops the inflammatory cell's migration, not its identity.

## Key facts

- Three filaments: **actin (7 nm, myosin, ATP, treadmilling)**, **intermediate filaments (10 nm, no motors, no polarity, mechanical strength)**, **microtubules (25 nm, kinesin/dynein, GTP, dynamic instability)**.
- Each filament has **one motor family**; intermediate filaments have none because their job is to bear tension.
- Cell crawling: **extend (actin polymerisation) → adhere (integrins) → contract (myosin) → detach rear.**
- Microtubules: plus ends outward, minus ends at the **centrosome**; **dynamic instability** lets them search the cytoplasm; GTP hydrolysis provides the timer.
- **Kinesin walks to the plus end; dynein to the minus end.** Organelle position is actively maintained.
- Antimitotics work in **both directions**: colchicine/vinca block polymerisation, taxol blocks disassembly — either way the spindle cannot cycle.
- **Centrosome = MTOC**; two centrosomes → bipolar spindle; extra centrosomes → aneuploidy.
- **Axoneme 9+2**: dynein arms make doublets slide; **nexin links convert sliding to bending.** Prokaryotic flagella are unrelated rotary machines of flagellin.
- **Basal body = centriole (9×0); axoneme = 9+2.**
- **Ciliopathies**: Kartagener (dynein absent → infection + situs inversus + infertility), polycystic kidney disease (sensory cilia), Bardet–Biedl.
- Vacuoles: turgor in plants, osmoregulation by contractile vacuole; **plant vacuole ≈ animal lysosome** for hydrolysis and storage.

## Practice questions

**1. Which pair correctly matches a cytoskeletal filament with its motor protein?**

A. Microfilaments — kinesin
B. Microtubules — myosin
C. Intermediate filaments — dynein
D. Microfilaments — myosin

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Actin (microfilaments) is powered by myosin; microtubules are powered by kinesin and dynein; intermediate filaments have no motors at all. Each filament family has exactly one motor family, which is why D is the only correct pairing and the others swap partners.

---

**2. How do cilia and flagella convert dynein activity into movement?**

A. Dynein depolymerises microtubules to shorten the axoneme
B. Dynein makes adjacent doublets slide; nexin links constrain the sliding so it becomes bending
C. Dynein pumps ions across the ciliary membrane
D. Dynein synthesises ATP for flagellar motion

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Dynein arms on one doublet walk along the neighbouring doublet, generating sliding force. Because nexin links and the basal anchor prevent free sliding, the force is expressed as a bend — repeated in waves, that is the beat. No shortening, ion pumping, or ATP synthesis at the axoneme itself (ATP is consumed by dynein) explains the motion.

---

**3. A patient with chronic sinusitis, situs inversus, and male infertility most likely has a defect in**

A. Keratin
B. Ciliary dynein
C. Collagen
D. The nuclear lamina

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The triad is Kartagener syndrome — absent dynein arms make cilia non-motile. Respiratory cilia fail to clear mucus (infections), nodal cilia fail to establish left–right asymmetry (situs inversus), and sperm cannot swim (infertility). One motor defect, three systems. Keratin and lamins cause skin and nuclear diseases; collagen is extracellular matrix.

---

**4. What is the function of the centrosome during mitosis?**

A. It stores chromosomes between divisions
B. It serves as the microtubule-organising centre from which the bipolar spindle is nucleated
C. It synthesises tubulin monomers
D. It performs cytokinesis by contraction

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The centrosome nucleates microtubules, and after duplication and separation the two centrosomes establish the two poles of the spindle — bipolarity is what ensures each daughter gets one complete chromosome set. Chromatin is in the nucleus (A), tubulin synthesis is ribosomal (C), and cytokinesis is an actin–myosin contractile ring (D).

---

**5. Taxol and colchicine both stop cell division. How can two drugs with opposite effects on microtubules both be antimitotic?**

A. They both inhibit DNA replication
B. The spindle must both assemble and disassemble to function; blocking either direction arrests the cycle
C. They both stabilise the actin cytoskeleton
D. They both block protein synthesis

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Dynamic instability — growth and shrinkage — is required for the spindle to capture chromosomes, align them, and then pull them apart. Colchicine prevents polymerisation; taxol prevents disassembly. Either way the cycle cannot proceed, so cells arrest in mitosis. The drugs are pharmacological opposites with the same endpoint because the process itself requires both directions.

---

**6. Which statement about intermediate filaments is correct?**

A. They are polar and transported by kinesin
B. They are built from tubulin dimers
C. They lack polarity, have no motor proteins, and function in mechanical strength
D. They polymerise using GTP hydrolysis

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Intermediate filaments are rope-like, non-polar, and motor-free — designed to bear tension and resist stretch. Keratins, vimentin, neurofilaments, and lamins are all members. Tubulin and GTP belong to microtubules; kinesin walks on microtubules, not intermediate filaments.

---

**7. The contractile vacuole of a freshwater protist exists to**

A. Store food for digestion
B. Expel excess water entering by osmosis, preventing lysis
C. Generate turgor pressure against a cell wall
D. Produce ATP by oxidative phosphorylation

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Freshwater is hypototic to the cytoplasm, so water constantly enters — without active expulsion the cell would burst. The contractile vacuole collects water and expels it by contraction. Turgor pressure is the plant central vacuole's role (C), and food digestion is the food vacuole (A).

---

**8. Which structure is correctly matched with its role?**

A. Cytoskeleton — enzyme sequestration
B. Cell cortex — shape and membrane support just beneath the plasma membrane
C. Centriole — DNA replication
D. Microvilli — cell motility

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The cortex is the actin-rich layer under the membrane that gives the cell shape and mechanical support. The cytoskeleton's roles are structural, transport, and motility, not enzyme storage; centrioles organise microtubules rather than replicating DNA; microvilli are absorptive surface expanders (actin bundles), while motility uses lamellipodia and flagella.

---

**9. Why does disruption of microtubules fragment and redistribute the Golgi apparatus?**

A. The Golgi is made of microtubules
B. Organelle position is actively maintained by motor proteins walking on microtubules; without tracks, positioning fails
C. The Golgi requires microtubules for its enzymatic reactions
D. Microtubules are the only route for protein entry into the Golgi

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Vesicles are pulled toward the centrosome by dynein and outward by kinesin; the Golgi's perinuclear position depends on that ongoing traffic. Collapse the tracks and the Golgi scatters — showing that intracellular geography is actively maintained, not a passive consequence of where things were made. The Golgi's chemistry needs enzymes and membranes, not microtubules as substrates.

---

**10. How does the loss of normal cytoskeletal regulation contribute to metastasis?**

A. Cancer cells need more microtubules to divide
B. Cells undergo an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, adopting actin-based motility and losing stable attachments
C. Intermediate filaments become the primary motor of movement
D. Metastasis occurs only when cilia are present

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Invasion requires a cell to detach, migrate, and invade — achieved by remodelling the cytoskeleton: downregulating keratins and desmosomes, upregulating vimentin and actin-based structures like invadopodia, and losing contact inhibition and anoikis. That reprogramming is the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Intermediate filaments never become motors (C), and cilia are irrelevant to migration (D).
