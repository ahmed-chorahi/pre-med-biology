# The Cardiovascular System

## Why it matters

Cells must be fed within a short diffusion distance of a supply line, because **diffusion time rises with the square of distance** ([02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md)). The cardiovascular system is the answer: a **closed pump moving fluid down pressure gradients** — the same physics as every other transport problem in the body, with three additions.

| Feature | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| **A rhythm** | Filling and ejection cycle ~100,000 times a day |
| **A self-starting electrical system** | The beat is generated inside the heart wall itself |
| **Feedback control** | Output and resistance adjust beat to beat to defend one variable |

That variable is **blood pressure** — the number medicine measures most often, because it summarises cardiac output, peripheral resistance, blood volume, and nervous and hormonal tone at once. The section rule holds: **structure → function → mechanism → regulation → homeostasis**, closing on the loops that defend pressure ([03 — Homeostasis: the full control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md)).

## Heart structure and function

### Four chambers: two pumps in series

Blood returning from the body must pass the lungs before it can go round again, so the right side drives the **pulmonary circuit** and the left side the **systemic circuit**, in series.

| Property | Right side | Left side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chambers | Right atrium → right ventricle | Left atrium → left ventricle |
| Outflow | Pulmonary artery | Aorta |
| Bed served | Lung — gas exchange | Whole body |
| Arterial pressure | **Low**, mean ~15 mmHg | **High**, mean ~90 mmHg |
| Ventricular wall | Thin (3–5 mm) | Thick (10–15 mm) |

**Why the pressures differ.** The lung is a delicate exchange bed: systemic-level pressure would drive fluid into the alveoli (**pulmonary oedema**). The body must perfuse a brain 30 cm above and feet 1 m below the heart against gravity, so it needs a high head of pressure. **Pressure is matched to the bed perfused**, and wall thickness reflects the afterload each ventricle faces. Because the pumps are in series their outputs must match beat for beat — hence left-sided failure backs up into the lungs, right-sided failure into the body.

### Wall layers

| Layer | Composition | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Epicardium** | Mesothelium, connective tissue, fat | Outer coat; carries coronary vessels |
| **Myocardium** | **Cardiac muscle** — striated, involuntary, intercalated discs | Contractile layer generating pressure |
| **Endocardium** | Endothelium + connective tissue | Smooth, non-thrombogenic lining |

Cardiac cells are coupled by **gap junctions** in the intercalated discs, so the myocardium contracts as a functional syncytium ([01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md)).

### Valves: pressure gradients make flow one-way

A valve opens when the gradient points one way and shuts when it reverses; nothing controls them actively.

| Valve | Between | Opens when | Closes when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tricuspid / mitral** (AV) | Atrium → ventricle | Atrial pressure exceeds ventricular (filling) | Ventricular pressure exceeds atrial (systole) |
| **Pulmonary / aortic** (semilunar) | Ventricle → artery | Ventricular pressure exceeds arterial (ejection) | Arterial pressure exceeds ventricular (diastole) |

**Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles** anchor the cusps so systole does not evert them into the atrium; they neither open nor close the valve. Poor anchoring gives regurgitation, a valve that will not open gives stenosis — both flow faults, not electrical ones.

### Coronary arteries: the pump needs its own supply

Myocardium is too thick to be fed from its chambers, so the heart has its own circulation: the **aortic root just above the aortic valve** feeds left and right **coronary arteries**, whose intramyocardial beds drain through cardiac veins into the coronary sinus.

Two consequences matter clinically. **Coronary filling is diastolic** — relaxed muscle stops compressing the intramural vessels — so tachycardia shortens diastole and can starve a diseased myocardium. And the supply is end-arterial with no bypass: sudden occlusion kills the muscle downstream within minutes to hours (**myocardial infarction**), which is why occlusion is catastrophic rather than uncomfortable.

## The cardiac cycle

The **cardiac cycle** is one beat: **systole** (contraction, ejection) and **diastole** (relaxation, filling) — a **pressure–volume arrow chain** in which pressure rises, a valve is forced open, volume moves, and the valve shuts again.

| Term | Definition | Resting value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** | Ventricular volume just before systole | ~120 mL |
| **End-systolic volume (ESV)** | Volume after ejection | ~50 mL |
| **Stroke volume (SV)** | EDV − ESV | **~70 mL** |
| **Ejection fraction (EF)** | SV ÷ EDV | **55–70%** |

### The chain

```
ATRIAL SYSTOLE — the "atrial kick"
        ↓  tops up the LAST ~20% of EDV; the first ~80% filled PASSIVELY

VENTRICULAR SYSTOLE
        ↓  ISOVOLUMETRIC CONTRACTION — all valves shut, pressure soars, volume constant
        ↓  ventricular > atrial → AV VALVES SHUT = S1
        ↓  ventricular > arterial → SEMILUNAR VALVES OPEN → EJECTION

VENTRICULAR DIASTOLE
        ↓  ventricle relaxes → arterial > ventricular → SEMILUNAR SHUT = S2
        ↓  ISOVOLUMETRIC RELAXATION — all valves shut, volume constant
        ↓  ventricular < atrial → AV VALVES OPEN → PASSIVE FILLING
```

**Sounds are closure events.** **S1** ("lub") is AV valve closure as ventricular pressure overtakes atrial pressure — start of systole, best at the apex; **S2** ("dup") is semilunar closure as arterial pressure overtakes ventricular — start of diastole, best at the base. A sound is the vibration from blood decelerating against a valve: **valves make noise when they close, not when they open**, so a stenotic or leaking valve makes a murmur on the wrong side of the cycle.

The **Wiggers diagram** plots pressure, volume, ECG, and sounds on one axis, so every valve event is visibly caused by **two pressure lines crossing** and every volume change by **an open valve** — learn the crossings rather than memorising the picture. **Ejection fraction** is clinical shorthand for squeeze: low EF with a normal EDV means a weak contraction, low EF with a ballooned EDV a dilated failing chamber.

## Electrical conduction: the self-starting system

### The arrow chain

```
SA NODE (right atrium; ~100 spontaneous depolarisations/min — the pacemaker)
        ↓  internodal pathways → BOTH ATRIA contract
        ↓
AV NODE — THE ~0.1 s DELAY so atria empty BEFORE ventricles fire
        ↓
BUNDLE OF HIS → RIGHT and LEFT BUNDLE BRANCHES (down the septum)
        ↓
PURKINJE FIBRES — rapid conduction up from the APEX
        ↓  ventricles contract apex → base, wringing blood into the outflow tracts
ATRIA REPOLARISE — buried inside the ventricular QRS complex
```

| Structure | Intrinsic rate | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SA node** | **~100/min** | Primary pacemaker — fastest inherent rhythm wins |
| **AV node** | ~40–60/min | **Delays ~0.1 s**; backup pacemaker |
| **Bundle of His + branches** | ~40–60/min | The **only** normal route down — the fibrous skeleton insulates atria from ventricles |
| **Purkinje fibres** | ~20–40/min | Very fast spread, apex-to-base squeeze |

The delay is the point: without it atria and ventricles would contract together and the atrial kick would be wasted.

### Autorhythmicity

Contractile cells hold a stable resting potential; **autorhythmic cells do not**. During diastole inward **"funny" Na⁺ channels (I_f)** and a Ca²⁺ clock make the membrane **drift toward threshold on its own** — **spontaneous depolarisation**. Threshold opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (T-type, then L-type, which triggers contraction), K⁺ efflux repolarises, and the drift restarts.

```
diastolic drift UP (funny Na⁺ leak + Ca²⁺ clock)
        ↓  threshold reached with NO external stimulus
Ca²⁺ in → depolarisation → K⁺ out → repolarisation → drift resumes
```

Nerves and hormones change only the **slope** of that drift: sympathetic input steepens it, parasympathetic flattens it. Hence a denervated transplanted heart still beats, and a stopped heart needs an electrical push, not a nerve impulse.

### ECG mapping

The **ECG** records the heart's summed **electrical** activity, not contraction itself.

| Wave/interval | Represents |
| --- | --- |
| **P wave** | **Atrial depolarisation** |
| **PR interval** | P start → QRS start = **AV delay + His–Purkinje conduction** (0.12–0.20 s; lengthens in AV block) |
| **QRS complex** | **Ventricular depolarisation** (atrial repolarisation is buried inside it) |
| **ST segment** | Fully depolarised ventricle — a shift signals ischaemia or injury |
| **T wave** | **Ventricular repolarisation** |
| **QT interval** | The **total electrical event of one ventricular beat** |

ECG is electrical, sounds are mechanical, pressures and volumes haemodynamic — three recordings of one cycle, stacked in the Wiggers diagram.

## Regulation: tuning output and pressure

### Frank–Starling: matching beats without nerves

**Greater filling → greater stretch → more force.** Within physiological limits, the more the ventricle holds at end-diastole, the more it ejects:

```
↑ venous return → ↑ END-DIASTOLIC VOLUME (stretch)
        ↓  sarcomeres near optimal overlap, greater Ca²⁺ sensitivity
        ↓  ↑ CONTRACTILITY of that beat → ↑ STROKE VOLUME
        ↓  output matches whatever the venous side delivered
```

Entirely **intrinsic** — no nerve, no hormone, no rate change — so it balances the ventricles beat to beat; this is why **preload** is a primary determinant of stroke volume.

### Neural and hormonal control

| Arm | Signal | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sympathetic** | Noradrenaline / adrenaline at **β₁** | **↑ HR, ↑ contractility, ↑ conduction**; α₁ vasoconstriction elsewhere |
| **Parasympathetic** | Acetylcholine, **vagus (X)**, **muscarinic M₂** | **↓ HR, ↓ AV conduction**; little effect on contractility |
| **Adrenaline** | Adrenal medulla (hormonal) | β₁ stimulation; α₁ constriction in skin and gut, β₂ dilation in muscle |
| **Angiotensin II, aldosterone, ADH** | Renin system, posterior pituitary | Vasoconstriction; Na⁺ and water retention |

At rest **vagal tone dominates**, so resting rate (~70) sits below the SA node's intrinsic ~100/min — the pacemaker is actively slowed. See [03 — Nervous system](03-nervous-system.md) and [04 — Endocrine system](04-endocrine-system.md).

### Cardiac output: worked calculation

**Cardiac output (CO) = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV).**

```
Rest:        HR 70 × SV 70 mL   = 4,900 mL/min ≈ 4.9 L/min
Exercise:    HR 150 × SV 100 mL = 15,000 mL/min = 15 L/min   (↑ ~3-fold)
Athlete:     HR 50 × SV 100 mL  = 5,000 mL/min  = 5 L/min — same output, fewer beats
```

Three variables move CO: **rate, preload, contractility** — and the athlete example shows why training bradycardia is a strong pump.

### The baroreceptor reflex: a complete negative-feedback loop

```
BLOOD PRESSURE RISES
        ↓  BARORECEPTORS (carotid sinus, aortic arch) stretch and fire faster
        ↓  afferent: glossopharyngeal (IX) + vagus (X)
MEDULLARY CARDIOVASCULAR CENTRE → ↑ PARASYMPATHETIC, ↓ SYMPATHETIC
        ↓  ↓ HR, ↓ contractility, ↓ arteriolar and venous tone → CO and TPR fall
PRESSURE RETURNS TO RANGE → receptor firing normalises → loop idles

PRESSURE FALLS (haemorrhage, standing): baroreceptors fire LESS →
        ↑ sympathetic / ↓ vagal → ↑ rate, ↑ contractility, VASOCONSTRICTION
PRESSURE RESTORED
```

It corrects deviations, not the stimulus, and does not reset quickly — though **chronic hypertension recalibrates the receptors to the prevailing pressure**, so the high value becomes the defended one. Components are analysed in [03 — Homeostasis: the full control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md).

## Blood vessels

Four tube types in series: **arteries** conduct and buffer, **arterioles** set resistance, **capillaries** exchange, and **venules and veins** store and return.

### Arteries: the Windkessel function

During systole elastic arteries **distend and store a stroke volume**; during diastole they **recoil and push it forward** — the **Windkessel effect**, without which pressure would collapse toward zero between beats. Muscular arteries then distribute blood and redirect it by constricting.

### Arterioles: where resistance is set

Poiseuille's law makes arteriolar control quantitative: **resistance ∝ 1/radius⁴**, so halving the radius multiplies resistance sixteen-fold.

```
Sympathetic α₁ / angiotensin II / ADH → smooth muscle CONTRACTS
        → radius ↓ → resistance ↑↑ → MAP ↑

Local metabolites (CO₂, H⁺, adenosine, K⁺, low O₂) → smooth muscle RELAXES
        → radius ↑ → hyperaemia to active tissue
```

**The heart sets flow; the arterioles set pressure**, and local metabolic control sends blood to whichever tissue is metabolising hardest, independent of the nervous system.

### Capillaries: exchange and Starling forces

A single endothelial layer allows both the concentration-driven diffusion of [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md) and the pressure-driven bulk flow of the **Starling forces**.

| Force | Direction | Value (systemic) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Capillary hydrostatic pressure** | **Outward — filtration**, transmitted from arteriolar pumping | ~35 mmHg at the arterial end, ~15 mmHg at the venous end |
| **Interstitial hydrostatic pressure** | Slightly outward | ~0 to −3 mmHg |
| **Blood colloid (oncotic) pressure** | **Inward — retention**, from plasma proteins, chiefly **albumin** | ~26 mmHg, nearly constant |
| **Interstitial osmotic pressure** | Outward, from protein leaked into tissue | ~1–8 mmHg |

```
ARTERIAL END: hydrostatic ~35 vs osmotic ~26 → NET OUTFLOW → FILTRATION
VENOUS END:   hydrostatic ~15 vs osmotic ~26 → NET INFLOW  → REABSORPTION
```

**The modern caveat:** venous reabsorption is small and **most filtered fluid returns through the lymphatic capillaries** — fail that drainage (blocked nodes, filariasis) and protein-rich fluid accumulates despite normal capillary pressures. Either way filtration must balance over 24 hours: low albumin (nephrotic syndrome) or raised venous pressure shift the balance toward oedema.

### Venules and veins: the compliant reservoir

Veins hold **~60–70% of total blood volume** — **capacitance vessels** whose tone moves large volumes, so venous tone changes preload and therefore output via Starling. Because venous pressure is only a few mmHg, three mechanisms return blood centrally:

| Mechanism | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Valves** | Bicuspid flaps block retrograde flow when upright |
| **Skeletal-muscle pump** | Surrounding muscle squeezes veins; valves direct flow centrally ([02 — Muscular system](02-muscular-system.md)) |
| **Respiratory pump** | Inspiration lowers thoracic and raises abdominal pressure ([06 — Respiratory system](06-respiratory-system.md)) |

Failed valves give varicose veins and dependent oedema — the pump running backwards.

### Arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis: the mechanism

**Arteriosclerosis** is any arterial stiffening, including age-related loss of elastin. **Atherosclerosis** is the specific **inflammatory, lipid-driven plaque disease of the intima**:

```
ENDOTHELIAL INJURY (hypertension, smoking, disturbed flow, hyperglycaemia)
        ↓  permeability ↑, adhesion ↑
LDL enters the intima, is OXIDISED → monocytes → MACROPHAGES → FOAM CELLS
        ↓  FATTY STREAK → smooth-muscle migration, fibrous cap, lipid core → PLAQUE
        ↓
STENOSIS → fixed obstruction → effort angina, organ ischaemia
        OR cap RUPTURE → THROMBOSIS → acute occlusion → INFARCTION (MI / ischaemic stroke)
```

**Stable disease is narrowing; acute disease is rupture.** The sudden event is a clot forming on a ruptured plaque, which is why antiplatelet drugs matter alongside lipid lowering.

## Blood pressure

### MAP ≈ CO × TPR — worked calculation

Mean arterial pressure (**MAP**) is what perfuses organs. Because diastole lasts longer than systole, MAP sits closer to diastolic:

```
From the readings: 120/80 → pulse pressure = 120 − 80 = 40
    MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓ × pulse pressure = 80 + 13 ≈ 93 mmHg

From output and resistance: CO = 5 L/min, TPR = 18 mmHg·min/L
    MAP = CO × TPR = 5 × 18 = 90 mmHg   (the methods agree)
```

| Determinant | Raised by | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cardiac output** | ↑ HR, ↑ SV, ↑ blood volume | Haemorrhage ↓ CO → MAP falls |
| **Total peripheral resistance** | Arteriolar constriction, stiff arteries, high viscosity | Vasoconstriction ↑ MAP at unchanged output |
| **Blood volume** | Renal retention, transfusion, excess salt | ↑ preload → ↑ CO → ↑ MAP |

Normal adult pressure is ~**120/80 mmHg**; hypertension is conventionally sustained ≥130/80 mmHg.

### Long-term control: RAAS (preview)

The baroreflex buys seconds; pressure held low for days is corrected by the kidney — a preview of [08 — Urinary system](08-urinary-system.md):

```
RENAL PERFUSION FALLS (↓ volume, ↓ CO, low Na⁺ at macula densa)
        ↓  JUXTAGLOMERULAR CELLS release RENIN
renin: angiotensinogen → ANGIOTENSIN I
        ↓  ACE (pulmonary endothelium) → ANGIOTENSIN II
ANGIOTENSIN II → VASOCONSTRICTION (TPR ↑)
        ↓  adrenal cortex → ALDOSTERONE → Na⁺ and water retained
        ↓  posterior pituitary → ADH → water retained (aquaporin-2)
        ↓  hypothalamic THIRST → intake ↑
VOLUME and PRESSURE RISE → renin switches off
```

Aldosterone and ADH act on the kidney, so the loop works over **hours to days** — hence **ACE inhibitors** and **ARBs** as first-line antihypertensives, with diuretics undoing the volume limb.

### Hypertension as sustained ↑CO or ↑TPR

High pressure has only two components: **too much flow, too much resistance, or both**. Early hyperadrenergic hypertension raises cardiac output; sustained high flow through constricted vessels remodels arterioles, so **TPR rises and persists** even when output normalises. **~90–95% is primary (essential)** — genetics, renal sodium handling, sympathetic tone, obesity — while secondary causes (renal artery stenosis, phaeochromocytoma, primary aldosteronism, coarctation) matter because some are correctable.

## Blood as a connective tissue

Blood is a **specialised fluid connective tissue** — cells in a liquid matrix — fitting the categories of [01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md).

### Formed elements

| Element | Origin | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Erythrocytes (RBCs)** | Red bone marrow | **No nucleus or organelles**; **biconcave disc**; packed with **haemoglobin**; lifespan **~120 days**; glycolysis only | Carry O₂ and some CO₂; shape maximises surface area, and deformability lets them cross narrow capillaries |
| **Platelets** | Megakaryocyte **fragments — not cells** | Anucleate, granule-rich | **Clotting**: adhesion, aggregation, procoagulant factors |
| **Leukocytes** | Bone marrow / thymus | Nucleated, several classes | Immunity — covered in [09 — Immune system](09-immune-system.md) |

RBCs shed their nucleus to make room for haemoglobin and lose mitochondria so they never consume the oxygen they carry.

### Plasma

**Plasma** is ~55% of blood volume, roughly **90% water**:

| Component | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Albumin** | Main source of **colloid osmotic pressure**; binds hormones and drugs |
| **Globulins** | Antibodies, transport proteins, clotting factors |
| **Fibrinogen** | Soluble precursor → fibrin |
| **Electrolytes** (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, HCO₃⁻) | Osmosis, excitability, **acid–base buffering** |
| **Nutrients, wastes, hormones** | Gut absorption, renal excretion, gas exchange, endocrine signalling ([07 — Digestive system](07-digestive-system.md), [08 — Urinary system](08-urinary-system.md)) |

Plasma taken after clotting is **serum** — plasma minus fibrinogen and the consumed clotting factors.

### Why viscosity matters

Blood is ~3–4 times more viscous than water, mainly from **red cells (haematocrit ~45%)** and proteins, and **resistance rises with viscosity**. Anaemia lowers viscosity and peripheral resistance, so cardiac output rises to compensate — a normal blood pressure can conceal a serious fall in oxygen delivery. Polycythaemia does the reverse, and sluggish flow is prothrombotic.

## Medical relevance

**Atherosclerotic heart disease and stroke are the same disease in different beds.** A carotid plaque rupturing into a thrombus gives an ischaemic stroke, the same sequence in a coronary artery a myocardial infarction, in a femoral artery claudication — so one risk-factor regimen treats all three.

**Hypertension is silent because it damages by load, not sensation.** Sustained pressure causes **left ventricular hypertrophy**, accelerates atherosclerosis, scars the kidney, and weakens vessel walls — **target-organ damage** over years with no symptoms, so the first presentation is often stroke, heart failure, or renal failure.

**Heart failure is compensation becoming disease.** Falling output is met first by **Frank–Starling**, then by **neurohormonal overdrive** — sympathetic drive, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone, and ADH raising rate, contractility, volume, and resistance to prop up pressure. The loop succeeds on paper while the patient retains volume, becomes oedematous, and loads a weak ventricle harder. **Compensated** failure is stable for months precisely because compensation works; **decompensation** comes when those mechanisms become the load — hence ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and aldosterone antagonists treat **the compensation, not the number it produces**.

**Atrial fibrillation turns an electrical fault into a clotting one.** Loss of coordinated atrial systole leaves blood stagnant in the appendage → **stasis** → thrombus → fragment embolises → cerebral artery occluded → **cardioembolic stroke**. Rhythm control is therefore separate from **anticoagulation**, because the hazard is the clot.

| Drug | Mechanism | Logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Warfarin** | Inhibits **vitamin K** recycling → factors **II, VII, IX, X** cannot be γ-carboxylated | Blocks *synthesis*; slow; monitored by INR |
| **Heparin** | Potentiates **antithrombin III** → inactivates thrombin and Xa | Immediate; acute thrombosis |
| **Aspirin** | Irreversible **COX-1** inhibition in platelets → less **thromboxane A₂** | Antiplatelet; anucleate platelets cannot replace the enzyme — see [05 — Enzymes](../01-biochemistry/05-enzymes.md) |
| **Thrombolytics (tPA)** | **Plasminogen → plasmin**, digesting fibrin | Dissolves an established clot — time-critical |

**Anaemia changes oxygen delivery even at normal pressure.** Pressure is not perfusion, and perfusion is not oxygenation: fewer red cells means less O₂ per litre, so output rises while the myocardium becomes vulnerable to supply–demand mismatch — angina can be anaemia's first sign in clear coronary arteries.

**Defibrillation resets synchronised activity.** In ventricular fibrillation the myocardium is a mosaic of micro-re-entrant circuits producing no output; a shock **depolarises the whole myocardium simultaneously**, extinguishing the wavelets so the SA node resumes as pacemaker. In organised fast rhythms **synchronised cardioversion** times the shock to the QRS, avoiding the vulnerable repolarisation window. **Thrombolysis** in infarction and ischaemic stroke is a race against the clock: flow restored within hours salvages tissue, beyond that it becomes necrosis.

**Foetal circulation is the same pump under different plumbing** — shunts bypass the non-functional lungs until birth makes the systemic circuit high-pressure; see [05 — Placenta and maternal–fetal exchange](../11-reproduction-and-development/05-placenta-and-maternal-fetal-exchange.md).

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Systole means ejection, diastole means filling" | They mean **contraction and relaxation**; ejection and filling are what happens *during* them — nothing moves while every valve is shut (isovolumetric phases). |
| "Heart sounds are made when valves open" | **S1 and S2 are closure sounds**, from blood decelerating against a valve slamming shut. Opening is silent unless the valve is stenotic or leaking. |
| "Arteries carry oxygenated blood" | Arteries carry blood **away from the heart**: the **pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood**. The rule is direction, not gas content. |
| "Arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis are the same" | **Arteriosclerosis** is any stiffening, including normal ageing; **atherosclerosis** is the intimal lipid-driven inflammatory plaque disease. |
| "Veins are passive pipes" | Veins are **compliant reservoirs holding ~60–70% of blood volume**; return needs valves plus the skeletal-muscle and respiratory pumps. |
| "The venous capillary end reabsorbs all filtrate" | Net reabsorption is small; **most filtered fluid returns via lymphatics**. Failed drainage causes oedema despite normal capillary pressures. |
| "The heart needs nerves to beat" | The **SA node depolarises spontaneously**; nerves only change the rate, so a denervated transplanted heart still beats. |
| "The ECG records contraction" | The ECG records **depolarisation and repolarisation**; contraction appears as pressure and volume change, sounds as the acoustic record. |
| "Blood pressure equals cardiac output" | **MAP ≈ CO × TPR** — normal output can accompany severe hypertension when arterioles are constricted. |
| "Warfarin and heparin work the same way" | **Warfarin** blocks hepatic synthesis of vitamin K–dependent factors (slow); **heparin** boosts **antithrombin III** against existing thrombin (fast). |

## Key facts

- The heart is **two pumps in series** — right side to the low-pressure lung (~15 mmHg), left side to the high-pressure body (~90 mmHg) — and their outputs must match beat for beat; walls are **epicardium, myocardium, endocardium** with gap-junction coupling.
- Four **passive pressure-operated valves** give one-way flow — **S1 = AV closure, S2 = semilunar closure** — and the cycle runs **atrial systole (kick ≈ 20% of filling) → isovolumetric contraction → ejection → isovolumetric relaxation → passive filling**; **SV = EDV − ESV**, normal **EF 55–70%**.
- **CO = HR × SV**: 70 × 70 mL ≈ 5 L/min at rest and ~15 L/min in maximal exercise; an athlete reaches 5 L/min at 50 bpm with a larger stroke volume.
- Conduction runs **SA node (~100/min) → AV node (0.1 s delay) → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibres (apex up)**; autorhythmicity rests on spontaneous diastolic depolarisation through **funny Na⁺ channels**.
- **ECG**: P = atrial depolarisation, PR = AV delay, QRS = ventricular depolarisation, T = ventricular repolarisation, QT = the whole ventricular electrical event.
- **Frank–Starling** — greater filling → greater stretch → greater force — matches output to venous return beat to beat **without nerves**.
- **Sympathetic β₁ raises rate and contractility; vagal M₂ lowers rate**; resting vagal tone holds heart rate below the intrinsic 100/min.
- **MAP ≈ CO × TPR** (5 L/min × 18 mmHg·min/L ≈ 90 mmHg, agreeing with 120/80); the **baroreflex** corrects in one or two beats, **RAAS and ADH** over hours to days.
- Vessels specialise: **arteries buffer (Windkessel)**, **arterioles set resistance (r⁴)**, **capillaries exchange (Starling forces)**, **veins hold ~60–70% of volume** and empty it via valves and the muscle and respiratory pumps.
- **Atherosclerosis** runs endothelial injury → oxidised LDL → foam cells → plaque → stenosis, or rupture → thrombosis → infarction: one disease in coronary, carotid, and peripheral beds.
- Blood is a **connective tissue**: **enucleated biconcave RBCs (~120 days)**, **platelet fragments**, **leukocytes**, in plasma where **albumin** gives colloid osmotic pressure.

## Practice questions

**1. Pulmonary pressure is far lower than systemic pressure because**

A. The right ventricle cannot generate force
B. The lung is a delicate exchange bed that would flood at systemic pressure
C. The pulmonary artery is too wide to hold pressure
D. Blood reaches the left atrium before passing the lung

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Pressure is matched to the bed perfused; systemic pressure would flood the alveolar–capillary membrane. The thin right wall reflects low afterload.

**2. The first heart sound (S1) is produced by**

A. Closure of the semilunar valves at the start of systole
B. Opening of the atrioventricular valves at the start of diastole
C. Closure of the atrioventricular valves as ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure
D. Turbulent flow across a stenotic aortic valve

**Answer: C**

Explanation: S1 begins systole — the vibration of the AV valves shutting as ventricular pressure overtakes atrial pressure. S2 is semilunar closure; D describes a murmur.

---

**3. The ~0.1 s delay at the AV node ensures that**

A. The ventricles repolarise before the atria contract
B. Atrial contraction finishes emptying the ventricles before ventricular systole
C. Purkinje fibres depolarise the apex before the septum
D. Coronary vessels fill during systole

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The delay lets atrial systole complete its ~20% top-up before the ventricles fire; without it both would contract together. Coronary filling is diastolic.

**4. A trained athlete at rest has a heart rate of 48/min and a stroke volume of 104 mL. Cardiac output is closest to**

A. 2.4 L/min
B. 10.4 L/min
C. 15.0 L/min
D. 5.0 L/min

**Answer: D**

Explanation: CO = HR × SV = 48 × 104 mL ≈ 4,992 mL/min ≈ 5.0 L/min — the same as an untrained adult at 70 × 70 mL. Athletic bradycardia reflects a larger stroke volume.

**5. Increased venous return raises stroke volume with no nervous input. This is the**

A. Baroreceptor reflex
B. Frank–Starling mechanism
C. Windkessel effect
D. Bainbridge reflex

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Greater filling stretches sarcomeres toward optimal overlap, increasing force — Starling's law, which balances the ventricles beat to beat. The baroreflex is neural.

**6. When arterial pressure falls, the medullary response is**

A. Decreased vagal and increased sympathetic output, raising rate, contractility, and tone
B. Increased vagal output and decreased sympathetic output
C. Increased ADH and decreased renin
D. Direct arteriolar constriction by the baroreceptors

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Reduced carotid sinus and aortic arch stretch lowers afferent firing through nerves IX and X, so the medulla withdraws vagal tone and drives sympathetic output. Baroreceptors are receptors.

**7. Arterioles are the principal site of peripheral resistance because**

A. Resistance varies with the fourth power of the radius, which arteriolar smooth muscle changes
B. They have the thickest wall of any vessel
C. They contain the most elastin
D. They hold the largest share of blood volume

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Poiseuille's law gives resistance ∝ 1/r⁴, so small changes in calibre produce large changes in resistance — hence "resistance vessels". Elastin is in arteries; most volume sits in veins.

**8. At the arterial end of a systemic capillary, fluid is filtered because**

A. Colloid osmotic pressure exceeds hydrostatic pressure
B. Lymphatic pressure forces fluid out
C. Capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds the opposing colloid osmotic pressure
D. Water is pumped actively through the endothelium

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Hydrostatic pressure (~35 mmHg) beats inward oncotic pressure (~26 mmHg), giving net outward bulk flow down a pressure gradient, not active transport. Much later returns via lymphatics.

**9. A patient develops atrial fibrillation. Anticoagulation is started mainly because**

A. Loss of atrial contraction causes stasis, thrombus formation, and embolic stroke risk
B. The ventricles cannot generate adequate pressure
C. Atrial fibrillation always occludes a coronary artery
D. Warfarin directly restores sinus rhythm

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Without effective atrial systole blood stagnates in the left appendage, forming thrombus that can embolise to the brain. Anticoagulation targets that clotting hazard, not the rhythm.

**10. Which pair correctly matches drug to mechanism?**

A. Aspirin inhibits vitamin K recycling so factors II, VII, IX, and X cannot be synthesised
B. Warfarin irreversibly blocks platelet COX-1 to reduce thromboxane A₂
C. Thrombolytics block platelet ADP receptors to prevent aggregation
D. Heparin potentiates antithrombin III, accelerating inactivation of thrombin and factor Xa

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Heparin acts on antithrombin III against already circulating thrombin and Xa, giving rapid anticoagulation. A describes warfarin, B describes aspirin, and C describes an ADP-blocking antiplatelet rather than a thrombolytic.
