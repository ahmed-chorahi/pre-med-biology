# The Respiratory System

## Why it matters

Every cell oxidises fuel and excretes CO₂ ([04 — ATP and metabolism](../03-cellular-processes/04-atp-and-metabolism.md)), but diffusion alone cannot service an organism — diffusion time rises with the **square of distance** ([02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md)). The lung therefore faces three linked problems, and each is solved by a structure built for exactly one job.

| Problem | Physics it obeys | Structure built for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Moving air (ventilation)** | Bulk flow down a **pressure gradient** | Airways plus respiratory muscles |
| **Exchanging gas** | **Diffusion** across the thinnest membrane in the body (~0.5 µm) | Alveolus wrapped in capillary |
| **Carrying gas** | Reversible chemical binding in a fluid | **Haemoglobin** inside red cells, moved by the pump ([05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md)) |

**Every clinical picture in respiratory medicine is one of these three failing**, and the pattern of failure tells you which:

| Failure | What is lost | Diseases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Obstruction** | Airflow — resistance ↑, air trapping | Asthma, COPD |
| **Diffusion loss** | Surface area or membrane thinness | Emphysema (area), fibrosis (thickness) |
| **Perfusion mismatch** | Blood flow to ventilated units | Pulmonary embolism |

The section rule holds throughout: **structure → function → mechanism → regulation → homeostasis**, closing on the loops that defend arterial blood gases ([03 — Homeostasis: the full control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md)).

## Anatomy: nose to alveolus

### The conducting zone: a conditioned tube

The **conducting zone** runs **nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles** (ending at terminal bronchioles). It conditions and moves air and — the key point — **contributes nothing to gas exchange**.

| Region | Specialised features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nose and nasal cavity** | Conchae, nasal hairs, dense capillary plexus | Filter, warm, humidify; the largest single share of airway resistance |
| **Pharynx** | Common passage for air and food | Conveys air; swallowing reflex protects it ([07 — Digestive system](07-digestive-system.md)) |
| **Larynx** | Epiglottis, vocal folds | Airway protection and voice |
| **Trachea** | **C-shaped cartilage rings, incomplete behind**, ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium, goblet cells | Holds the tube open; oesophagus can expand into the gap |
| **Bronchi** | Cartilage plates, ciliated epithelium | Branches to each lobe and segment |
| **Bronchioles** | **No cartilage — smooth muscle only**; terminal bronchioles are the last purely conducting passages | Smooth muscle sets calibre, so this is where **bronchoconstriction happens** |

That tube — **~150 mL** — is the **anatomical dead space**: refreshed with every breath, never seeing a capillary.

### Cilia and the mucociliary escalator

Ciliated epithelium beats ~10–20 times per second **toward the pharynx**, dragging a 2 µm mucus layer behind it; goblet cells supply the mucus, and trapped particles are swallowed or expectorated — a **mechanical** defence layered over the immune one ([09 — Immune system](09-immune-system.md); commensals add their own in [07 — Beneficial microorganisms](../07-microbiology/07-beneficial-microorganisms.md)).

```
INHALED PARTICLE → trapped in MUCUS (hairs, turbulence, charge)
        ↓  cilia beat UP toward the pharynx — the "mucociliary escalator"
        ↓  swallowed (stomach acid finishes it) or coughed up
SMOKE OR VIRUS kills cilia → mucus STALLS → stagnant pool
        ↓  smoker's cough = the remaining clearance (bulk flow upward)
        ↓  infection risk ↑ (pneumonia below)
```

### The respiratory zone: where exchange happens

The **respiratory zone** begins at the **respiratory bronchioles** and runs through **alveolar ducts** to the **alveoli** — the first place a breath meets blood.

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Alveoli** | **~300 million** |
| **Surface area** | **~70 m²** — roughly half a badminton court folded into the chest |
| **Respiratory membrane thickness** | **~0.5 µm** (often quoted as thin as 0.2 µm in the thinnest spots) |
| **Capillary blood volume** | ~60–100 mL, all of it in contact with air at once |

### The alveolar wall, cell by cell

| Cell | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Type I pneumocyte** | Extremely thin **squamous** cell (~0.1 µm), covers **~95% of alveolar surface** | Its thinness *is* the diffusion path — structure serving Fick's law |
| **Type II pneumocyte** | Cuboidal, microvilli, lamellar bodies | Secrete **surfactant — dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC)**; also the progenitor that regenerates Type I after injury |
| **Alveolar macrophage** ("dust cell") | Motile phagocyte roaming the surface | Phagocytoses anything that escaped the escalator |
| **Capillary endothelium** | Single thin layer, fenestrated only in effect by fusion with the basement membrane | Completes the membrane; blood is never more than ~0.5 µm from air |

These are the specialisations of [01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md) pushed to their structural limit.

## Ventilation mechanics

### Boyle's law is the whole mechanism

At constant temperature and gas amount, **pressure is inversely proportional to volume (P ∝ 1/V)**. Muscles change thoracic volume, volume changes pressure, and the gradient drives air — nothing pumps air directly.

### The inspiration arrow chain

```
PHRENIC NERVE (C3–C5) fires → DIAPHRAGM CONTRACTS (dome flattens)
        + EXTERNAL INTERCOSTALS lift the ribs "up and out"
        ↓  THORACIC VOLUME ↑
        ↓  parietal pleura follows the chest wall → intrapleural SPACE expands
        ↓  lungs, held by pleural coupling, expand too → INTRAPULMONARY VOLUME ↑
        ↓  INTRAPULMONARY PRESSURE FALLS BELOW atmospheric (about −1 to −3 mmHg)
        ↓  mouth-to-alveolus gradient → AIR FLOWS IN until pressures equalise
```

**Expiration at rest is passive**: contraction stops, elastic recoil and surfactant-damped surface tension restore the chest and lung volume, pressure rises **above** atmospheric (~+1 to +3 mmHg), air leaves. **Forced** expiration recruits internal intercostals and abdominal muscles ([02 — Muscular system](02-muscular-system.md)).

| Property | Meaning | Change in disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Compliance** | ΔV per unit ΔP — how easily the lung stretches | ↑ in emphysema (floppy); ↓ in fibrosis (stiff) |
| **Elastic recoil** | Tendency to return to resting volume | ↓ in emphysema → air trapping |

### Surface tension and surfactant: Laplace's law

About **two-thirds of resting lung recoil comes from water at the air–liquid interface** of the alveolar lining, not from elastin. Surface water pulls inward — the same surface properties as in [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md) — and the pressure it generates obeys **Laplace's law**:

```
P = 2T / r        P = collapsing pressure, T = surface tension, r = radius
```

Because **P rises as r falls**, a small alveolus would sit at higher pressure than a large one and **empty into it** — until it collapses. Surfactant prevents this:

| | Without surfactant | With surfactant (DPPC) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface tension | High, constant | **Reduced**; falls further as the alveolus shrinks and the film is compressed |
| Small alveoli | Higher P → collapse → **atelectasis** | Pressures equalise → **stable** |
| Work of breathing | Enormous | Normal |
| Clinical | **Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) of the newborn** — premature type II cells cannot make enough | Surfactant replacement therapy, antenatal steroids |

### The pleural space: why the lungs stay inflated

**Intrapleural pressure is normally negative** — roughly −4 to −8 cmH₂O — because the chest wall pulls outward while the lung pulls inward, and the sealed fluid film between them transmits the difference. That negativity holds the alveoli open at end-expiration.

```
CHEST EXPANDS → intrapleural pressure becomes MORE negative → lung is pulled open
SEAL LOST (pneumothorax: chest wound, ruptured bleb) → pleural pressure = atmospheric
        ↓  transmural gradient gone → LUNG COLLAPSES ELASTICALLY
        ↓  plus the mediastinum may shift → venous return falls → shock
```

**Pneumothorax is the loss of a negative pressure nothing actively maintains** — it exists only while the container is sealed. The same pressure swing drives the **respiratory pump** for venous return, so ventilation and circulation are inseparable ([05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md)).

## Gas exchange

### Partial pressures set every gradient

**Dalton's law**: the total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of its component partial pressures, and it is the **partial pressure**, not the percentage, that drives diffusion.

| Gas | Inspired (dry, sea level) | Alveolar air | Arterial blood | Mixed venous blood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **O₂** | ~159 mmHg (21%) | **~104 mmHg** | **~100 mmHg** | ~40 mmHg |
| **CO₂** | ~0.3 mmHg | **~40 mmHg** | **~40 mmHg** | **~45 mmHg** |
| N₂ | ~597 mmHg | ~573 mmHg | ~573 mmHg | ~570 mmHg |

Inspired air loses pressure to **water vapour and mixing** before it reaches the alveoli; **arterial pO₂ (100) sits just below alveolar (104)**; and the **CO₂ gradient is tiny (45 → 40)** while the **O₂ gradient is large (104 → 40)** — an asymmetry that matters below.

### Fick's law of diffusion

```
Rate of transfer ∝ ( AREA × DIFFUSIBILITY × PRESSURE GRADIENT ) / THICKNESS
```

| Variable | Alveolus's answer | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Area** | ~70 m² | **Emphysema** destroys alveolar walls → area ↓ |
| **Thickness** | ~0.5 µm | **Fibrosis** deposits collagen → thickness ↑ |
| **Gradient** | Large for O₂ | Low inspired O₂ (altitude), low alveolar pO₂ (hypoventilation) |
| **Diffusibility** | Fixed by the gas | CO₂ is far more soluble |

### Why diffusion defects show up in oxygen first

**CO₂ is about 20 times more soluble in tissue than O₂**, so it crosses even a thickened, scarred membrane with ease. Oxygen, less soluble, fails first: **diffusion impairment causes hypoxaemia before CO₂ retention** — a raised pCO₂ is a *late* sign of diffusion disease and an *early* sign of pure hypoventilation.

```
ALVEOLUS pO₂ 104 → RBC (pO₂ ~40 entering) → O₂ DISSOLVES, then binds haemoglobin
ALVEOLUS pCO₂ 40 ← RBC (pCO₂ 45 entering) → CO₂ LEAVES DOWN ITS GRADIENT
        ↓  equilibration complete in ~0.25 s — only a THIRD of capillary transit time
        ↓  flat reserve: perfusion can quadruple in exercise before gradients run out
```

## Gas transport in the blood

### Oxygen: haemoglobin and the sigmoid curve

| Form of O₂ | Fraction | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dissolved in plasma** | **~1.5%** | Sets **pO₂** — the number on an arterial blood gas; the only form exerting partial pressure |
| **Bound to haemoglobin (O₂Hb)** | **~98.5%** | Bulk carriage — ~1.34 mL O₂ per gram of Hb |

Four haemoglobin subunits, each with a haem group and Fe²⁺, bind O₂ **cooperatively**: the first molecule binds the tense (**T**) state poorly and shifts the molecule toward the relaxed (**R**) state, so the next bindings are progressively easier. The plot of saturation against pO₂ is therefore the **sigmoid dissociation curve**, **P₅₀ ≈ 26 mmHg**:

| Region of curve | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Upper, flat (pO₂ > 70)** | Loading is safe — a fall from 100 to 80 barely changes saturation; this is why **pulse oximetry is insensitive to early hypoxia** |
| **Steep middle (pO₂ 26–60)** | Unloading: small pressure drops release large amounts of O₂ to active tissue |
| **Right shift** | Lower affinity — more unloading where it is needed |

### The Bohr effect: unloading where metabolism happens

Anything the metabolising tissue produces **shifts the curve right**, lowering affinity precisely where CO₂ has been made ([00 — Acids, bases and pH](../00-foundations/06-acids-bases-and-ph.md)):

| Factor | Shift | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **↑ pCO₂** | **Right** | Unloads O₂ at the tissue |
| **↓ pH (↑ H⁺)** | **Right** | Same — venous blood is slightly acidic |
| **↑ Temperature** | **Right** | Working muscle is warm |
| **↑ 2,3-BPG** | **Right** | Adaptation to altitude, anaemia, stored blood |
| **↓ pCO₂ / ↑ pH** | **Left** | Reloading in the lungs |

**Worked example:** exercising muscle at pCO₂ 50 mmHg, pH 7.30, 38 °C pulls saturation down at any given pO₂ — haemoglobin hands oxygen over *because* the tissue is working, and the lungs then restore left-shifted loading conditions.

### Carbon dioxide: mostly bicarbonate

| Form | Fraction | Where it is made |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** | **~70%** | **Carbonic anhydrase in the red cell** |
| **Carbaminohaemoglobin** | **~23%** | Bound to globin, not haem — **deoxy**haemoglobin binds it better |
| **Dissolved** | **~7%** | Sets pCO₂ |

```
IN THE TISSUE CAPILLARY
CO₂ diffuses into the RBC
        ↓  CO₂ + H₂O --CARBONIC ANHYDRASE--> H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
HCO₃⁻ is EXPORTED to plasma; Cl⁻ moves in ("CHLORIDE SHIFT")
        ↓  H⁺ is BUFFERED BY DEOXYHAEMOGLOBIN (a better buffer when deoxygenated)
IN THE LUNG — the whole sequence reverses as O₂ binds
H⁺ released from Hb + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ --CA--> CO₂ + H₂O → CO₂ EXHALED
```

Two linked effects: the **Haldane effect** — **deoxygenated blood carries more CO₂**, because deoxyhaemoglobin binds CO₂ better and buffers H⁺ better. It is the mirror image of the **Bohr effect**, so each circulation is efficient both ways: **loaded with O₂ exactly where CO₂ is dumped, loaded with CO₂ exactly where O₂ is dumped.**

## Control of breathing

### The rhythm generator

| Centre | Location | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dorsal respiratory group** | Medulla | Sets the basic rhythm; **inspiration** |
| **Ventral respiratory group** (incl. **pre-Bötzinger complex**) | Medulla | Generates rhythm; recruits **forced expiration** |
| **Pontine centres** (pneumotaxic, apneustic) | Pons | **Fine-tune** — truncate inspiration so breathing is smooth, not gasping |

The output leaves the medulla via the **phrenic nerve (diaphragm)** and **intercostal nerves**; voluntary control descends from the cortex, which is why you can hold your breath temporarily ([03 — Nervous system](03-nervous-system.md)).

### Chemoreceptors: two locations, one dominant signal

| Feature | **Central chemoreceptors** | **Peripheral chemoreceptors** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Where** | Ventrolateral **medulla** (outside the BBB) | **Carotid bodies** (nerve IX), **aortic bodies** (nerve X) |
| **Stimulus** | **H⁺ in interstitial fluid and CSF** — CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier freely while H⁺ does not, so **CO₂ is what raises it** | **↓ pO₂ (steep below 60 mmHg)**, ↑ pCO₂, ↓ arterial pH |
| **Share of drive** | **~70–80%** of the total response to CO₂ | Small at rest; **the only O₂ sensor in the body** |
| **Speed** | Slower (CO₂ must build in CSF) | Fast — seconds |

```
CO₂ RISES (exercise, airway obstruction, sedation)
        ↓  CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier → H₂CO₃ → H⁺
        ↓  CENTRAL chemoreceptors fire faster → medullary centre ↑ rate and depth
        ↓  ventilation ↑ → CO₂ BLOWN OFF → pCO₂ falls → loop idles
```

**CO₂ — not O₂ — is the dominant day-to-day drive.** A normal person tolerates pO₂ down to 60 mmHg before peripheral chemoreceptors meaningfully increase ventilation, while a few mmHg of pCO₂ produce a brisk response. The loop structure is in [03 — Homeostasis: the full control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md).

### The clinical twist: hypoxic drive

In **chronic CO₂ retention** (long-standing COPD) the kidney holds HCO₃⁻ for days, **normalising CSF pH**, so the central receptor no longer responds to the chronically high CO₂. The remaining stimulus is **hypoxia**, sensed only by the carotid bodies — and uncontrolled high-flow oxygen **abolishes the last drive to breathe**, letting pCO₂ rise further. Hence **controlled oxygen (24–28%, Venturi mask)**: oxygenation is titrated, never assumed.

### Other inputs, and respiratory compensation of pH

Breathing also adjusts to **proprioceptors** in moving limbs, pain, emotion, fever, and voluntary cortex command. Because CO₂ is an acid anhydride, **ventilation is a fast lever on pH**: blowing off CO₂ raises pH, retaining it lowers pH — the **respiratory limb of acid–base compensation**, used in metabolic acidosis (deep **Kussmaul** breathing) and in the blood gas table below ([00 — Acids, bases and pH](../00-foundations/06-acids-bases-and-ph.md)).

## Lung volumes and capacities

A **volume** is one compartment; a **capacity** is a sum of two or more volumes.

| Measurement | Definition | Typical adult value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | One quiet breath | **~500 mL** |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | Extra inspired above TV | ~3,000 mL |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | Extra expired below TV | ~1,100 mL |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Left after maximal expiration | **~1,200 mL — never empty** |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | TV + IRV + ERV | **~4,600 mL** |
| **Total lung capacity (TLC)** | VC + RV | ~6,000 mL |
| **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** | ERV + RV — volume sitting in the lung between breaths | ~2,300 mL |

**Residual volume is why the alveolus never empties** — gas exchange is continuous, not intermittent. A spirometer measures only expired air, so TLC and RV need gas dilution or plethysmography.

### Spirometry patterns: a mechanism table

| Feature | **Obstructive** | **Restrictive** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Basic fault** | Resistance ↑ — air gets in but **out slowly** | Lung or chest wall will not **stretch** |
| **FEV₁** | **↓↓** | ↓ |
| **FVC** | ↓ (mild) or near normal | **↓** |
| **FEV₁/FVC** | **< 0.70 — the diagnostic row** | **Normal or ↑** |
| **TLC** | Normal or ↑ (air trapping) | **↓** |
| **RV** | **↑↑** | ↓ |
| **Examples** | **Asthma, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis** | **Pulmonary fibrosis, pneumothorax, kyphoscoliosis, neuromuscular weakness** |
| **Mechanism** | Narrowed airway + lost recoil → dynamic air collapse on expiration | Stiff lung or restricted wall → normal volume unreachable |

### Ventilation–perfusion matching

Gas exchange needs **both** ventilation (V) and perfusion (Q): the ideal alveolus has **V/Q ≈ 1**, and the whole lung averages **~0.8** because of regional differences and a small physiological shunt.

```
ALVEOLAR pO₂ FALLS (mucus plug, collapse, low inspired O₂)
        ↓  LOCAL pulmonary arteriole CONSTRICTS (hypoxic vasoconstriction)
        ↓  blood REDIRECTED to better-ventilated units
        ↓  V/Q matched again → arterial pO₂ defended
```

**Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is unique to the lung**: everywhere else hypoxia **dilates** arterioles to feed metabolising tissue ([05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md)); the lung constricts because its job is not to feed itself but to **match blood to air**. The two extremes are named after what is wasted: **dead space** (ventilated, not perfused — embolism) and **shunt** (perfused, not ventilated — collapse, pneumonia).

## Medical relevance

**Asthma is reversible obstruction with an immune trigger.** Allergen cross-links **IgE** on mast cells → degranulation → histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins → bronchial smooth muscle contracts while oedema and mucus narrow the lumen ([09 — Immune system](09-immune-system.md)). **β₂ agonists** relax that muscle; inhaled corticosteroids damp the inflammation. The hallmark is **reversibility** after a bronchodilator — what separates asthma from COPD.

**COPD is two diseases in one label.** **Emphysema** destroys alveolar walls → **area lost** → diffusion defect with hyperinflation; **chronic bronchitis** → goblet metaplasia and mucus → **obstruction**. Patients trade one against the other: the "pink puffer" (near-normal gases, wasted) versus the "blue bloater" (mucus-dominated, hypoxic, oedematous). **Smoking is the shared cause** — it paralyses cilia, oxidises antiproteases so unchecked elastase chews the walls, and inflames the airway.

**Pneumonia fills the exchange space.** Alveoli full of exudate are ventilated but not diffusing — a shunt. **Encapsulated organisms**, above all ***Streptococcus pneumoniae***, resist macrophage uptake because the capsule blocks attachment ([01 — Bacterial structure and physiology](../07-microbiology/01-bacterial-structure-and-physiology.md)); the **pneumococcal vaccine** opsonises that capsule, and the **mucociliary escalator** is the first line the organism must survive.

**Pulmonary embolism is perfusion loss with ventilation intact.** A deep-vein thrombus lodges in the pulmonary artery, starving a block of alveoli of blood — **increased physiological dead space**. The patient hyperventilates (low pCO₂, respiratory alkalosis) yet stays hypoxic: **ventilation without perfusion is wasted effort**, the mirror image of a shunt.

**Pneumothorax breaks the pressure linkage.** Loss of the sealed, negative pleural space lets the lung collapse; a tension pneumothorax adds a one-way valve, pushing the mediastinum and **compressing the great veins** — a cardiovascular emergency caused by a respiratory breach.

**ARDS is surfactant failure in the adult.** Sepsis, aspiration, or pneumonia injure the epithelium; plasma protein floods in and **inactivates surfactant** → alveoli flood and collapse → stiff lungs, shunt, refractory hypoxaemia. **PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure)** holds them open at end-expiration, restoring FRC and countering the Laplace collapse.

**Arterial blood gases are the acid–base triangle in one sample.**

| Pattern | pH | PaCO₂ | HCO₃⁻ | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | 7.35–7.45 | 35–45 | 22–26 | — |
| **Acute respiratory acidosis** | **↓** | **↑** | normal/slightly ↑ | Ventilation failing (COPD exacerbation, opioid) |
| **Chronic respiratory acidosis** | near normal | **↑** | **↑↑** | Kidney has retained HCO₃⁻ — compensation, not correction |
| **Metabolic acidosis, compensated** | **↓** | **↓** | **↓** | Lungs blowing off CO₂ (Kussmaul) |
| **Respiratory alkalosis** | **↑** | **↓** | normal/↓ | Hyperventilation — anxiety, PE, altitude |

Rule of thumb: **pH opposite to CO₂ means a respiratory cause; pH tracking HCO₃⁻ means a metabolic one.** Type 1 respiratory failure is **low O₂ alone**; type 2 adds **raised CO₂** — hypoventilation.

**Oxygen and ventilators are mechanical fixes for the three problems.** Supplemental O₂ raises the alveolar gradient (helping diffusion loss and shunt) but does nothing for obstruction, and can blunt hypoxic drive in chronic retainers. A ventilator supplies the pressure gradient directly when muscles cannot; **PEEP** supplies the end-expiratory pressure that surfactant and recoil would maintain.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Expiration is an active process" | At rest it is **passive** — elastic recoil and damped surface tension do the work. Internal intercostals and abdominals are recruited only for forced expiration. |
| "The conducting zone exchanges gas" | It **conditions and moves** air only; its ~150 mL is **anatomical dead space**. Exchange begins at the **respiratory bronchiole**. |
| "pO₂ on a blood gas equals oxygen content" | **PaO₂ measures only the ~1.5% dissolved**; **content** is dominated by haemoglobin-bound O₂. Anaemia gives a normal pO₂ with dangerously low content. |
| "Pulse oximetry measures pO₂" | It measures **haemoglobin saturation (SpO₂)**, sitting on the flat upper curve — so it misses early hypoxia and cannot report dissolved O₂ at all. |
| "A low pO₂ means CO₂ must be high" | **Diffusion defects impair O₂ first** because CO₂ is ~20× more soluble. Raised CO₂ signals **hypoventilation**, not a thick membrane. |
| "Oxygen is the main stimulus to breathe" | **CO₂ (via H⁺ at central chemoreceptors) drives ~70–80% of resting ventilation**; O₂ matters only when pO₂ falls below ~60 mmHg. |
| "Giving high-flow O₂ to a breathless COPD patient is always safe" | In chronic CO₂ retainers the **hypoxic drive** may be all that remains, so uncontrolled O₂ can suppress ventilation further — titrate to 24–28%. |
| "V/Q mismatch always means not enough air" | Either side can fail: **dead space** = ventilated but unperfused (embolism); **shunt** = perfused but unventilated (collapse, pneumonia). |
| "Fibrosis and emphysema both reduce diffusion" | By different terms in Fick's law: fibrosis **increases thickness**, emphysema **destroys area**. Compliance moves in opposite directions (↓ vs ↑). |

## Key facts

- Three separate problems — **bulk flow** down a gradient, **diffusion** across ~0.5 µm, **haemoglobin binding** — fail clinically as **obstruction, diffusion loss, and perfusion mismatch**.
- The **conducting zone** (nose → bronchioles) warms, humidifies, filters, and holds **~150 mL of dead space**; the **respiratory zone** starts at respiratory bronchioles: **~300 million alveoli, ~70 m²**.
- Alveolar wall: thin **Type I pneumocytes** (diffusion), **Type II pneumocytes (surfactant — DPPC)**, **macrophages**, over a dense capillary network.
- Inspiration: **diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thoracic volume ↑ → intrapleural space expands → intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric → air flows in** (Boyle's law, P ∝ 1/V); rest expiration is **passive recoil**.
- **Laplace's law (P = 2T/r)** means small alveoli would collapse without surfactant — the mechanism of **neonatal RDS**; **intrapleural negativity keeps lungs inflated**, and pneumothorax abolishes it.
- **Fick's law**: rate ∝ area × gradient ÷ thickness — **emphysema destroys area, fibrosis thickens the membrane**; because **CO₂ is ~20× more soluble**, O₂ fails first and CO₂ retention is late.
- **O₂: ~98.5% on haemoglobin, ~1.5% dissolved** (the dissolved fraction sets pO₂); cooperative binding gives the **sigmoid curve**, **P₅₀ ≈ 26 mmHg**.
- **Bohr effect** (↑CO₂, ↓pH, ↑temp, ↑2,3-BPG → right shift) unloads O₂ at working tissue; the **Haldane effect** lets deoxygenated blood carry more CO₂.
- **CO₂ transport: 70% bicarbonate** (carbonic anhydrase + chloride shift), **23% carbaminohaemoglobin**, **7% dissolved**.
- Breathing is rhythmic from the **medullary dorsal (inspiratory) and ventral groups**, fine-tuned by the **pons**; **central chemoreceptors read CO₂-derived H⁺ (~70–80% of drive)**, **peripheral chemoreceptors read pO₂ <60 mmHg, CO₂, and acidosis**.
- **Chronic CO₂ retainers rely on hypoxic drive**, so oxygen must be controlled; **ventilation is the fast lever on blood pH** (respiratory compensation).
- **Spirometry**: obstructive = **FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 with ↑RV**; restrictive = **↓TLC with a normal ratio**; residual volume never empties.
- **Hypoxic vasoconstriction** redirects blood from poorly ventilated alveoli — the opposite of systemic hypoxic vasodilation — so mismatch shows as **dead space (embolism)** or **shunt (collapse)**.

## Practice questions

**1. During quiet inspiration, air enters the lungs because**

A. Thoracic volume rises, intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric, and air flows down the gradient
B. The diaphragm actively creates a vacuum by expanding the alveolar cells
C. The respiratory muscles pump air directly into the alveoli
D. Elastic recoil of the chest wall raises alveolar pressure above atmospheric pressure

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Muscles change volume, volume changes pressure (Boyle's law), and the gradient drives flow — nothing pumps air. Elastic recoil raises pressure and drives air *out*, so expiration at rest is passive.

---

**2. A premature neonate develops progressive atelectasis and chest retractions. The mechanism most directly involved is**

A. Loss of intrapleural negativity
B. Failure of the mucociliary escalator
C. Excessive surface tension causing small alveoli to collapse by Laplace's law
D. Destruction of alveolar surface area by proteases

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Immature type II pneumocytes make too little surfactant, so surface tension stays high and, since P = 2T/r, small alveoli collapse into larger ones. A describes pneumothorax; D describes emphysema.

**3. A patient with emphysema and one with pulmonary fibrosis both have impaired gas exchange. In Fick's law they differ in**

A. The pressure gradient, which is zero in both
B. Emphysema reduces area, fibrosis increases thickness
C. Fibrosis reduces area, emphysema increases thickness
D. Diffusibility of oxygen, which differs between the two

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Emphysema destroys alveolar walls, cutting the area term; fibrosis deposits collagen, raising the thickness term — the divisor. Gradient and gas diffusibility are unchanged by either disease.

**4. Which statement about oxygen transport is correct?**

A. The dissolved fraction determines pO₂, while haemoglobin carries the bulk
B. About 98.5% of oxygen is carried dissolved in plasma
C. Pulse oximetry reports the dissolved partial pressure directly
D. Haemoglobin binds oxygen non-cooperatively, giving a linear curve

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Only ~1.5% is dissolved, yet that fraction exerts the pO₂ reported on a blood gas; ~98.5% rides on haemoglobin. Pulse oximetry reads haemoglobin saturation, and four subunits bind cooperatively, giving the sigmoid curve.

**5. Active skeletal muscle unloads more oxygen than resting muscle at the same arterial pO₂ mainly because of**

A. A left shift of the dissociation curve caused by cooling
B. Increased 2,3-BPG in the arterial blood
C. The Bohr effect — local ↑CO₂, ↓pH and ↑temperature shift the curve right
D. Conversion of haemoglobin to the tense T state by oxygen itself

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Working muscle produces CO₂, H⁺ and heat, all of which lower haemoglobin's affinity and shift the curve right, so more O₂ is released at any given pO₂. Cooling shifts the curve left.

**6. The dominant day-to-day stimulus to breathe is**

A. Arterial pO₂ falling below 60 mmHg
B. CO₂ raising H⁺ at central chemoreceptors in the medulla
C. Plasma potassium concentration
D. Stretch of pulmonary stretch receptors

**Answer: B**

Explanation: CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier and forms H⁺ where central chemoreceptors sit, supplying ~70–80% of resting drive; the O₂ response matters only below ~60 mmHg, sensed peripherally.

**7. A patient with long-standing COPD and chronic hypercapnia is given high-flow oxygen and becomes drowsy with a rising pCO₂. The best explanation is**

A. Oxygen directly paralyses the diaphragm
B. Oxygen converted the disorder from obstructive to restrictive
C. Oxygen increased the surface tension of the alveoli
D. The oxygen abolished the hypoxic drive that had become their main ventilatory stimulus

**Answer: D**

Explanation: With chronically raised CO₂, renal bicarbonate retention normalises CSF pH, so carotid-body hypoxia becomes the remaining drive. High-flow oxygen removes it, ventilation falls, and CO₂ rises — hence controlled 24–28% oxygen.

**8. Spirometry shows FEV₁ 1.2 L, FVC 3.6 L, ratio 0.33, and residual volume is markedly increased. The pattern is**

A. Restrictive, because vital capacity is reduced
B. Obstructive, because airflow is limited and air is trapped on expiration
C. Normal, because all values lie within adult ranges
D. Restrictive, because FEV₁ is below normal

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The diagnostic row is the FEV₁/FVC ratio — below 0.70 means obstruction; here 0.33 with raised residual volume shows air trapping. Restrictive disease lowers volumes but keeps the ratio normal or raised.

**9. After a pulmonary embolism, arterial pO₂ falls while pCO₂ is low. The mechanism is**

A. Increased physiological dead space — ventilation without perfusion
B. A shunt with continued perfusion of non-ventilated alveoli
C. Thickening of the respiratory membrane
D. Reduced haemoglobin affinity for oxygen

**Answer: A**

Explanation: The embolus blocks perfusion while ventilation continues, so fresh air is wasted — dead space — and the patient blows CO₂ down by hyperventilating. Option B describes a shunt, as in pneumonia.

**10. An arterial blood gas reads pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 60 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 26 mmol/L. The interpretation is**

A. Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation
B. Respiratory alkalosis
C. Respiratory acidosis, uncompensated or acutely compensated
D. Fully compensated metabolic alkalosis

**Answer: C**

Explanation: pH is low and PaCO₂ is high — pH moving opposite to CO₂ identifies a respiratory cause, while the near-normal HCO₃⁻ shows renal retention has not yet caught up. A metabolic cause would show pH falling with HCO₃⁻.
