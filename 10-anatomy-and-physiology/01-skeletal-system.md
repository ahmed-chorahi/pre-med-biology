# The Skeletal System

## Why it matters

The adult skeleton is about **206 bones**, and it is the only organ system that is at once frame, levers, armour, mineral bank and blood factory:

```
SUPPORT — bears load          LEVERAGE — bones as bars, joints as fulcrums
PROTECTION — brain, cord,     MINERAL RESERVOIR — 99% of body calcium,
             heart, lungs                    released on demand
HAEMATOPOIESIS — red marrow makes every blood cell (+ fat in yellow marrow)
        ↓ all five jobs run from ONE tissue
BONE — a mineralised, vascular, self-remodelling connective tissue
```

Two facts make the rest derivable. **Bone is a living connective tissue** of the class in [01 — The four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md): its cells secrete the abundant extracellular matrix that does the work, then get buried in it. And **the skeleton remodels itself according to the loads placed on it** — use it and it thickens, ignore it and it dissolves, which is why a racquet arm is thicker than its pair and bed rest strips bone. Bone is therefore an organ in the sense of [02 — Organs and organ systems](../09-human-biology/02-organs-and-organ-systems.md): a vascular, self-maintaining mass of several tissue types.

The chapter follows **structure → function → mechanism → regulation → homeostasis**, because each structural feature answers a problem: diffusion through mineral, load along a line, calcium held against a gradient.

## Bone as an organ

### Compact versus spongy bone

| Feature | **Compact (cortical)** | **Spongy (cancellous, trabecular)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Look | Dense and solid; ~80% of skeleton mass | Honeycomb of struts (**trabeculae**) with marrow spaces |
| Where | Shaft (diaphysis) and outer shell of every bone | Ends (epiphyses); inside flat and irregular bones |
| Unit | Built of **osteons** | No osteons; lamellae follow stress lines |
| Why | A hollow tube gives maximum stiffness for minimum weight | Material is saved where strength is not needed; struts lie **along the load lines** |
Femoral-neck trabeculae arch along the lines of walking, and osteoporosis erodes them from inside.

### The osteon: structure built around diffusion

The unit of compact bone is the **osteon (Haversian system)** — rings about 200 µm across.

| Part | What it is | Why it is *here* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Central (Haversian) canal** | Lengthwise canal with **capillary, venule, nerve**, cross-linked by **perforating (Volkmann's) canals** | Matrix blocks bulk flow, so the **only** supply runs in canals, joined so the bone is one tree |
| **Concentric lamellae** | 4–20 rings, collagen in **alternating directions** | Alternating ply stops a crack running through — plywood logic |
| **Lacunae** | Cavities each holding one **osteocyte** | The builder is trapped by its own matrix, in a pocket with a way out |
| **Canaliculi** | Microscopic channels radiating from every lacuna | **Why they exist:** osteocytes are buried in stone, so their **processes** thread through, joined by **gap junctions**, carrying nutrients, wastes and signals |

Mineral blocks capillaries, so cells survive by **relay rather than direct supply** — and the channels double as a sensory web.

### The three bone cells

| Cell | Origin | Job and mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Osteoblast** | Mesenchymal stem cell | **Builds** — secretes unmineralised **osteoid** (type I collagen plus proteoglycans), then crystallises **hydroxyapatite** into it; needs **ATP** ([04 — ATP and metabolism](../03-cellular-processes/04-atp-and-metabolism.md)) and collagen ([03 — Proteins](../01-biochemistry/03-proteins.md)); releases **alkaline phosphatase** |
| **Osteocyte** | An osteoblast buried in its own matrix | **Mechanosensor** in a lacuna; secretes **sclerostin**, which inhibits formation when bone is unloaded |
| **Osteoclast** | **Haematopoietic** stem cell — a monocyte relative | **Dissolves bone** — seals a **sealing zone**, pumps **H⁺ via a V-type H⁺/ATPase** through its **ruffled border** (gastric-acid logic, [06 — Acids, bases and pH](../00-foundations/06-acids-bases-and-ph.md)), then **cathepsin K** digests collagen |

The three are coupled: build, sense, destroy — old bone is **replaced**, not patched, and matrix rather than cell does the job.

### Periosteum, endosteum, and marrow

| Structure | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Periosteum** — outer fibrous layer, inner **osteogenic layer** | Anchors **tendons and ligaments** (Sharpey's fibres) and carries vessels inward; the osteogenic layer supplies **width growth** and **fracture repair** — richly innervated, hence periosteal injury hurts |
| **Endosteum** | Osteogenic lining of internal surfaces; supplies cells for remodelling |
| **Red marrow** | Haematopoietic tissue in spongy bone (sternum, pelvis, ribs, skull, epiphyses); cells enter thin-walled **sinusoids** ([05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md)) |
| **Yellow marrow** | Adipose in long-bone shafts; reconverts to red under demand |

## Bone formation, growth, and remodelling

### Intramembranous versus endochondral ossification

| | **Intramembranous** | **Endochondral** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting tissue | Flat sheet of mesenchyme | **Hyaline cartilage model** |
| Process | Mesenchyme condenses → osteoblasts differentiate directly → osteoid mineralises | Model grows as cartilage → centre ossifies → **vessels invade, osteoclasts clear cartilage, bone replaces it** |
| Bones formed | **Skull vault**, facial bones, **clavicle**, part of mandible | Limbs, pelvis, ribs, vertebrae, skull base |
| Why chosen | A thin plate carries little load and needs no internal vessels | **A large bone cannot be supplied through solid mineral.** Cartilage is avascular, grows fast, and is a template that can then be invaded — vascularisation is what makes big bones possible |

Cartilage persists afterwards only at the **growth plates** while growth continues and at **articular surfaces** for life; at puberty the plates ossify (epiphyseal closure) and longitudinal growth stops. The foetal skeleton is cartilage first, bone second ([11 — Reproduction and development](../11-reproduction-and-development/)), and that avascular start is why adult cartilage heals poorly.

### Growth plates: how bones get longer

A long bone lengthens only at its **epiphyseal (growth) plate**, a disc of cartilage between epiphysis and shaft.

| Zone | What happens | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Resting (germinative)** | Sparse chondrocytes anchored to the plate | Stem-like reservoir; anchors the plate |
| **Proliferative** | Cells divide and stack into **columns** like coins | **This is where length is made** |
| **Hypertrophic** | Cells swell greatly; matrix calcifies | Swelling pushes epiphysis from shaft; calcification stiffens the matrix and starves the cells |
| **Invasion** | Cells die; **capillaries and osteoblasts enter**; osteoid laid down | Cartilage is removed as fast as bone is laid down, so the plate holds its thickness while the bone lengthens |

Length is **cartilage growth followed by bone replacement** — bone never elongates itself — set by growth and thyroid hormones, then sex steroids at puberty. Width is separate: **appositional growth** adds bone from the periosteum while osteoclasts enlarge the marrow cavity.

### Wolff's law and mechanotransduction

**Wolff's law**: bone is deposited where stress is greatest and resorbed where stress is absent — trabeculae align with the lines of force, cortex thickens on loaded surfaces.

```
LOAD (gravity, muscle pull, impact) deforms the matrix slightly
        ↓ interstitial fluid DRAGS through CANALICULI
OSTEOCYTES sense shear → prostaglandins, nitric oxide, SCLEROSTIN falls
        ↓
HIGH-STRESS areas → osteoblasts deposit → thicker cortex/trabeculae
LOW-STRESS or MICROCRACKED areas → osteoclasts resorb → mass lost
        ↓ over weeks to months
BONE MASS AND TRABECULAR ORIENTATION MATCH THE LOAD LINES
```

Hence the racquet arm's thicker humerus and the arching trabeculae of the femoral neck — and the converse: **disuse** (cast, bed rest, spaceflight) activates osteoclasts and strips per cent of bone mass per month.

## Skeleton organisation

### Axial versus appendicular

The split is a **function** split: what is central is guarded, what is peripheral moves.

| | **Axial (80 bones)** | **Appendicular (126 bones)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parts | **Skull (22)**, **vertebral column (26)**, **ribcage (24 ribs + sternum)**, hyoid, ossicles | **Limbs** plus the **pectoral and pelvic girdles** attaching them |
| Primary job | **Protection** — encloses brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs | **Movement** — long levers muscles swing; girdles pass limb load to the axis |
| Shape and origin | Mostly flat and irregular; skull vault intramembranous | Long (limbs) and irregular (girdles); entirely endochondral |

The pelvic girdle is appendicular yet weight-bearing, so hip fractures matter far more than their location suggests; the ribcage is axial yet must move for breathing.

### Bone shapes: form follows the load

| Shape | Geometry | Examples | Why this shape |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Long** | Shaft with expanded ends | **Femur, humerus, phalanges** | A hollow tube gives maximum stiffness for minimum mass; ends spread load at joints |
| **Short** | Cube-like, one face per side | **Carpals, tarsals** | Many small joints share compression yet still allow movement |
| **Flat** | Thin broad plates | **Skull vault, sternum, scapula, ribs** | A wide **shield** for an organ and a wide anchor for muscle |
| **Irregular** | Complex, fits nothing else | **Vertebrae, hip bone** | A ring for the cord, a body for load, processes for muscle and ribs |
| **Sesamoid** | Small, round, inside a tendon | **Patella** | Raises the tendon's **leverage angle**, reduces friction, protects the tendon |

## Joints

A **joint (articulation)** is any place two bones meet, classified first by **what binds them**, then — within the movable class — by **shape**: the more a union gives way, the more it moves and the less stable it is.

### Fibrous and cartilaginous joints

| Class | Binding material | Movement | Examples and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fibrous** | Dense fibrous tissue continuous with periosteum | **None (sutures)**, slight (syndesmosis) | Skull **sutures** make one rigid box that spreads a blow over its whole area |
| **Synchondrosis** | **Hyaline cartilage** | **None** | Growth plate, first rib to sternum — **temporary**: holds while bone grows, then becomes bone |
| **Symphysis** | **Fibrocartilage** disc | **Slight** gliding and compression | **Intervertebral discs**, pubic symphysis: proteoglycan water is a shock absorber, and slight give lets the pelvis widen in childbirth |

### The synovial joint: structure before movement

Every synovial joint solves one problem: **two hard surfaces rubbing under load would grind to dust.**

| Component | Why it is *here* |
| --- | --- |
| **Articular capsule** — outer **fibrous layer**, inner **synovial membrane** | Fibrous layer resists distraction and gives stability; the membrane lines everything *except* cartilage. Its type A cells clear debris, its type B cells secrete **hyaluronan (hyaluronic acid)** and **lubricin** |
| **Synovial fluid** | **Lubricin** coats cartilage, **hyaluronan** gives squeeze-film viscosity — friction lower than ice on ice. Cartilage is avascular, so the fluid is also its **nutrition and waste route** |
| **Articular cartilage** — 2–4 mm of hyaline | Bears compression and absorbs impact; **aneural** (painless until bone beneath is exposed) and **avascular** (heals poorly) |
| **Menisci / discs** — fibrocartilage pads | **Deepen the socket**, turn point contact into broad load-bearing, damp shock |
| **Ligaments and bursae** — dense regular collagen; synovial-lined sacs | Ligaments cap the range, resist dislocation and carry **mechanoreceptors** for proprioception — hence a sprained joint feels unstable; bursae stop tendons grinding on bone |

**Synovial joints are the most mobile and least stable by construction**: fusion buys strength by surrendering movement; a fluid-filled gap buys movement by surrendering fusion. Stability therefore comes from shape, ligaments and above all **muscle tone** — which is why joint rehabilitation is muscle retraining, and the shallow-socketed shoulder dislocates most easily.

### Synovial subtypes: shape sets the permitted movement

| Type | Surfaces | Movements | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hinge (ginglymus)** | Convex into concave, like a door | **Flexion and extension only** | **Elbow**, interphalangeal joints |
| **Ball-and-socket** | Head in a deep socket | **All movements**, including rotation | **Shoulder** (shallow socket → dislocates); **hip** (deep socket → fractures instead) |
| **Pivot** | Ring on a peg | **Rotation about one axis** | **Atlas–axis (C1–C2)** turns the head; **radius on ulna** turns the palm |
| **Saddle (sellar)** | Concave and convex at right angles | Two axes, **opposition** | **First carpometacarpal (thumb)** — opposition defines hand function |
| **Gliding (plane)** | Flat faces | **Sliding** in one plane | **Intercarpal, intertarsal**, spinal facets — small movements that **add up** |
| **Condyloid (ellipsoid)** | Oval head in an elliptical socket | Flex/ext, ab/adduction, circumduction — **no rotation** | **Radiocarpal wrist**, knuckles |

## The skeleton's five functions

### 1. Support and leverage — the lever classes

Because bone is rigid and muscle can only **pull**, movement takes the form of **levers**: **fulcrum** (joint), **effort** (muscle via tendon), **load** (limb or object). Which sits in the middle defines the class — and the class, what is traded.

| Class | Arrangement | In the body | What it buys |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **First class** | Effort — **fulcrum** — load (see-saw) | **Nodding the head**: atlanto-occipital joint between the head's weight and the neck muscles | Balance and reversal of direction |
| **Second class** | Fulcrum — **load** — effort (wheelbarrow) | **Standing on tiptoe**: fulcrum at the toe joints, load is body weight at the ankle, effort is calf muscle on the heel | **Mechanical advantage > 1** — a small muscle lifts the whole body |
| **Third class** | Fulcrum — **effort** — load (tweezers) | **Biceps flexing the elbow**: insertion just distal to the joint | **Speed and range** — a small shortening moves the hand far. **The most common class** |

The body trades force for **distal speed and reach**. Muscles and tendons belong to [02 — Muscular system](02-muscular-system.md).

### 2. Protection

Protection works by **rigidity and geometry**: a mineralised shell spreads a local force over a wide area.

| Structure | Encloses | Protective geometry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cranium** | Brain | Rigid vault joined by **immovable sutures** — energy must be absorbed by fracture elsewhere |
| **Vertebral column** | **Spinal cord** | Each vertebra is a **bony ring** behind a weight-bearing body, so the cord sits inside bone |
| **Ribcage** | Heart, lungs, great vessels | Cartilage at the front lets the thorax **expand for breathing** and **absorb impact by deforming** |

### 3. Mineral reservoir

Bone stores about **99% of the body's calcium** as **hydroxyapatite, Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂**, within the type I collagen of the osteoid. Blood calcium must hold **2.2–2.7 mmol/L**: lower and neurons fire spontaneously (tetany, arrhythmia), higher and stones or confusion follow — yet nerve conduction, muscle contraction, clotting and hormone secretion all need that range. The skeleton is a reservoir precisely because **calcium cannot be held anywhere else at that concentration**.

**Collagen gives tensile strength, mineral gives compressive hardness** — concrete reinforced with rebar: without mineral bone is soft (rickets), without collagen it is brittle (osteogenesis imperfecta). Because the store is endocrine-controlled, the skeleton shares one loop with gut and kidney:

```
LOW BLOOD Ca2+ → PTH → osteoclast RESORPTION of hydroxyapatite → Ca2+ into blood
HIGH BLOOD Ca2+ → CALCITONIN → resorption inhibited → Ca2+ banked in bone
VITAMIN D (calcitriol) → gut ABSORPTION of dietary Ca2+ → reservoir refilled
```

### 4. Haematopoiesis

Red marrow makes roughly **200 billion erythrocytes a day**, plus every leucocyte and platelet. Stem cells sit on **spongy trabeculae**, mature squeezed between struts, and pass through thin-walled **sinusoids** into the venous return — a **sheltered, vascular, high-surface-area niche**. Haemorrhage or altitude converts **yellow → red** marrow, marrow disease causes bone pain, and the **sternum and iliac crest** are used for biopsy as spongy and superficial. Bulk transport of the output is [05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md).

### 5. Fat storage

Yellow marrow is one of the largest adipose depots — roughly a tenth of skeletal mass — an **energy reserve** mobilised when marrow must make red cells again. Fat as a tissue is in [01 — The four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md).

## Regulation of bone mass

Bone is not fixed: adults rework about **5–10% of the skeleton each year** through **remodelling**, in which resorption and formation are coupled within millimetres of each other.

### The remodelling cycle

| Step | Cells | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Activation** | Osteocytes, osteoblasts, T cells | Damage, hormonal or mechanical signals raise **RANKL**, which binds **RANK** on precursors → they fuse and mature |
| **Resorption** | Osteoclast | Sealing zone, **ruffled-border H⁺ pump**, cathepsin K → a pit; Ca²⁺ released into blood |
| **Reversal** | Monocyte-derived cells | Debris cleared; the pit is marked for refill |
| **Formation and rest** | Osteoblasts, then lining cells | Osteoid fills the pit and mineralises, then the surface goes quiet until the next stimulus |

If resorption outruns formation over many cycles, bone mass falls — that one imbalance is the mechanism of osteoporosis.

### The three hormones: the calcium arrow chain

| Signal | Source and stimulus | Action | Net effect on blood Ca²⁺ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** | Parathyroid glands; **low** Ca²⁺ via the calcium-sensing receptor | Acts on osteoblasts → **RANKL up, osteoprotegerin down** → **osteoclasts stimulated** | **Raises** Ca²⁺; also reabsorbs it in the kidney and **activates vitamin D** |
| **Calcitonin** | Thyroid parafollicular **C cells**; **high** Ca²⁺ | **Inhibits osteoclasts** | **Lowers** Ca²⁺ by banking it — minor in adults, important in infancy |
| **Vitamin D — calcitriol** | Skin → liver → **kidney** 1α-hydroxylase (switched on by PTH) | Acts on the **gut**, inducing TRPV6 and calcium-binding proteins | **Raises** Ca²⁺ supply so the reservoir refills |

```
BLOOD Ca2+ FALLS → parathyroid CaSR detects it → PTH RELEASED
        ├→ osteoblast RANKL ↑ → OSTEOCLASTS resorb bone → Ca2+ out
        ├→ KIDNEY: Ca2+ reabsorbed; 1α-hydroxylase ON → CALCITRIOL made
        └→ CALCITRIOL → GUT: Ca2+ (and phosphate) absorption ↑
        ↓
BLOOD Ca2+ RISES → negative feedback → PTH secretion falls        (see 04)
============================================================
BLOOD Ca2+ RISES → thyroid C cells release CALCITONIN
        ↓ osteoclasts inhibited → Ca2+ deposited as hydroxyapatite
BLOOD Ca2+ FALLS → calcitonin secretion falls
```

**PTH resorbs bone to protect the blood, calcitonin banks it when blood is rich, and vitamin D refills it from the diet** — which is why vitamin D deficiency starves both blood and bone. The loop is receptor → control centre → effector → response ([03 — Homeostasis control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md), [09 — Homeostasis](../00-foundations/09-homeostasis.md)); the glands are [04 — Endocrine system](04-endocrine-system.md) and renal vitamin D activation is [08 — Urinary system](08-urinary-system.md).
### Homeostasis: bone inside the whole-body loop

Calcium control is a multi-organ negative-feedback loop in which **bone is the reservoir, not the set-point holder**: receptors are the parathyroid CaSR and osteocyte mechanoreceptors, control centres the parathyroid and thyroid C cells, effectors osteoclasts, osteoblasts, kidney tubules and gut epithelium. Three rules follow — **load is a signal** (no input → resorption dominates), **hormone supply sets the reservoir** (oestrogen loss → resorption unopposed), and **chronic acidosis promotes calcium loss** ([06 — Acids, bases and pH](../00-foundations/06-acids-bases-and-ph.md)). The pumps involved are **ATP-driven** ([03 — Active transport](../03-cellular-processes/03-active-transport.md)), and osteocytes sense deformation through their cytoskeleton ([05 — Structure and movement](../02-cell-biology/05-structure-and-movement.md)).

## Medical relevance

**Osteoporosis is a remodelling imbalance.** After about 35, formation no longer fully replaces resorption, and the gap widens sharply at **menopause**: oestrogen restrains osteoclast lifespan and RANKL signalling, so its withdrawal leaves resorption unopposed and trabeculae and cortex thin. Fractures follow where load concentrates on bone that has lost its struts — **vertebral bodies** (crush → height loss, often silent), the **distal radius** (fall on an outstretched hand), and the **femoral neck**, the fracture that carries real mortality. Glucocorticoids, smoking, immobility, low weight and calcium or vitamin D deficiency act on the same balance. **DEXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry)** gives a T-score at hip and spine; **antiresorptives** such as bisphosphonates disable osteoclasts, and prevention is weight-bearing exercise, **calcium and vitamin D**, and managing oestrogen loss.

**Rickets and osteomalacia are failures of mineralisation, not of quantity.** Too little vitamin D (little sun, poor diet, malabsorption, kidney failure) means low intestinal calcium and phosphate, so **osteoid is laid but never mineralised**. In a child the growth plate disorganises and softens, so weight-bearing legs bow (**genu varum**) with widened wrists — **rickets**. In an adult with closed plates the same defect softens existing bone — **osteomalacia**, with pain, weakness and "Looser's" fractures. The crux: osteoporosis is **too little bone**, osteomalacia **too little mineral in a normal amount of bone**.

**Paget's disease is remodelling run without control.** Focal areas show **excessive, abnormal osteoclasts** resorbing far too fast, followed by chaotic woven-bone replacement in a **mosaic pattern** — larger, more vascular, more brittle bone: bowed tibia, expanded skull, deafness from compression of the auditory nerve, high-output strain, fracture. **Alkaline phosphatase is high while calcium and PTH stay normal**; bisphosphonates suppress the osteoclast excess.

**Fracture healing is the remodelling sequence played out in weeks**, possible because bone is vascular and cellular:

```
FRACTURE → haematoma → INFLAMMATION clears debris
        → SOFT CALLUS of fibrocartilage bridges the gap
        → vascular invasion → HARD CALLUS of woven bone (clinical union, weeks)
        → REMODELLING: woven → lamellar bone along stress lines, contour restored
```

Immobilisation helps union but strips bone elsewhere; non-union follows displacement, infection or poor blood supply.

**Marrow disease and bone metastases show the skeleton's two vulnerabilities.** Since blood cells are made in bone, **leukaemia** — malignant overproduction of immature white cells — fills the marrow cavity, causing bone pain, cytopenias from crowding-out, and in children tenderness over the long bones. The same blood supply makes bone favourite soil for **metastases**: prostate cancer typically makes dense **osteoblastic** lesions, breast cancer destructive **osteolytic** ones — either way remodelling is hijacked, giving pain, hypercalcaemia (PTH-related peptide) and pathological fractures. Night pain that does not rest away should raise suspicion; infection in bone — **osteomyelitis** — is met by [09 — Immune system](09-immune-system.md).

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Osteoblasts break bone and osteoclasts build it" | Names reversed in memory: **blast = build** (secretes osteoid), **clast = break** (acid and protease dissolve matrix). |
| "Bone is dead, dried-out material" | Bone is **vascular, cellular and remodelled continuously** — osteocytes live in lacunae for decades, fed through canaliculi. |
| "Canaliculi are tiny blood vessels" | Only the **central and perforating canals** carry vessels; canaliculi **hold osteocyte processes** — a living relay, not plumbing. |
| "A fracture is set, then it heals" | A fixed sequence: **haematoma → inflammation → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling** over months; stability and blood supply decide whether it completes. |
| "Synovial fluid exists to lubricate" | Lubrication (hyaluronan, lubricin) is half of it: cartilage is **avascular**, so the fluid is also its **nutrition and waste route**. |
| "Joints are classified by shape" | First by **binding material** — fibrous, cartilaginous (synchondrosis, symphysis), synovial — and only **within synovial joints** by shape. |
| "Axial versus appendicular is an anatomical accident" | Functional split: **axial (80) protects** brain, cord, heart and lungs; **appendicular (126) moves** limbs and passes their load to the axis. |
| "The kidney controls calcium; bone just stores it" | Control is **PTH–calcitonin–vitamin D** on bone, kidney and gut together — bone is the **reservoir that makes fine control possible**. |
| "Osteoporosis and osteomalacia are the same" | **Osteoporosis = too little bone**; **osteomalacia = osteoid that will not mineralise** (vitamin D deficiency); **rickets** is that defect at a child's growth plate. |
| "Exercise is optional for bone" | Bone follows **Wolff's law**: osteocytes sense load, so **disuse activates resorption** — a cast or bed rest measurably thins bone. |

## Key facts

- The skeleton does **five jobs at once** — support, leverage, protection, mineral reservoir, haematopoiesis (plus fat storage) — from one tissue.
- **Bone is a vascular, living connective tissue that remodels against load**; matrix, not cell, does the mechanical work.
- **Compact bone** is osteons; **spongy bone** is trabeculae along stress lines — material only where load exists, so osteoporosis erodes bone from within.
- An **osteon** = central canal (vessel and nerve) + concentric **lamellae** + **lacunae** (osteocytes) + **canaliculi**, needed because **mineral blocks vessels**: cells survive by relay via gap junctions.
- **Osteoblasts build** (osteoid → hydroxyapatite), **osteocytes sense** (load, sclerostin), **osteoclasts destroy** (sealing zone, ruffled-border H⁺ pump) — one coupled remodelling unit.
- **Intramembranous ossification** builds the skull vault directly; **endochondral ossification** replaces a **cartilage model** — the way to vascularise a large bone while it grows.
- Length is made at the **growth plate** — cartilage columns proliferate, swell, calcify, are invaded and replaced by bone; appositional growth adds width.
- **Wolff's law**: load drags fluid through canaliculi → osteocytes signal → bone laid on stressed lines, removed from unstressed ones, so disuse causes osteopenia.
- **Axial (80 bones)** protects; **appendicular (126 bones)** moves; shapes follow role — long = lever, short = compression, flat = shield, irregular = complex fit, **sesamoid = leverage** (patella).
- **Joints trade stability for mobility**: fibrous immovable, cartilaginous slight movement, **synovial most mobile and least stable** — stability comes from shape, ligaments and muscle; fluid **feeds the avascular cartilage**.
- Bone mass is set by **PTH (resorbs bone, raises blood Ca²⁺), calcitonin (inhibits resorption) and vitamin D (gut absorption)**, plus load and oestrogen, in negative feedback ([04 — Endocrine system](04-endocrine-system.md)).

## Practice questions

**1. Osteocytes deep in mineralised matrix survive because**

A. Capillaries grow into each lacuna
B. Plasma percolates through the porous matrix
C. Nutrients and signals pass along canaliculi holding osteocyte processes joined by gap junctions
D. They are metabolically inactive

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Matrix blocks vessels, so supply is relayed cell to cell through the canaliculi — exactly why they exist. Vessels run only in central canals (A); osteocytes are long-lived (D).

**2. Which pairing of bone cell with mechanism is correct?**

A. Osteoblast — secretes cathepsin K to dissolve mineral
B. Osteoclast — lays down osteoid that later mineralises
C. Osteocyte — a multinucleated haematopoietic cell
D. Osteoclast — fuses from monocyte precursors, sealing a pit with a ruffled border that acidifies it

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Osteoclasts are macrophage-lineage, multinucleated cells that seal a pit and dissolve bone with acid plus protease. Osteoblasts build (A, B); osteocytes are single-nucleated (C).

---

**3. Most of the skeleton forms endochondrally rather than directly in a membrane chiefly because**

A. Cartilage is harder than bone
B. A large bone needs an avascular, fast-growing template that vessels can then invade
C. Membranous bone cannot remodel later
D. Osteoblasts need prior cartilage contact

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Mineralised matrix excludes capillaries, so a large bone must be built where blood can arrive — cartilage is the template then replaced. The skull vault shows direct formation works for thin plates (A, C).

---

**4. A bed-ridden patient loses hip bone mass despite adequate calcium intake. This is best explained by**

A. Dietary calcium passes through the gut unabsorbed
B. Vitamin D destroys hydroxyapatite
C. Osteoblasts need dietary calcium to survive
D. Wolff's law — absent load makes osteocyte signalling favour resorption

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Load is a maintenance signal; without it sclerostin rises and osteoclasts dominate, giving disuse osteopenia within weeks. Intake is adequate (A); vitamin D drives mineralisation (B).

**5. Which statement about the two divisions of the skeleton is correct?**

A. The axial skeleton contains the limbs and girdles
B. The appendicular skeleton contains the ribcage and sternum
C. Axial (80) is chiefly protective; appendicular (126) chiefly produces movement
D. Both are grouped by bone shape rather than position

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Axial = skull, spine and ribcage (protection); appendicular = limbs and girdles (levers). A and B reverse the divisions, and the split is positional (D).

**6. The patella is a sesamoid bone because it**

A. It lies inside a tendon, raising that tendon's leverage
B. It is cube-shaped and distributes compression
C. It shields the spinal cord
D. It forms in the skull vault

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Sesamoid bones sit within tendons crossing a joint, redirecting the pull and shielding the tendon. Carpals are short (B), vertebrae irregular (C), the patella forms in the quadriceps tendon (D).

**7. The joint allowing the thumb to oppose the fingers is a ________ joint because its surfaces are ________.**

A. hinge; mutually concave, one plane only
B. saddle; concave and convex at right angles, two axes
C. pivot; a ring on a peg, rotation only
D. condyloid; flat faces, sliding only

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The first carpometacarpal joint is saddle-shaped, giving the thumb two-axis movement and opposition. Hinge joints allow one plane (A), pivot joints rotation (C).

**8. Standing on tiptoe is a second-class lever because**

A. The fulcrum lies between effort and load
B. The effort lies between fulcrum and load
C. The load lies between the fulcrum (toe joints) and the effort (calf muscle)
D. The Achilles tendon is the fulcrum

**Answer: C**

Explanation: In a second-class lever the load sits in the middle, giving a mechanical advantage above one. A is a first class, B a third class, D mistakes insertion for pivot.

**9. In vitamin D deficiency, blood calcium falls chiefly because**

A. The kidney excretes rather than reabsorbs calcium
B. The thyroid releases excess calcitonin
C. Osteoblasts convert bone into cartilage
D. Intestinal calcium absorption falls, so PTH-driven resorption supplies the blood

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Calcitriol induces the gut's calcium transporters; without it absorption fails and PTH compensates by resorbing bone — the origin of rickets. The kidney activates vitamin D (A); calcitonin lowers calcium further (B).

**10. A post-menopausal woman sustains a vertebral crush fracture without trauma. The mechanism is most likely**

A. Vitamin D failure of cartilage mineralisation
B. Disorganised remodelling with mosaic woven bone, as in Paget's disease
C. Loss of oestrogen removing its restraint on osteoclasts, so resorption exceeds formation
D. Marrow overproduction expanding the vertebral body

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Oestrogen limits osteoclast survival and RANKL signalling; its withdrawal leaves resorption unopposed, so trabeculae thin and the loaded vertebral body collapses. Vitamin D failure gives soft bone (A), Paget's mosaic bone (B).
