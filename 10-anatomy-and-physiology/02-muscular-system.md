# The Muscular System

## Why it matters

Every purposeful movement — a blink, a stride, a swallow, a heartbeat — is **chemistry converted to force**. Muscle converts the energy of ATP into mechanical work using machinery you have already met: **actin and myosin sliding past each other** ([05 — Structure and movement](../02-cell-biology/05-structure-and-movement.md)). This chapter adds the **control layer** — nerve, calcium, the reflex arc — that turns biochemistry into graded, timed motion.

The **sliding-filament mechanism** is the most examined mechanism in human physiology because cell biology, biophysics and clinical medicine meet there: same ATP, same actin–myosin as before, with architecture, contraction types, tone, fatigue and muscle disease all following from that engine and its regulation.

```
NERVOUS SIGNAL (regulation) → does it contract, how much, how fast?
        ↓ Ca2+ released into the cytoplasm
TROPONIN–TROPOMYOSIN SWITCH  → myosin-binding sites on actin exposed
        ↓ cross-bridge cycling
SLIDING FILAMENTS            → sarcomere shortens → fibre pulls
        ↓ force transmitted through TENDON to BONE
MOVEMENT at a joint          → work + heat + posture maintained
```

The chapter follows **structure → function → mechanism → regulation → homeostasis**. Prerequisites: [01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md), [05 — Structure and movement](../02-cell-biology/05-structure-and-movement.md), [04 — ATP and metabolism](../03-cellular-processes/04-atp-and-metabolism.md), [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md).

## Skeletal muscle architecture

### Muscle → fascicle → fibre: nested bundles

Skeletal muscle is an **organ** of nested bundles, each layer sleeved in connective tissue so force can be summed, transmitted one way, and supplied with blood and nerves:

| Level | Composition | Connective tissue sleeve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Muscle** (organ) | Fascicles + vessels, nerves, fat | **Epimysium** (dense irregular) |
| **Fascicle** | Bundle of fibres | **Perimysium** (dense regular; carries vessels and nerves) |
| **Fibre** (one cell) | Sarcolemma, **peripheral nuclei**, myofibrils, mitochondria, glycogen | **Endomysium** (reticular fibres + basal lamina) |
| **Myofibril** | Chain of sarcomeres | — |
| **Sarcomere** | Z line to Z line | — |

Three facts follow: **one fibre can span much of the muscle** (sartorius, tens of centimetres), so one cell shortens the organ; **nuclei sit peripherally** because myofibrils fill the interior; and fibres are **multinucleated** from embryonic **myoblast fusion**. The sleeves follow the matrix logic of [01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md) and merge into a **tendon** inserting into bone ([01 — Skeletal system](01-skeletal-system.md)).

### The sarcomere: what moves and what does not

A **sarcomere** runs from one **Z line** to the next, repeated thousands of times; because they sit in register across every myofibril, the fibre looks **striated**. The banding's behaviour during contraction proves sliding filaments:

| Region | Filaments present | During contraction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Z line** | Thin filaments anchored (α-actinin) + titin | **Moves inward** — Z lines approach |
| **I band** | Thin only, either side of Z | **Narrows** |
| **A band** | Whole thick filament | **Constant** — the diagnostic observation |
| **H zone** | Thick only, centre | **Narrows** — thin filaments encroach |
| **M line** | Anchors thick filaments (myomesin) | Stays centred |
| **Sarcomere** (Z to Z) | — | **Shortens** |

**No filament changes length — only overlap changes**: myosin pulls thin filaments inward and Z lines approach; had filaments shortened, the A band would shrink too — hence its constancy is the key observation. **Titin** runs Z line to M line, centres the thick filament and springs the sarcomere back without ATP.

### Myofilaments and the regulatory switch

| Filament | Component | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin** | **F-actin** | Two helical strands; each monomer carries a **myosin-binding site** |
| **Thin** | **Tropomyosin** | Coiled coil in the actin groove — **blocks the sites at rest** |
| **Thin** | **Troponin** | **T** anchors to tropomyosin, **C binds 2 Ca²⁺**, **I** is inhibitory |
| **Thin** | **Nebulin** | Inextensible ruler setting thin-filament length |
| **Thick** | **Myosin** | Tail bundles into the filament; **hinge** is the lever arm; **head** bears the **actin-binding and ATPase sites** — myosin is an enzyme ([05 — Enzymes](../01-biochemistry/05-enzymes.md)) |

The resting block is **steric**: tropomyosin covers the binding sites, so although ATP and myosin are present no cross-bridges form — Ca²⁺ never touches myosin, it moves the shield. The apparatus sits in sarcoplasm rich in mitochondria and glycogen beside the Ca²⁺-storing **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)**, enclosed by the **sarcolemma**, whose fluid-mosaic composition governs every ion step below ([01 — Membrane structure and fluid mosaic](../03-cellular-processes/01-membrane-structure-and-fluid-mosaic.md)).

### Satellite cells and limited regeneration

Fibres are **permanent cells** — terminally differentiated and effectively post-mitotic ([01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md)). Repair rests on **satellite cells** between sarcolemma and basal lamina:

```
injury → myofibre necrosis (membrane breach, Ca2+ influx, proteases)
        ↓ satellite cell (Pax7+) on the basal lamina ACTIVATES
fuses with the damaged fibre → new nuclei, local repair
        ↓ repeated damage EXHAUSTS the satellite pool
FIBROSIS + fat replace muscle
```

Chronic damage therefore leaves scar — the end point of the dystrophies below.

## Excitation–contraction coupling

**Excitation–contraction (E–C) coupling** links the electrical event at the surface to the mechanical event inside, and **Ca²⁺ is the link**: nerve supplies the signal, fibre the machinery, ATP the energy.

### The neuromuscular junction

Each fibre is contacted by one motor neuron at the **motor end plate**, a specialised chemical synapse:

| Component | Feature | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Motor terminal** | Vesicles + mitochondria | One vesicle = one **quantum** (~5 000 ACh) |
| **Cleft** | ~50 nm; basal lamina holds **acetylcholinesterase** | Signal terminated enzymatically |
| **End-plate folds** | Receptors concentrated at crests | Multiplies area → large **safety factor** |
| **Nicotinic receptor** | Ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channel; **2 ACh** to open | Graded **end-plate potential (EPP)** → threshold |

### The full arrow chain

```
motor neuron ACTION POTENTIAL reaches the axon terminal
        ↓ Ca2+ enters → vesicles fuse → ACh released into the cleft
ACh binds NICOTINIC receptors on the motor end plate
        ↓ end-plate potential (graded, local) → threshold
SARCOLEMMA DEPOLARISATION → muscle action potential (all-or-none)
        ↓ travels along the surface and DOWN THE T-TUBULES
DHPR VOLTAGE SENSOR in the T-tubule changes conformation
        ↓ mechanically opens RYR CHANNELS ON THE SR
Ca2+ FLOODS THE CYTOSOL from the sarcoplasmic reticulum
        ↓ Ca2+ BINDS TROPONIN C
tropomyosin SHIFTS OFF THE MYOSIN-BINDING SITES
        ↓ cross-bridge cycling begins
CONTRACTION
```

The **triad** — one T-tubule flanked by two SR terminal cisternae — converts electrical to chemical: in **skeletal** muscle the DHPR change **mechanically opens RyR1**, while in **cardiac** muscle Ca²⁺ entering through DHPR **opens RyR2** (**calcium-induced calcium release**), which is why removing extracellular Ca²⁺ stops the heart but not skeletal release.

### Relaxation is active, not passive

```
ACETYLCHOLINESTERASE hydrolyses ACh in the cleft
        ↓ EPP decays; Na+/K+ ATPase restores the gradients; RyR closes
SERCA PUMPS Ca2+ BACK INTO THE SR USING ATP
        ↓ cytosolic [Ca2+] falls → Ca2+ leaves troponin C
tropomyosin RE-COVERS the binding sites
        ↓ no new cross-bridges → elastic elements restore length → RELAXATION
```

**Relaxation costs ATP continuously**, and anything raising cytosolic Ca²⁺ keeps muscle switched on.

## The cross-bridge cycle

| Step | Event | Head carries | Energy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Cock** | ATP hydrolysed; head swings upright | **ADP + Pi** | Chemical energy stored as strain |
| 2. **Attach** | Exposed actin site; weak → strong binding | ADP + Pi | No ATP yet |
| 3. **Power stroke** | Pi release → head rotates, filament slides; ADP released | — | Chemical → mechanical: this is the force |
| 4. **Detachment** | **New ATP binds** → affinity collapses → head lets go | **ATP** | **ATP required HERE** |
| 5. **Re-cock** | ATP hydrolysed → head returns to the state of step 1 | ADP + Pi | **ATP required HERE** |

```
COCKED head (ADP + Pi) → binds ACTIN
        ↓ POWER STROKE: Pi released, filament slides, ADP released
rigor state: head locked on actin, empty of nucleotide
        ↓ NEW ATP BINDS → head DETACHES
ATP HYDROLYSED → head RE-COCKED → ready for the next site
        ↓ repeat while Ca2+ and ATP persist
```

- **ATP is needed for both detachment and re-cocking**, so muscle spends ATP shortening, holding or switching off; myosin's own ATPase converts the energy, the nerve only gates it.
- **No ATP means no detachment**: after death the SR leaks Ca²⁺ (removing the troponin block) while ATP synthesis has stopped, so heads bind and lock — **rigor mortis**.
- **Only a few heads attach at once**, giving a smooth sustained tug; **fast myosin hydrolyses ATP faster**, which is why fibre types differ in speed and fatigue resistance.

## Graded force: contraction types, motor units, summation

### Isotonic, isometric — and where the force is greatest

| Type | Muscle length | Tension | External work | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Concentric (isotonic)** | **Shortens** | Matches load | Positive (force × distance) | Lifting a dumbbell |
| **Eccentric (isotonic)** | **Lengthens while active** | **Greatest of all** | Negative — absorbs energy | Lowering it; downhill walking |
| **Isometric** | Unchanged | Rises to a ceiling | **Zero** (distance = 0), yet ATP is consumed | Pushing a wall; holding a plank |

**Eccentric contractions produce the most force**: attached cross-bridges are held under load while further heads attach, and titin plus other passive elements resist being stretched. The cost is **microdamage** — over-stretched sarcomeres fail, inflammation follows ([01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md)) and **delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS)** peaks 24–72 hours later; repetition gives the **repeated-bout effect**. And **contraction does not require shortening — it requires cycling**: isometric force burns ATP with no movement.

### Motor units and recruitment

A **motor unit** is **one alpha motor neuron plus every fibre it innervates** — the smallest command the nervous system can issue and the unit of recruitment. Small units (extraocular, thumb) give fine control; large units (gluteus maximus) give power but coarse gradation. Force rises without breaking the all-or-none law, which applies **per fibre and per motor unit**, never to the whole muscle: **recruitment** adds units — smallest, slowest, most fatigue-resistant first (**Henneman's size principle**) — and **rate coding** fires them faster, so Ca²⁺ cannot be cleared between action potentials. Both push toward tetanus, taken step by step below.

### Wave summation and tetanus

```
ONE stimulus → a TWITCH: contraction + relaxation run to completion
        ↓ second stimulus BEFORE relaxation finishes
residual Ca2+ + new Ca2+ → more cross-bridges → LARGER force
        ↓ WAVE SUMMATION → frequency rises, twitches overlap
UNFUSED (INCOMPLETE) TETANUS — force ripples, never reaches zero
        ↓ SERCA cannot clear Ca2+ between stimuli
FUSED (COMPLETE) TETANUS — smooth MAXIMAL sustained force
```

Everyday sustained contraction (standing, gripping) is low-grade **physiological tetanus** — unlike **tetanus disease**, where *Clostridium tetani* toxin removes spinal inhibition and gives spastic paralysis ([03 — Nervous system](03-nervous-system.md)).

### Tone, fatigue, and the limits of regulation

**Tone** is continuous, low-frequency, **asynchronous** firing of motor units, so overlapping partial twitches give smooth low-level contraction. It maintains posture and stabilises joints and — never fully relaxing — **costs ATP around the clock**; gamma motor neurons adjust it via the spindle. Loss gives flaccid paralysis (lower motor neuron lesion, myasthenia gravis), excess spasticity (upper motor neuron lesion).

**Fatigue** is the progressive fall in force during sustained activity, and it is **not** simple ATP depletion:

| Factor | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Glycogen depletion** | Substrate runs out for glycolysis and oxidative metabolism |
| **Extracellular K⁺ accumulation** | T-tubules depolarised → membrane inexcitable, action potentials fail |
| **Inorganic phosphate (Pi)** | Lowers SR Ca²⁺ release and myofibrillar Ca²⁺ sensitivity |
| **Impaired Ca²⁺ handling** | RyR and SERCA release less Ca²⁺ per action potential |
| **Central fatigue** | The CNS withdraws drive before the muscle fails |

**ATP is not depleted directly**: phosphocreatine buffers it and glycolysis ([05 — Glycolysis](../03-cellular-processes/05-glycolysis.md)) regenerates it almost to exhaustion, so failing force is **protective** against the contracture true ATP depletion would cause. Systemically, muscle is a homeostatic organ — the largest **heat generator** (shivering), a major **glucose sink** (insulin-independent GLUT4 recruitment) and the effector of the stretch reflex ([03 — Homeostasis control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md)).

## Muscle tissue compared: skeletal, cardiac, smooth

All three use the same actin–myosin engine; they differ in how it is switched on, how fast it runs, and how long it can run.

| Feature | **Skeletal** | **Cardiac** | **Smooth** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cell | Long fibres, **multinucleate, nuclei peripheral** | **Branched**, 1–2 central nuclei | **Spindle-shaped**, one nucleus |
| **Striations** | **Yes** (sarcomeres in register) | **Yes** | **No** — dense bodies, no sarcomeres |
| **Control** | **Voluntary** — somatic motor neuron, ACh at NMJ | **Involuntary** — intrinsic pacemaker, autonomic modulation | **Involuntary** — nerves, hormones, stretch |
| **Speed** | **Fastest** | Intermediate | **Slowest** |
| **Fatigue resistance** | Relatively **fatigable** | Highly **fatigue-resistant** (aerobic) | Highly **fatigue-resistant** |
| **Ca²⁺ source** | SR via T-tubule action potential | SR **plus** extracellular Ca²⁺ (L-type) | Mostly **extracellular** + SR (IP₃-gated) |
| **Switch** | **Troponin–tropomyosin** | **Troponin–tropomyosin** | **Calmodulin → MLCK** (no troponin) |
| **Cell coupling** | None — fibres innervated individually | **Intercalated discs**: gap junctions + desmosomes | Gap junctions in many sheets |
| **Tetanus** | Can tetanise | **Cannot** — long refractory period | Sustains partial contraction indefinitely |

### Skeletal: speed and volition

Built for **chosen, large, fast forces**: multinucleation feeds a long cell, peripheral nuclei leave myofibrils unobstructed, and each fibre is independently addressable by one motor neuron — what makes graded, selective movement possible.

### Cardiac: the functional syncytium

**Intercalated discs** weld cardiac myocytes with **fascia adherens** (anchor actin), **desmosomes** (intermediate filaments stop cells tearing apart) and **gap junctions** (low-resistance ionic pathways → electrical continuity):

```
pacemaker potential in the SA node → action potential
        ↓ cell-to-cell through GAP JUNCTIONS
atria contract AS ONE UNIT → AV node delay → ventricles AS ONE UNIT
        ↓ REFRACTORY PERIOD (~250 ms) outlasts the contraction
heart CANNOT be summed or tetanised → always relaxes and refills
```

Without the disc, billions of myocytes would contract on their own schedules and the ventricle would generate no net pressure — hence **cardiac muscle is a functional syncytium**: many cells, one electrical event, one coordinated squeeze. A gap-junction defect or fibrotic scar disrupts rhythm rather than weakening the wall ([05 — Cardiovascular system](05-cardiovascular-system.md)).

### Smooth: cheap, slow, sustained

Thin and thick filaments anchor to **dense bodies** scattered through the cytoplasm, so the lattice shortens along any axis and far further than a striated sarcomere — with no troponin:

```
autonomic nerve, hormone, or local stretch
        ↓ receptor → G-protein → phospholipase C → IP3
IP3 opens SR Ca2+ channels; Ca2+ also enters from OUTSIDE the cell
        ↓ cytosolic Ca2+ rises → binds CALMODULIN → activates MLCK
MLCK PHOSPHORYLATES the myosin light chain → CONTRACTION
        ↓ myosin phosphatase removes the phosphate → relaxation
LATCH STATE: dephosphorylated heads REMAIN ATTACHED
        → force held for hours with almost no ATP
```

**Latch bridges** are why vessels hold tone, the bladder holds urine and the gut moves content without tiring. Smooth muscle is **multi-unit** (iris, airways — individually innervated) or **single-unit** (gut, uterus — gap junctions).

## Levers and biomechanics

### Lever classes in the body

Movement happens where muscle force acts on bone **across a joint** — joint is the fulcrum, insertion is the effort, whatever moves is the load:

| Class | Arrangement | Body example | Mechanical effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **First** | Effort — **fulcrum** — load | **Atlanto-occipital joint**: nodding the head | Balanced; advantage ≈ 1 |
| **Second** | Fulcrum — **load** — effort | **Standing on tiptoe**: ankle fulcrum, body weight at the ball of the foot, gastrocnemius–soleus pulling the calcaneus | **Force multiplier** (MA > 1) |
| **Third** | Fulcrum — **effort** — load | **Biceps at the elbow** lifting an object in the hand | **Speed and range** (MA < 1) — most common in the body |

**Mechanical advantage** = effort arm ÷ load arm. Because most muscles insert **just beyond** their joint, the biceps may shorten 2–3 cm while the hand travels 30 cm: the body systematically **trades force for speed and range**. Where force matters, **physiological cross-sectional area** (myofibrils in parallel) sets maximum tension — hence pennate deltoid packs more fibres into a given volume while sartorius buys range.

### Tendons: transmitting force without losing it

| Property | **Muscle belly** | **Tendon** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tissue | Contractile cells, mitochondria, capillaries | **Dense regular tissue**, parallel type I collagen |
| Vascularity | Rich | Poor → **slow healing** |
| Behaviour | Shortens, complies, makes heat | **Stiff** — stretches less than 5% before failure |
| Energy | Spends ATP | **Stores and returns elastic energy** (Achilles recoil) |

Force must reach the bone **before** the muscle can shorten appreciably, so the link is stiff while the belly is fleshy — perfused, mitochondria-rich and able to change length. The **myotendinous junction** is deeply folded to multiply force-transfer area, and force spreads laterally through endomysium so the muscle acts as one unit.

### Antagonistic pairs and the reflex arc

Muscles act in groups: the **agonist** produces the action (biceps flexes the elbow), the **antagonist** opposes it (triceps), a **synergist** assists (brachioradialis), a **fixator** holds the origin still (rotator cuff steadying the humeral head). Antagonist relaxation is organised in the spinal cord by **reciprocal inhibition**:

```
sudden stretch (or tendon tap) on the BICEPS
        ↓ muscle spindle → Ia afferent fires
spinal cord, one synapse away:
   excitatory branch → ALPHA MOTOR NEURON to biceps → CONTRACTION
   inhibitory interneuron → motor neuron to triceps → RELAXATION
        ↓ posture corrected BEFORE the brain is consulted
```

Running continuously, this loop produces **tone**; it is a negative-feedback arm with muscle as effector and spindle as receptor ([03 — Homeostasis control loop](../09-human-biology/03-homeostasis-control-loop.md)).

## Medical relevance

**Myasthenia gravis is a disease of the safety factor.** Autoantibodies target the **nicotinic acetylcholine receptor** at the motor end plate, destroying junctional folds, so each release of ACh opens fewer channels and the end-plate potential falls short of threshold. The result is **fatigable weakness** — ptosis and diplopia worsening through the day, bulbar and limb weakness worsening with use — an immune attack on a specialised structure ([01 — Four tissue types](../09-human-biology/01-four-tissue-types.md)). Treatment raises cleft ACh (pyridostigmine) or removes the thymus.

**Muscular dystrophies are mechanical failures of membrane anchorage.** In **Duchenne dystrophy** (X-linked, dystrophin gene on Xp21) **dystrophin** is absent; it normally links internal actin to the dystroglycan complex and then to extracellular matrix. Without that shock absorber each contraction shears the membrane, Ca²⁺ floods in and the fibre necrotises — satellite-cell repair limps along until exhausted and muscle is replaced by fat and collagen: **pseudohypertrophy of the calves**, raised creatine kinase, the **Gowers sign**, loss of ambulation. **Becker dystrophy** is the milder form with partial dystrophin: protein missing → membrane fragile → repeated necrosis → regenerative exhaustion → fibrosis.

**Rhabdomyolysis is muscle breakdown becoming a kidney problem.** When sarcolemmas fail en masse — crush injury, seizures, extreme exertion, statins — **myoglobin** floods the plasma, is filtered into the nephron, precipitates in acidic tubules and poisons tubular cells, causing **acute kidney injury**; hyperkalaemia from the same necrosis threatens the heart first. Treatment follows mechanism: fluids to dilute and alkalinise the urine, plus removal of the cause ([08 — Urinary system](08-urinary-system.md)).

**Muscle cramps are sustained involuntary motor-unit activity.** A cramped muscle locks into repeated firing with painful distortion of terminal nerve endings; dehydration, electrolyte disturbance and fatigue lower the threshold. Stretching relieves it because stretch activates the **Golgi tendon organ**, whose Ib afferents inhibit the alpha motor neuron.

**Cardiac glycosides exploit the pumps of transport.** **Digoxin** inhibits the **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase** of cardiac myocytes ([03 — Active transport](../03-cellular-processes/03-active-transport.md)); intracellular Na⁺ rises, the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger therefore extrudes less Ca²⁺, intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulates and each contraction is stronger (**positive inotropy**) in heart failure. The therapeutic index is narrow: toxicity presents as nausea and arrhythmias, classically with yellow-green visual disturbance.

**Exercise physiology is fuel selection under load.** ATP arrives in overlapping windows: **phosphocreatine** for seconds, **anaerobic glycolysis** (glucose → lactate, [05 — Glycolysis](../03-cellular-processes/05-glycolysis.md), [08 — Fermentation](../03-cellular-processes/08-fermentation.md)) for tens of seconds to minutes, then **oxidative metabolism** for sustained work ([04 — ATP and metabolism](../03-cellular-processes/04-atp-and-metabolism.md)). **Slow oxidative (Type I)** fibres are mitochondria- and myoglobin-rich and fatigue-resistant; **fast glycolytic (Type IIx)** fibres make large forces quickly but tire. Endurance training raises capillary and mitochondrial density, resistance training raises cross-sectional area — the variable that sets maximum force.

**Rigor mortis is the cross-bridge cycle with the ATP removed.** After death, ATP synthesis stops while the sarcoplasmic reticulum leaks Ca²⁺, so troponin C saturates and heads form cross-bridges — but with no ATP step 4 cannot occur and the heads **cannot detach**, locking the muscle until autolysis resolves it. It is the clearest demonstration that **ATP is required for relaxation, not merely for contraction**.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "The nervous system powers contraction" | The nerve supplies only the **signal and Ca²⁺ release**; energy is **ATP hydrolysed by myosin** and used by SERCA — hence rigor mortis. |
| "The A band shortens during contraction" | The **A band is constant** (length of the thick filament); the **I band and H zone narrow** as overlap increases — filaments slide, never shorten. |
| "Contraction means the muscle shortens" | **Isometric** contractions make tension with no movement yet burn ATP; **eccentric** ones lengthen the muscle while active. |
| "Eccentric contractions are the weakest" | They produce the **greatest force** of any contraction — and cause the microdamage behind **DOMS**. |
| "All-or-none means the whole muscle goes maximally or not at all" | Applies **per fibre and motor unit**; the whole muscle is graded by **recruitment** and **rate coding**. |
| "Tetanus is an infection" | Physiological **tetanus** is fused contraction at high frequency; **tetanus disease** is toxin removing spinal inhibition → spastic paralysis. |
| "Relaxation is a passive rebound" | Needs **SERCA pumping Ca²⁺ into the SR with ATP** plus acetylcholinesterase clearing ACh: no ATP, no relaxation. |
| "Cardiac muscle is skeletal muscle with junctions" | It **self-excites**, has a **long refractory period preventing tetanus**, runs aerobically and needs extracellular Ca²⁺ (CICR). |
| "Smooth muscle ignores calcium" | Smooth muscle depends on Ca²⁺ but takes most of it **from outside the cell**, switching through **calmodulin → MLCK**, not troponin. |
| "Lactic acid simply exhausts the muscle's ATP" | ATP is regenerated almost to exhaustion; fatigue is **multifactorial** — glycogen, extracellular K⁺, Pi, Ca²⁺ handling, central drive. |

## Key facts

- **Nested bundles**: muscle → fascicle → fibre in **epimysium, perimysium, endomysium**, then **myofibril → sarcomere**; fibres are multinucleated with **peripheral nuclei** from myoblast fusion.
- A sarcomere runs **Z line to Z line**; contraction gives **constant A band, narrowing I band and H zone, approaching Z lines** — **no filament changes length, only overlap changes**.
- Thin filament = **actin + tropomyosin + troponin (T/C/I) + nebulin**; thick = **myosin** (tail, hinge, head with actin-binding and ATPase sites); titin is the Z-to-M spring.
- **E–C coupling**: motor neuron AP → ACh → nicotinic receptors → EPP → sarcolemma AP → **T-tubules** → **DHPR** → **RyR opens** → **Ca²⁺ binds troponin C** → tropomyosin shifts → cross-bridge cycling.
- **Relaxation is active**: acetylcholinesterase clears ACh and **SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ into the SR using ATP**, so tropomyosin re-covers the binding sites.
- Cross-bridge cycle: **cocked (ADP+Pi) → attach → power stroke → new ATP binds → detach → hydrolyse → re-cock**; ATP is needed to **detach and re-cock** — hence **rigor mortis**.
- Force is graded by **recruitment** (smallest motor units first — **Henneman's size principle**) and **rate coding**; the **motor unit — one neuron plus its fibres — is the unit of command**.
- **Wave summation → unfused tetanus → fused tetanus** as frequency rises; **cardiac muscle cannot tetanise** because its refractory period outlasts its contraction.
- **Eccentric contractions give the most force** and cause **DOMS**; **isometric contraction gives tension without movement** yet burns ATP; **tone** is asynchronous low-frequency firing; **fatigue** reflects glycogen, extracellular K⁺, Pi and Ca²⁺ handling — **not direct ATP depletion**.
- **Skeletal**: voluntary, troponin-switched, SR-fed, fast and fatigable; **cardiac**: intrinsic, CICR-fed, disc-coupled, never fatigues or tetanises; **smooth**: involuntary, calmodulin–MLCK-switched, extracellular-Ca²⁺-fed, with latch bridges.
- **Second-class levers multiply force (tiptoe), third-class multiply speed and range (biceps)**; tendons are stiff so force is transmitted; antagonists relax by **reciprocal inhibition**.

## Practice questions

**1. Which observation during contraction directly proves that filaments slide rather than shorten?**

A. The H zone widens as thick filaments compress
B. The A band stays constant while the I band narrows
C. The Z lines move apart as the sarcomere extends
D. Thick filaments become visibly shorter

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The A band is the thick filament's length and never changes; only overlap does. The thin-only I band therefore narrows while the A band holds.

---

**2. In skeletal muscle, which event immediately follows activation of the DHPR voltage sensor?**

A. ACh is released from the motor terminal
B. SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasmic reticulum
C. RyR channels open and Ca²⁺ floods the cytosol
D. Acetylcholinesterase degrades acetylcholine in the cleft

**Answer: C**

Explanation: The DHPR conformational change mechanically opens RyR, releasing Ca²⁺ — the step linking excitation to contraction. ACh release precedes the action potential; SERCA and acetylcholinesterase act in relaxation.

---

**3. Why does rigor mortis occur after death?**

A. The sarcoplasmic reticulum stops releasing Ca²⁺, so tropomyosin never moves
B. Myosin heads cannot detach because no ATP is available to bind them
C. Acetylcholine persists in the cleft and continuously depolarises the fibre
D. Sarcomeres are dissolved by autolytic enzymes

**Answer: B**

Explanation: ATP synthesis stops while the SR leaks Ca²⁺, so heads attach — but the ATP-binding step needed for detachment cannot occur, and they lock. Rigor is inability to detach, not to attach.

---

**4. Which step of muscle relaxation requires direct ATP hydrolysis?**

A. Propagation of the action potential along the sarcolemma
B. Binding of Ca²⁺ to troponin C
C. Pumping of Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA
D. Hydrolysis of acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase

**Answer: C**

Explanation: SERCA is an ATPase moving Ca²⁺ against its gradient into the SR, so relaxation is active. Troponin C binding starts contraction, and acetylcholinesterase needs no ATP.

**5. As a lifter increases the load, whole-muscle force rises. Which mechanism explains this?**

A. Each motor unit contracts more strongly as the load rises
B. Muscle action potentials grow larger with heavier loads
C. Additional motor units are recruited and their firing frequency rises
D. The antagonist muscle relaxes more completely at heavier loads

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Each fibre obeys all-or-none, so force rises by recruiting more units (Henneman's size principle) and firing them faster. Action-potential amplitude does not change.

**6. Stimulation at a frequency that prevents full repolarisation between stimuli produces**

A. Complete relaxation between each twitch
B. Wave summation with declining peak force
C. Fused tetanus — a smooth maximal sustained contraction
D. Immediate flaccid paralysis

**Answer: C**

Explanation: When stimuli arrive faster than SERCA clears Ca²⁺, twitches fuse into a smooth maximal contraction. Lower frequencies give unfused tetanus.

**7. Lowering a weight works the muscle harder than lifting it. Which statement is correct?**

A. Eccentric contractions produce the least force and cause no damage
B. Eccentric contractions produce the greatest force and are the main cause of DOMS
C. Isometric contractions produce external work because the muscle shortens
D. Concentric contractions exceed eccentric force at the same load

**Answer: B**

Explanation: During lengthening, cross-bridges are held under load while more heads attach and titin resists stretch, so tension exceeds that of shortening. The cost is microdamage — DOMS.

**8. Drooping eyelids and double vision that worsen through the day most likely reflect**

A. Autoantibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptors eroding the safety factor
B. Absence of dystrophin causing repeated sarcolemmal tearing
C. Failure of SERCA to sequester Ca²⁺ in the sarcoplasmic reticulum
D. Autoantibodies blocking troponin so tropomyosin cannot shift

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Myasthenia gravis antibodies destroy or internalise nicotinic receptors, so repeated ACh release drives the end-plate potential below threshold. Dystrophin absence gives dystrophy instead.

**9. Progressive weakness in Duchenne muscular dystrophy results ultimately because**

A. Myosin heads permanently lose the ability to bind ATP
B. Repeated membrane damage exhausts satellite cells, so muscle is replaced by fibrous tissue and fat
C. Cardiac-type intercalated discs form between skeletal fibres
D. Motor neurons fail to release acetylcholine

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Without dystrophin, contraction repeatedly tears the sarcolemma; satellite cells repair until exhausted and non-contractile tissue takes over. The defect is the membrane–cytoskeleton link.

**10. Digoxin increases cardiac contractility because inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase**

A. Directly opens RyR channels on the sarcoplasmic reticulum
B. Prevents acetylcholine breakdown at the cardiac synapse
C. Raises intracellular Na⁺, reducing Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange so intracellular Ca²⁺ rises
D. Blocks troponin C so tropomyosin shifts further off actin

**Answer: C**

Explanation: With the sodium gradient reduced, the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger extrudes less Ca²⁺, so cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises and each contraction is stronger. Digoxin's target is the sodium–potassium pump.
