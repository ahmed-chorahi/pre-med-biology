# Beneficial Microorganisms

## Why it matters

Microbiology is usually taught as a catalogue of threats, which gives a distorted picture: **the vast majority of microorganisms are not pathogenic, and many are indispensable.** Your own gut contains roughly as many bacterial cells as your body contains human cells, and without them you would digest less well, synthesise no vitamin K, resist colonisation by far worse organisms, and train an immune system that had never learned to distinguish friend from foe. Agriculture runs on bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use. Food preservation, bread, cheese, wine, and yoghurt are all microbial processes conducted deliberately. Modern biotechnology is built almost entirely on microorganisms as producers — of antibiotics, enzymes, vaccines, and recombinant proteins.

The organising idea of this chapter is **context dependence**, and it is the same idea that opened [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](06-host-pathogen-interaction.md). An organism's effect on you depends on *where it is, how much of it there is, what else is present, and what the host is doing.* *Escherichia coli* K-12 is a laboratory workhorse and a harmless gut resident; *E. coli* O157:H7 in the same gut produces Shiga toxin and causes haemolytic uraemic syndrome. *Clostridioides difficile* is an unremarkable member of a healthy flora until a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics removes its competitors — and then it is the cause of pseudomembranous colitis. Nothing about the organism changed; the ecosystem did.

Third, this chapter closes the loop on the ecology you met in [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](05-microbial-reproduction-and-transmission.md). Fermentation, sewage treatment, nitrogen fixation, and industrial biotechnology are all **managed microbial ecosystems** — populations whose growth conditions are chosen so that their metabolism produces a result we want. Learn them as ecology applied on purpose, and the individual species names stop being arbitrary.

## Normal flora: the other half of host–pathogen interaction

The sites, resident species, and the concept of **colonisation resistance** are set out in [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](06-host-pathogen-interaction.md); what belongs here is what the flora *does for you* rather than how it is defended against.

| Contribution | Mechanism | Consequence when lost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Colonisation resistance** | Competing for nutrients and adhesion sites, producing bacteriocins, maintaining low pH (vagina) or normal redox (gut) | Antibiotic-suppressed gut → ***C. difficile*** expansion; vaginal flora loss → bacterial vaginosis or candidiasis |
| **Vitamin synthesis** | Bacterial synthesis of biotin, folate, B₁₂; **menaquinone (vitamin K)** from gut *Bacteroides* and *E. coli* | Clinically relevant in newborns (haemorrhagic disease of the newborn — vitamin K injection at birth) and after prolonged antibiotic courses |
| **Immune system training** | Continuous low-level antigenic stimulation shapes IgA production, regulatory T-cell balance, and lymphoid tissue development | Germ-free animals have underdeveloped gut-associated lymphoid tissue and exaggerated inflammatory responses |
| **Metabolic cooperation** | Fermentation of dietary fibre to **short-chain fatty acids** (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that colonocytes use as energy; bile acid transformation | Reduced butyrate → impaired colonic epithelial health; altered bile acid pools affect lipid digestion |
| **Barrier against pathogens** | Physical occupation of niches plus hostile chemistry | Any disruption opens niches — the reason hospital infections follow antibiotic use |

**Probiotics** are defined as live microorganisms that confer a health benefit when administered in adequate amounts — *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, *Saccharomyces boulardii* (a yeast, which is why it survives antibacterial therapy). The evidence base is real but narrow: benefit is best demonstrated for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and for prevention of *C. difficile* recurrence, with strain-specific effects elsewhere. A probiotic is **not** an antibiotic, and it does not treat an established invasive infection — it is an attempt to re-establish an ecosystem.

## Fermentation: controlled spoilage

Fermentation is anaerobic metabolism of an organic substrate — the chemistry is in [08 — Fermentation](../03-cellular-processes/08-fermentation.md). Microbiologically, its value is that the products are hostile to everything else: **lactic acid drops pH below the range most spoilage organisms tolerate; ethanol disrupts membranes; low pH and low redox exclude aerobes.** Preservation by fermentation is therefore preservation by microbiology — the desired organisms acidify the environment faster than competitors can grow.

| Food | Organisms | Metabolic product | What stops the process |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Yoghurt** | *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* + *Streptococcus thermophilus* (defined starter, mutual stimulation) | Lactic acid → casein coagulation, flavour | Temperature on pasteurisation |
| **Cheese** | Rennet (chymosin) first; then *Lactococcus*, *Propionibacterium* (Emmental eyes = CO₂), *Penicillium roqueforti* / *camemberti* | Acid, CO₂, proteolysis and lipolysis for flavour | Rind, salt, ageing conditions |
| **Sauerkraut, kimchi** | *Leuconostoc*, *Lactobacillus* (successive waves on the vegetable's own surface) | Lactic acid in brine | Salt concentration, oxygen exclusion |
| **Sourdough bread** | Wild *Lactobacillus* + *Saccharomyces/Candida* community | CO₂ (rise) + lactic/acetic acid (flavour) | Baking at ~200 °C |
| **Beer, wine, spirits** | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* | **Ethanol + CO₂** from sugars; wine depends on grape-surface yeasts | Alcohol tolerance of the yeast itself (≈15% for wine strains) |
| **Vinegar** | *Acetobacter* (aerobic) | Ethanol → acetic acid | Ethanol exhaustion; oxygen supply |
| **Soy sauce, miso** | *Aspergillus oryzae* (koji) then lactic fermentation and *Zygosaccharomyces* | Enzymes hydrolyse soy/wheat protein and starch; salt stops spoilage | Salt, time |
| **Tempeh** | *Rhizopus oligosporus* | Fungal mycelium binds soybeans into a cake; protein pre-digested | Cooking |
| **Olives, pickles** | *Lactobacillus plantarum* in brine | Lactic acid | Brine acidity |

**The line between fermentation and spoilage is intent plus control.** The same lactic acid production that makes yoghurt makes milk turn; the difference is a defined starter inoculated in dominance, controlled temperature, and a target the process stops at. Without a dominant starter, the wrong organism wins — botulism in inadequately acidified canned food is the classic failure, because *Clostridium botulinum* is a fermenting anaerobe whose toxin is the hazard.

## Industrial and pharmaceutical microorganisms

### Antibiotics: microorganisms poisoning their competitors

Most antibiotics are **natural products of microbial competition** — the same ecological warfare visible on any plate where *Penicillium* clears a zone of staphylococci.

| Producer | Product | Class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Penicillium chrysogenum* | Penicillin | β-lactam |
| *Streptomyces griseus* | Streptomycin | Aminoglycoside |
| *Streptomyces aureofaciens* | Tetracycline | Tetracycline |
| *Streptomyces erythraeus* | Erythromycin | Macrolide |
| *Cephalosporium acremonium* | Cephalosporins | β-lactam |
| *Bacillus brevis* | Polymyxin | Peptide (membrane-active) |

Semisynthetic derivatives (modified side chains) and fully synthetic drugs (quinolones, trimethoprim) followed, but the **discovery pipeline still runs through soil microorganisms** — and soil is the competitive habitat where producing a chemical weapon is worth its metabolic cost. Resistance genes in that same soil are ancient, which is where the genes selected for in hospitals originally came from (see [06 — Evolution](../06-evolution/02-natural-selection.md)).

### Enzymes, and the organisms engineered to make them

Microbial enzymes are sold as industrial catalysts because a microbe can be grown by the tonne and its genome edited to overproduce a protein: **amylases** (starch hydrolysis, textiles, dishwashing detergents), **proteases** (meat tenderising, detergents), **lipases** (flavour in cheese, degreasing), **pectinases** (fruit juice clarification), **lactase** (lactose-free milk), **glucose isomerase** (high-fructose corn syrup). Detergent enzymes are the highest-volume application by far, and their discovery from thermophilic and alkaliphilic bacteria — enzymes that work at 60 °C and pH 10 — is a direct readout of where in nature such functions evolved.

### Recombinant hosts: *E. coli* and yeast as factories

| Host | What it is used for | Why it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ***Escherichia coli* K-12** (avirulent laboratory strain) | Human **insulin**, growth hormone, interferon, interleukins, research proteins | Fast, cheap, genetics completely characterised; plasmid expression systems |
| ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae*** | **Hepatitis B vaccine**, insulin analogue production, some clotting factors | Eukaryote — folds and glycosylates proteins bacteria cannot; GRAS status; phage-resistant |
| ***Bacillus subtilis*** | Secreted industrial enzymes | Secretes proteins straight into the medium |
| **Bacteriophage M13, λ** | Cloning vectors, phage display | DNA manipulation tools |

The tool kit itself is microbial: **restriction enzymes** that cut DNA at defined sequences, **plasmids** as vectors, **DNA ligase**, and selectable markers — all derived from the bacterial defence systems described in [01 — Bacterial structure and physiology](01-bacterial-structure-and-physiology.md) (restriction–modification) and applied through the molecular biology of [05 — Molecular Biology](../05-molecular-biology/). **CRISPR–Cas9** is another bacterial immune system — the palindromic repeats recorded past phage infections, and Cas protein that shreds matching viral DNA — repurposed as genome editing.

### Bioremediation and waste treatment

- **Oil spills** — hydrocarbon-degrading *Pseudomonas* and *Alcanivorax* bloom around a spill; bioremediation works by fertilising the population (nitrogen, phosphorus) rather than adding exotic organisms, because the capacity is usually already present in the environment.
- **Activated sludge and trickling filters** — aerated tanks where a mixed community oxidises organic waste; the output is measured as **BOD (biological oxygen demand)** reduction. Solids are settled and part is returned as inoculum — a deliberately managed ecosystem with a resident population, exactly like a sourdough starter at municipal scale.
- **Anaerobic digestion** — *Methanosaeta*, *Methanosarcina* (archaeal methanogens — see [02 — Archaea](02-archaea.md)) complete the breakdown to **CH₄**, used for biogas; the same process explains swamp gas and ruminant methane.
- **Sewage treatment closes the microbial loop**: pathogen reduction is achieved partly by the process itself and partly by the ecological principle that a population crashes when its substrate is exhausted.

### Biocontrol in agriculture

***Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt)** produces an insecticidal crystal protein activated in the alkaline gut of specific insect larvae — sprayed as a biological pesticide, or expressed directly in crops (Bt cotton, Bt maize) so the plant synthesises its own insecticidal protein. **Bacteriophages** are used as specificity-targeted alternatives to antibiotics in livestock and food safety; **Trichoderma** fungi suppress root pathogens; **mycorrhizal and rhizobial inoculants** are sold as seed coatings (below).

## Microbes in agriculture

### Nitrogen fixation: the only reaction that feeds the world

Atmospheric N₂ is inert (triple bond) and unavailable to plants. **Biological nitrogen fixation** reduces it to ammonia using the enzyme **nitrogenase**, and only certain prokaryotes carry the enzyme:

```
N₂ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ + 16 ATP  ──nitrogenase──►  2NH₃ + H₂ + 16 ADP + 16 Pi
        │
        ├─ Rhizobium, Bradyrhizobium, Sinorhizobium  → LEGUME ROOT NODULES
        │     (symbiotic; plant supplies carbon, bacteria supply ammonia)
        ├─ Azotobacter, Azospirillum                 → free-living in soil
        └─ Anabaena, Trichodesmium (cyanobacteria)   → free-living in water/paddy
```

Three points carry the whole mechanism: nitrogenase is **irreversibly inactivated by oxygen**, so the nodule maintains low free O₂ while still respiring — leghaemoglobin binds O₂ to keep it available at low concentration (the nodule is pink, and the pigment is the visual proof of function); the reaction costs **16 ATP per N₂**, which the plant pays in photosynthate — which is why the symbiosis is worth its price to both partners; and the bacteria are specific — *Rhizobium leguminosarum* to peas, *R. meliloti* to alfalfa — a recognition exchange of flavonoids and Nod factors that decides which plant gets which bacterium.

**Crop rotation** with a legume works because nodules leave fixed nitrogen behind in residues and roots. The industrial alternative, the **Haber–Bosch process**, now fixes more nitrogen than all biology combined — and the ecological consequences of that surplus (eutrophication, dead zones) are covered in [03 — Nutrient cycles](../12-ecology/03-nutrient-cycles.md).

### Mycorrhizae: the other symbiosis

About 90% of land plants form **mycorrhizae** — fungal hyphae extending the effective root surface by orders of magnitude and trading **phosphorus** (and water, and micronutrients) for plant sugar. **Arbuscular mycorrhizae** (Glomeromycota, intracellular arbuscules) associate with most crop plants; **ectomycorrhizae** (pine, oak; sheath around the root) dominate temperate forests. The association is ancient — fossil roots with arbuscules predate the evolution of roots themselves — and it explains why most plants struggle in sterilised soil: the partner that mines the phosphate is missing.

## Medical relevance

**Faecal microbiota transplantation is colonisation resistance as therapy.** For recurrent *C. difficile* infection — repeatedly relapsing after antibiotics — transfer of screened donor stool restores the competitor community that the patient keeps losing, with cure rates around 80–90%, higher than further antibiotic courses. It is the clearest clinical proof that the ecosystem, not a single organism, is what protects the gut.

**Vaccine production uses these same hosts.** Hepatitis B surface antigen is made in yeast; virus-like particles and recombinant subunits are made in *E. coli* and insect cells — the manufacturing platform is microbial even when the target disease is viral.

**Diagnostics exploit microbial biochemistry.** The **Limulus amoebocyte lysate test** detects endotoxin (lipid A) in injectable fluids and devices by clotting horseshoe-crab blood extract — a direct application of the exotoxin/endotoxin distinction in [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](06-host-pathogen-interaction.md). Fermentation-based tests (indole, citrate, lactose) are the same reactions used to identify pathogens.

**Antibiotic discovery still depends on microbes**, which is why soil screening programmes and genome mining of *Streptomyces* continue — and why stewardship matters: every course of antibiotics is a selection event against the organisms we depend on for discovery as well as the pathogens we treat ([06 — Evolution](../06-evolution/02-natural-selection.md)).

**Agricultural microbiology is medical in effect.** Nitrogen-fixing inoculants reduce the need for industrial fertiliser; Bt crops reduce insecticide exposure; mycorrhizal inoculants reduce phosphate fertiliser run-off. Conversely, plant pathogens are still mostly microbes — the fungi and oomycetes of [04 — Fungi and protozoa](04-fungi-and-protozoa.md) — so crop health is microbiology too.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Bacteria are harmful by definition" | Most species are neutral or beneficial; **pathogenicity is the exception and is often strain-specific** — *E. coli* K-12 vs O157:H7 is the standard example. |
| "Normal flora and infection are different categories" | They are the **same organisms in different contexts**. Location, number, and host status decide the outcome — see [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](06-host-pathogen-interaction.md). |
| "Probiotics are a type of antibiotic" | Probiotics are **live protective organisms**; antibiotics are chemicals that kill or inhibit microbes. Their goals can even conflict. |
| "Fermentation is just rotting" | Fermentation is **controlled** — defined starter, dominated environment, deliberate endpoint. Spoilage is the same class of metabolism without control. |
| "All microbe-derived drugs are antibiotics" | Antibiotics are one class; microbes also yield **immunosuppressants, cholesterol-lowering statins (from *Penicillium*), and anticancer agents**, plus recombinant proteins from engineered hosts. |
| "Nitrogen fixation is done by plants" | Plants cannot reduce N₂. They **host** the fixers (nodules) or benefit from free-living ones; legumes supply carbon in exchange for ammonia. |
| "Mycorrhizae are parasites" | They take plant sugar but return **phosphorus, water, and pathogen protection** — the exchange is mutualistic, demonstrated by growth collapse in mycorrhiza-free soil. |
| "Germ-free life would be healthier" | Germ-free animals have impaired immune development, thinner gut mucosa, and greater susceptibility to invasion — **colonisation resistance and immune training require microbes.** |
| "Vitamin K deficiency is common in adults on antibiotics" | It is clinically important chiefly in **newborns**, whose gut is not yet colonised — hence routine vitamin K at birth — and in rare malabsorption, not in ordinary adult antibiotic courses. |
| "Bt toxin is sprayed on Bt crops" | In Bt crops the crystal protein is **expressed in the plant tissue** and must be eaten by the larva; spraying Bt is a separate, older practice — the toxin is the same either way. |
| "Bioremediation can clean up anything" | It works where the pollutant is **biodegradable and conditions can be maintained** — heavy metals and complex chlorinated compounds largely are not, and oxygen and nutrient supply limit the rate. |
| "Yeast fermentation and yeast respiration are the same" | Same organism, different pathways: **aerobic respiration yields ~15× more ATP**; fermentation runs when oxygen limits respiration, producing ethanol and CO₂ — bread rises and wine ferments for the same enzymatic reason. |

## Key facts

- **Context determines outcome**: the same species is mutualist or pathogen depending on site, number, and host defences — *E. coli* K-12 vs O157:H7, *Candida* carriage vs candidaemia, *C. difficile* before and after antibiotics.
- Normal flora provide **colonisation resistance, vitamin synthesis (notably K), immune training, and metabolic cooperation**; their destruction is itself a cause of disease.
- **Fermentation preserves food** by lowering pH or raising ethanol — lactic acid bacteria dominate dairy and vegetable fermentations; yeast dominates alcoholic ones; a **defined starter** is what separates fermentation from spoilage.
- Fermentation failure permits **clostridial** spoilage — *C. botulinum* in inadequately acidified anaerobic foods.
- Most antibiotics are **microbial competition products** — *Penicillium*, *Streptomyces* — which is why soil remains the discovery reservoir and why environmental resistance genes are ancient.
- Industrial hosts: ***E. coli* K-12** for cheap bacterial expression, ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae*** for eukaryotic folding and glycosylation (hepatitis B vaccine), *Bacillus* for secreted enzymes.
- **Restriction enzymes, plasmids, ligase, and CRISPR–Cas9 are all bacterial systems** repurposed as biotechnology tools.
- **Biological nitrogen fixation**: nitrogenase, 16 ATP per N₂, oxygen-sensitive — legume nodules with **leghaemoglobin**, free-living *Azotobacter*, cyanobacteria; the basis of crop rotation and the biological counterpart to Haber–Bosch.
- **Mycorrhizae** trade phosphorus for plant carbon to ~90% of land plants — the reason plants fail in sterilised soil.
- **Anaerobic digestion** ends in archaeal **methanogenesis**; aerobic treatment is measured as **BOD** removal — waste treatment is applied microbial ecology.
- **Faecal microbiota transplantation** cures recurrent *C. difficile* by restoring the community, not by adding a drug — the strongest clinical evidence for colonisation resistance.
- **Bt crops** express an insecticidal crystal protein in tissue; **phage biocontrol** is the specificity-matched alternative to antibiotics in agriculture.
- Probiotics are **live protective organisms with strain-specific evidence** — they are not antibiotics, and they do not treat invasive infection.

## Practice questions

**1. After a prolonged course of a broad-spectrum antibiotic, a patient develops pseudomembranous colitis. The underlying failure is best described as**

A. The antibiotic directly damaging the colonic epithelium
B. Loss of colonisation resistance allowing *Clostridioides difficile* to expand and produce toxin
C. Overgrowth of *Lactobacillus* producing excessive lactic acid
D. Conversion of the antibiotic into a toxin by gut bacteria

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Normal residents exclude newcomers by competing for nutrients and adhesion sites and by producing inhibitory substances — **colonisation resistance**. Antibiotics remove the competitors, and the intrinsically resistant, spore-forming *C. difficile* proliferates and releases toxins A and B, causing the pseudomembranes. A confuses an idiosyncratic drug reaction with the established mechanism; C would be protective, and lactic-acid organisms are not the cause; D describes no recognised reaction.

**2. Which pairing of organism and product is correct?**

A. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* — lactic acid in yoghurt
B. *Lactobacillus bulgaricus* — ethanol in wine
C. *Acetobacter* — acetic acid in vinegar
D. *Penicillium roqueforti* — carbon dioxide that opens Emmental cheese

**Answer: C**

Explanation: *Acetobacter* is an aerobic ethanol oxidiser — it converts wine alcohol to acetic acid, which is vinegar. A is wrong: *Saccharomyces* makes ethanol and CO₂, while yoghurt acid is lactic; B inverts the two; D assigns Emmental's eyes to the wrong organism — they are CO₂ from *Propionibacterium freudenreichii*, whereas *P. roqueforti* blue-moulds cheese such as Roquefort and Stilton.

---

**3. Nitrogen-fixing root nodules must maintain an unusual internal atmosphere because**

A. Nitrogenase requires oxygen as a substrate
B. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by oxygen, so O₂ must be kept low while respiration still proceeds
C. Leghaemoglobin stores oxygen for the plant's roots
D. Nodules are anaerobic so that methanogenesis can occur

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Nitrogenase has an iron–molybdenum cofactor that is destroyed by O₂, yet the bacterium needs oxygen for the 16 ATP per N₂ that the reaction costs. **Leghaemoglobin** buffers free oxygen at a low level — binding it tightly so respiration is fed but nitrogenase survives — which is why active nodules are pink. Nitrogenase uses electrons and ATP, not O₂ (A); leghaemoglobin's role is scavenging, not storage for the plant (C); methanogenesis is unrelated (D).

---

**4. Faecal microbiota transplantation works in recurrent *C. difficile* infection primarily by**

A. Introducing a single protective species that produces an antibiotic
B. Restoring the resident community so that colonisation resistance excludes *C. difficile*
C. Replacing the patient's damaged colonic epithelium
D. Supplying antibodies against *C. difficile* toxin

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The relapsing problem is ecological — each antibiotic course strips the flora again. Transplantation restores a **complete competitor community**, which regains the adhesion sites, nutrients, and inhibitory products that suppress *C. difficile*; cure rates far exceed those of further antibiotics. No single organism accounts for the effect (A); the epithelium heals once toxin exposure stops (C); stool contains negligible neutralising antibody relative to the ecological effect (D).

**5. The hepatitis B vaccine is produced in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* rather than *E. coli* because**

A. Yeast grows faster than *E. coli*
B. Yeast, as a eukaryote, can fold and glycosylate the surface antigen in a form that elicits protective immunity
C. Bacteria cannot be transformed with plasmids
D. Yeast cells are not destroyed by heat

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The surface antigen must display conformational and carbohydrate-dependent epitopes to induce protective antibody — post-translational processing that a bacterium's cytoplasm cannot perform. Yeast provides eukaryotic folding and glycosylation with the safety and cheapness of microbial fermentation. *E. coli* grows faster (A) and is readily transformed (C); heat stability is irrelevant to the choice of host (D).

**6. Which statement about mycorrhizal associations is correct?**

A. The fungus parasitises the root and reduces plant growth
B. The fungus extends the absorptive surface and trades phosphorus and water for plant-derived sugar
C. Only legumes form mycorrhizae
D. The association matters only in nutrient-rich soils

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Mycorrhizal hyphae explore soil volumes unreachable by roots and channel phosphorus, water, and micronutrients to the plant in exchange for photosynthate — the exchange is why most land plants grow poorly in mycorrhiza-free soil. The association is mutualistic, not parasitic (A); it is widespread across plant families, not restricted to legumes (C, which confuses mycorrhizae with rhizobia); it is most valuable precisely when phosphate is scarce (D).

**7. A cheese-maker adds *Penicillium roqueforti* to the curd. The organism's primary contribution is**

A. Rapid acidification that prevents all fungal growth
B. Proteolysis and lipolysis during ageing that generate characteristic flavour and blue veining
C. Ethanol production that preserves the cheese
D. Nitrogen fixation that raises the protein content

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Mould-ripened cheeses depend on fungal enzymes — proteases and lipases — breaking down casein and fat into flavour compounds over weeks of ageing, with hyphae creating the blue-green veins along air channels. Rapid acidification by lactic starters is characteristic of hard and fresh cheeses, not the defining role of the blue mould; ethanol is not the preservation mechanism here; and no cheese microbe fixes nitrogen.

**8. Which of the following is an example of a microbial enzyme used industrially?**

A. Catalase produced by human liver
B. Amylase from *Bacillus* used in detergents and starch processing
C. Insulin synthesised by the pancreas
D. Haemoglobin from red blood cells

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Microbial amylases hydrolyse starch at scale in food processing and laundry detergents — one of the highest-volume industrial enzyme classes, originally sourced from soil *Bacillus* and *Aspergillus* and now produced by engineered strains. A and C are human proteins, not industrial microbial products; D has no role in biotechnology. Recombinant human insulin, by contrast, is the *product* expressed *in* microbes, not a microbial enzyme.

---

**9. Which statement about restriction enzymes is correct?**

A. They are eukaryotic defence systems against bacteria
B. They cut DNA at specific recognition sequences and were discovered as a bacterial defence against bacteriophages
C. They are used by viruses to destroy host genomes
D. They synthesise DNA from an RNA template

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Restriction–modification systems let bacteria methylate their own DNA and cut unmodified (foreign, phage) DNA at defined palindromic sites — the property that made them the first precision tools of genetic engineering. They are bacterial, not eukaryotic (A); they are the cell's own defence, not a viral weapon (C); reverse transcriptase, not a restriction enzyme, copies RNA into DNA (D).

**10. The primary reason legume–rhizobium symbiosis is evolutionarily stable is that**

A. The plant fixes nitrogen itself and merely houses bacteria
B. Both partners gain: the plant receives fixed nitrogen it cannot obtain otherwise, the bacterium receives carbon it cannot fix itself
C. The bacterium gains nitrogen from the plant
D. The plant forces the bacteria to work without reward

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Neither partner is doing the other's job — plants cannot reduce N₂, and bacteria cannot photosynthesise. Each supplies what the other cannot, so cheating (fixing nothing, paying no carbon) is selected against by partner choice: Nod-factor recognition, sanctions against non-fixing nodules, and the cost of nodule construction. A and C invert the exchange; D ignores that the bacterium receives photosynthate, which is the entire point for it.
