# Fungi and Protozoa

## Why it matters

Fungi and protozoa are the two groups of microbial eukaryotes you will meet clinically, and they share one decisive problem: **they are built like your own cells.** A bacterium offers a wall, a 70S ribosome, and a folate pathway that human cells do not have — three clean targets. A fungus has 80S ribosomes, mitochondria, and a nucleus, just as you do, which is why antifungal therapy is narrower, slower, more toxic, and more likely to fail than antibacterial therapy. A protozoan is worse still: a eukaryotic parasite that lives inside your cells, whose essential pathways overlap with yours almost completely.

That is the organising idea of this chapter. Everything else — the chitin wall, the ergosterol membrane, the apicoplast — is an attempt to find the *one* difference that can be exploited safely. When you learn an antifungal or an antiprotozoal drug, the question to ask is always: **what does this organism have that I do not?**

Second, these organisms are masters of **stage switching**. A fungus switches between yeast and mould form to suit the temperature of the host; a protozoan alternates between a feeding trophozoite and a resistant cyst that survives outside a body. Each switch has a consequence — the cyst is why faecal–oral protozoal diseases spread through water, and the tissue cyst is why infection can relapse years after the original episode.

## Fungi: eukaryotic, walled, and slow

Fungi are eukaryotes — nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, **80S ribosomes** — but they differ from plants in ways that matter immediately.

| Feature | Fungi | Plants | Protozoa | Human cells |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wall** | **Chitin** (+ β-1,3-glucan, mannoproteins) | **Cellulose** | **Absent** | Absent |
| **Membrane sterol** | **Ergosterol** | Sitosterol/stigmasterol | Various (cholesterol often acquired) | **Cholesterol** |
| **Stored carbohydrate** | Glycogen | Starch | Glycogen | Glycogen |
| **Motility** | Non-motile (zoospores in oomycetes only) | Non-motile | Flagella/amoeboid/cilia | Cell crawling, cilia |
| **Ribosomes** | **80S** | 80S | **80S** | **80S** |
| **Nutrition** | Absorptive (secretes enzymes) | Photosynthetic | Ingestive/phagocytic | Heterotrophic |

**Chitin** — a polymer of *N*-acetylglucosamine — is a strong, flexible polysaccharide that also forms arthropod exoskeleton. It is completely absent from plants and from animals, which immediately marks the fungal wall as a drug target. Note the sugar: chitin is NAG polymerised β-1,4, the same sugar found in bacterial peptidoglycan, but with no NAM and no peptide cross-links — which is why lysozyme does little to fungal walls.

### Yeasts, moulds, and dimorphism

| Form | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Yeast** | Unicellular, reproduces by **budding**; oval cells 3–10 μm | *Candida*, *Cryptococcus*, *Saccharomyces* |
| **Mould (mould)** | Filamentous; branching **hyphae** forming a network (**mycelium**); spores on specialised structures | *Aspergillus*, *Rhizopus*, dermatophytes |
| **Dimorphic** | **Yeast at 37 °C (in the host); mould at 25 °C (in the environment)** — with *Blastomyces*, *Histoplasma*, *Coccidioides*, *Sporothrix*, *Talaromyces* | The classic thermal switch |

Dimorphism is a virulence strategy: the environmental form produces airborne spores that are small enough to reach the alveoli, and the body-temperature form is the one that grows in tissue. The transition itself is triggered by temperature and by the host's calcium–calmodulin and pH signalling.

### Hyphae: septate vs coenocytic

```
SEPTATE (cross walls with pores)      COENOCYTIC / ASEPTATE (no cross walls)
                                       continuous multinucleate cytoplasm

 ─────┤├──────┤├──────┤├─────         ═══════════════════════════════════
      │        │        │              ○   ○   ○   ○   ○   ○   ○
 ─────┤├──────┤├──────┤├─────         ═══════════════════════════════════
      │        │        │
 Aspergillus, Candida,                Rhizopus, Mucor (Mucorales)
 dermatophytes                        broad, ribbon-like, irregular width
```

| Feature | Septate | Coenocytic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cross walls** | Present, with central pores allowing cytoplasmic flow | Absent |
| **Typical width** | Narrow, uniform (2–4 μm for *Aspergillus*) | Broad, irregular, ribbon-like (7–25 μm) |
| **Branching angle** | ~45° dichotomous (*Aspergillus*) | **Right angles**, haphazard (Mucorales) |
| **Clinical use** | Histology identifies genus directly | The broad non-septate ribbon with right-angle branching in a nasal biopsy of a diabetic patient is **mucormycosis** until proven otherwise |

**The microscopy distinction is a diagnosis**, not a description: septate narrow hyphae at 45° → *Aspergillus*; broad non-septate hyphae at 90° → *Rhizopus*/*Mucor*.

### Spores

Fungal spores are **reproductive and dispersal structures** — nothing like a bacterial endospore, which is a survival dormancy of a single cell:

| Spore type | Formed by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conidia** (aleuriospores) | Asexual, exposed on conidiophores, airborne | *Aspergillus fumigatus* — the infectious dose is inhaled conidia |
| **Arthroconidia** | Hyphae fragmenting into chains | *Coccidioides immitis* — highly infectious in the laboratory |
| **Chlamydoconidia** | Thick-walled survival spores within hyphae | *Candida*, some dermatophytes |
| **Sporangiospores** | Inside a sporangium | *Rhizopus* |
| **Sexual spores** (ascospores, basidiospores) | Sexual cycle; ascus or basidium | *Saccharomyces* (ascospores), *Cryptococcus* (basidiospores) |
| **Endospores** | Spherule filling with endospores in tissue | *Coccidioides* — the tissue phase |

**Two rules that prevent confusion:** fungal spores *increase in number* (they are reproductive); bacterial endospores do not. And fungal spores are the **infectious form in the environment** — it is the conidium you inhale, not the hypha, that establishes pulmonary histoplasmosis.

### Mycoses: three categories

| Category | Entry and behaviour | Organisms | Host |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Superficial / cutaneous** (dermatophytosis) | Contact with keratinised tissue; **cannot invade below the epidermis** — no vascular access | *Trichophyton*, *Microsporum*, *Epidermophyton* → tinea corporis, pedis, capitis, cruris, unguium | Immunocompetent |
| **Systemic / endemic (dimorphic)** | Inhaled spores → pulmonary infection → may disseminate | *Histoplasma*, *Blastomyces*, *Coccidioides*, *Paracoccidioides* | Immunocompetent (often asymptomatic or mild) |
| **Opportunistic** | Enter when defences fail; normal flora or environmental contaminants | ***Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*, *Cryptococcus neoformans***, *Mucorales*, *Pneumocystis jirovecii* | **Immunocompromised** — HIV, neutropenia, transplant, steroids, diabetes |

Dermatophytes secrete **keratinases** and are confined to dead keratin — which explains why treatment takes months for nails: the drug must reach avascular, slowly growing tissue, and the infected keratin must grow out.

The three opportunistic organisms worth knowing in detail:

- ***Candida albicans*** — commensal of gut, mouth, vagina. Causes oral thrush, vulvovaginal candidiasis, oesophagitis in HIV, and candidaemia from central lines and catheters (biofilm-forming). Dimorphic in tissue: budding yeast **plus pseudohyphae and true hyphae**; the **germ tube test** (incubate in serum, look for emerging tube without constriction) is the rapid identification step.
- ***Aspergillus fumigatus*** — small conidia (2–3 μm) that reach the alveoli; in neutropenia, invasive pulmonary aspergillosis with angioinvasion and haemoptysis; allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis in cystic fibrosis and asthma; **galactomannan** in serum is the biomarker.
- ***Cryptococcus neoformans*** — an **encapsulated** yeast (glucuronoxylomannan capsule) from pigeon droppings; inhaled → disseminates to the meninges in HIV/AIDS. **India ink** shows the capsule as a clear halo; **urease** and **mucicarmine** are diagnostic. Cryptococcal meningitis is treated with amphotericin B plus flucytosine, then fluconazole — and the capsule makes it a textbook example of a virulence structure in [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](06-host-pathogen-interaction.md).

### Antifungal targets and the selective-toxicity problem

Because fungi are eukaryotic, every target must be checked against the human equivalent.

| Target | Drug class | Mechanism | Why it is selective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ergosterol synthesis — lanosterol 14-α-demethylase (CYP51)** | **Azoles**: fluconazole, voriconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole | Inhibit a fungal cytochrome P450 → ergosterol depleted, methylated sterols accumulate → membrane defective, growth **fungistatic** | Fungal CYP51 differs from human CYPs — but not completely: human CYP3A4/17/2C9 are partly inhibited → **drug interactions and teratogenicity** |
| **Ergosterol synthesis — squalene epoxidase** | **Allylamines**: terbinafine | Blocks squalene → ergosterol; squalene toxic to fungus | Distinct from human enzyme |
| **Ergosterol in the membrane** | **Polyenes**: **amphotericin B**, nystatin | Binds ergosterol directly, forming pores → ion leakage → fungicidal ("sterol fence": prefers ergosterol over cholesterol) | Partial selectivity — **binds cholesterol weakly → nephrotoxicity, infusion reactions** |
| **β-1,3-glucan in the wall** | **Echinocandins**: caspofungin, micafungin | Inhibit **β-1,3-glucan synthase** → wall cannot maintain osmotic integrity | **Humans have no cell wall and no glucan synthase → excellent tolerance** |
| **Nucleic acid synthesis** | **Flucytosine (5-FC)** | Taken up by fungal **cytosine deaminase** → converted to 5-fluorouracil → blocks thymidylate synthase and RNA | Activation requires a fungal enzyme humans lack |
| **Mitotic spindle** | **Griseofulvin** | Disrupts **microtubule** assembly → fungistatic; deposits in keratin | Narrow use — dermatophytes only |
| **Mitochondrial electron transport (cytochrome *bc*₁)** | **Atovaquone** | Blocks complex III in *Plasmodium*, *Toxoplasma*, and *Pneumocystis* | Parasite cytochrome *b* differs from host complex III |

**The logic to carry forward:** the **cell wall** is the best target because we have none; **ergosterol** is the next best but the membrane chemistry overlaps with cholesterol enough to cause toxicity; **80S ribosomes are useless as a target** because they are identical in structure to ours. That is the whole reason antifungal courses are long and why systemic antifungal therapy carries real adverse effects.

## Protozoa: eukaryotic parasites with a stage problem

Protozoa are **single-celled eukaryotes** — 80S ribosomes, mitochondria (usually), a nucleus, no cell wall. They are classified by their means of locomotion, and each group carries a signature disease.

| Group | Locomotion | Diagnostic stage | Major pathogens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sarcodina (amoebae)** | **Pseudopods** (amoeboid crawling on actin) | Trophozoite and cyst | ***Entamoeba histolytica***, *E. dispar*, *Acanthamoeba* (keratitis), *Naegleria* (meningoencephalitis) |
| **Mastigophora (flagellates)** | **Flagella** | Trophozoite; cyst (except *Trichomonas*) | ***Giardia lamblia***, ***Trichomonas vaginalis***, ***Trypanosoma brucei***, ***T. cruzi***, *Leishmania* |
| **Ciliophora (ciliates)** | **Cilia** | Trophozoite; cyst | ***Balantidium coli*** — the only ciliate pathogen |
| **Apicomplexa (sporozoans)** | Gliding by **apical complex**; no flagellum in the mature stage | Oocyst / sporozoite | ***Plasmodium*** (malaria), ***Toxoplasma gondii***, *Cryptosporidium*, *Cyclospora*, *Babesia* |

### Trophozoite vs cyst — the central distinction

```
                    TROPHOZOITE                      CYST
            ────────────────────────────   ────────────────────────────
Shape        irregular, motile              rounded, defined wall
Metabolism   feeding, active                dormant, no feeding
Reproduction binary fission (yes)           none
Environment  HOST TISSUE only               SOIL / WATER / HOST
Resistant    no — dies quickly outside      YES — survives months,
                                            chlorination, stomach acid
Diagnostic   acute/liquid stool,            chronic/asymptomatic
significance tissue biopsy                  carriage
Disease role causes symptoms                TRANSMITS infection
```

**The rule:** the **trophozoite causes disease**, the **cyst transmits it**. Fresh diarrhoeal stool must be examined *warm* because trophozoites disintegrate within an hour; cysts persist and are what a lab finds in a stored specimen. This is why faecal–oral transmission of protozoal disease is so efficient — one cyst ingested with contaminated water survives gastric acid and establishes infection in the gut. The persistence and transmission of cysts is treated in [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](05-microbial-reproduction-and-transmission.md).

### The apicoplast: an organelle that is a drug target

Apicomplexans were once thought to be "primitive" because they lack obvious organelles. They are not primitive — they carry a **plastid**, the **apicoplast**, a relic of secondary endosymbiosis of a red alga, related to the chloroplast described in [02 — Cell Biology](../02-cell-biology/04-energy-and-containment-organelles.md).

```
Apicoplast: double membrane, no photosynthesis, genome present
        │
        ├─→ type II FATTY ACID SYNTHASE (FAS II)   → phospholipids
        ├─→ MEP/DOXP pathway → isoprenoid precursors  → unique to parasites
        ├─→ haem biosynthesis
        └─→ translation on plastid-type ribosomes

        druggable because none of these pathways operates
        in the human cytosol in the same form
```

**Drugs exploiting the apicoplast and its parasites:**

| Drug | Target | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fosmidomycin** | DOXP reductoisomerase (MEP pathway) | Apicoplast isoprenoid synthesis — research use |
| **Clindamycin, doxycycline, azithromycin** | **Apicoplast ribosomes and translation** | Slow because the apicoplast must be diluted out of the parasite over several cycles — explains why these are **slow-acting antimalarials** |
| **Atovaquone** | Mitochondrial **cytochrome *bc*₁** | Rapid kill; resistance arises rapidly if used alone → combined as atovaquone–proguanil |
| **Pyrimethamine, proguanil** | Parasite **dihydrofolate reductase** | Host DHFR is less sensitive — the basis of the antifolate combination |
| **Sulfadiazine** | Parasite dihydropteroate synthase | Folate synthesis — humans lack the pathway entirely |
| **Artemisinins** | Activated by haem iron; **multiple targets including protein damage** | Rapid-acting; the frontline of malaria treatment |
| **Chloroquine** | Blocks **heme detoxification** (β-haematin formation) in the parasite food vacuole | Free heme is toxic to the parasite; resistance is a transport mutation (*pfcrt*) |

### Plasmodium: the life cycle you must be able to draw

```
MOSQUITO (Anopheles) bite
        │  injects SPOROZOITES (salivary gland)
        ▼
LIVER  ── invade hepatocytes ──► exo-erythrocytic SCHIZOGONY
        │   (~7–10 days; P. vivax/ovale form dormant HYPNOZOITES
        │    that reactivate months–years later)
        ▼  thousands of merozoites released into blood
RBC    ── invade erythrocytes ──► ring → trophozoite → SCHIZONT
        │                          (synchronous RBC rupture)
        │   released merozoites re-infect new RBCs  ◄── repeat
        │                          48 h (P. falciparum/vivax/ovale)
        │                          72 h (P. malariae) = FEVER PERIODICITY
        │   some differentiate into GAMETOCYTES
        ▼
MOSQUITO takes up gametocytes → SEXUAL STAGE in gut
        │  zygote → ookinete → oocyst → sporozoites
        ▼  migrate to salivary glands — cycle repeats
```

| Point | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Disease mechanism** | RBC rupture releases heme, merozoites, and cytokine-inducing material → **TNF-driven paroxysmal fever**; sequestration of infected RBCs in microvasculature causes cerebral malaria (*P. falciparum*) |
| **Why RBC?** | No nucleus, no MHC class I — the parasite hides from antibody and T cells inside a cell that cannot present antigen |
| **Diagnosis** | **Thick film** (detection, lyses RBCs to concentrate parasites); **thin film** (species identification by morphology and % parasitaemia) |
| **Resistance** | Chloroquine (*pfcrt*), sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (DHFR mutations), artemisinin partial resistance (Kelch13) in South-East Asia |

| Organism | Group | Stage that infects | Transmission | Disease and signature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ***Entamoeba histolytica*** | Amoeba | **Cyst** | Faecal–oral, water | Amoebic dysentery with blood and mucus; **trophozoites ingest RBCs** (pathognomonic); liver abscess with "anchovy sauce" pus. *E. dispar* is morphologically identical but non-pathogenic |
| ***Giardia lamblia*** | Flagellate | **Cyst** | Faecal–oral, beaver/cattle-contaminated water | Malabsorptive, foul fatty diarrhoea; no invasion, no blood. Trophozoite: binucleate "face", ventral suction disc |

| ***Trypanosoma brucei*** | Flagellate | **Trophozoite (procyclic)** | **Tsetse fly (*Glossina*)** bite | African sleeping sickness; **antigenic variation of VSG** — sequential coats defeat antibody response; meningoencephalitic stage |
| ***Trypanosoma cruzi*** | Flagellate | Amastigotes in gut | Triatomine bug **faeces rubbed into bite** | Chagas disease: myocarditis, megaesophagus, megacolon; congenital transmission |
| ***Leishmania* spp.** | Flagellate | Amastigote in macrophage | **Sandfly** bite | Visceral (kala-azar), cutaneous, mucocutaneous |
| ***Toxoplasma gondii*** | Apicomplexan | **Oocyst** (cats) / tissue cyst | Cat litter, undercooked meat, transplacental | Congenital hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis; **reactivation encephalitis in HIV** |
| ***Cryptosporidium*** | Apicomplexan | **Oocyst** | Waterborne, chlorination-resistant | Self-limiting cholera-like diarrhoea in immunocompetent; **severe and chronic in HIV** — no reliable drug |
| ***Plasmodium*** | Apicomplexan | Sporozoite | *Anopheles* mosquito | Malaria — see above |
| ***Balantidium coli*** | Ciliate | **Cyst** | Faecal–oral (pig reservoir) | Balantidiasis — dysentery resembling amoebiasis |

### Why protozoal disease is harder to treat than bacterial disease

| Reason | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| **Eukaryotic machinery** — 80S ribosomes, tubulin, DNA replication, folate enzymes all resemble ours | Few selective targets; narrow therapeutic index |
| **Intracellular location** (macrophages, hepatocytes, RBCs, neurons) | Drug must penetrate host cells, not just reach the organism |
| **Multiple stages with different biology** | A drug killing trophozoites may do nothing to cysts or hypnozoites — **primaquine** is needed for *P. vivax* hypnozoites and for transmission blocking |
| **Long or complex courses** | Antimalarials, antiamoebics, and anti-*Toxoplasma* therapy run for weeks |
| **Slow or absent replication in some stages** | Drugs targeting replication fail against dormant cysts — the reason relapse occurs |
| **No effective vaccine for most** | Prevention rests on vector control and prophylaxis |

**The practical clinical rule follows:** antibacterial therapy can be short and curative; antiprotozoal therapy is often **stage-specific, longer, and partly suppressive**, and some infections (chronic *Entamoeba* carriage, *Toxoplasma* tissue cysts, *P. vivax* hypnozoites) are treated specifically to eliminate a dormant stage that would otherwise relapse.

## Medical relevance

**Antifungal choice follows the site.** Topical azoles or terbinafine for cutaneous disease; oral fluconazole for mucosal candidiasis; echinocandin first-line for candidaemia (biofilm-active, well tolerated); **amphotericin B plus flucytosine** then step-down fluconazole for cryptococcal meningitis (the combination is used because amphotericin is rapidly fungicidal and flucytosine is fungicidal but resistance emerges quickly alone); **voriconazole** for invasive aspergillosis. Amphotericin's **nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions** ("amphoterrible") follow directly from its partial binding to cholesterol — the price of partial selectivity.

**The HIV connection is unavoidable.** Cryptococcal meningitis, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia, oesophageal candidiasis, toxoplasmic encephalitis, and cryptosporidial diarrhoea are all **AIDS-defining opportunistic infections**. Their presence in a patient is a clinical readout of CD4 count: *Pneumocystis* below ~200 cells/µL, toxoplasma and cryptococcus below ~100.

**Malaria kills more people than any other protozoan disease.** Chloroquine resistance now covers most *P. falciparum*-endemic regions, so **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** is the standard; atovaquone–proguanil and doxycycline are used for prophylaxis by travellers. G6PD deficiency protects against primaquine-induced haemolysis — a pharmacogenomic test that must precede radical cure of *P. vivax*.

**Chagas and sleeping sickness are vector-borne chronic diseases** that present decades after exposure — chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy appears 10–30 years after the initial bite, which is why screening of blood donors and congenital testing matter. **Benznidazole and nifurtimox** work best in the acute phase and poorly in established chronic disease.

**Mucormycosis in uncontrolled diabetes** is a medical emergency: angioinvasive hyphae cause black eschar on the palate and orbit, and treatment is **surgical debridement plus amphotericin B** — controlling the underlying diabetic ketoacidosis is part of therapy because the fungus thrives on iron availability and acidic, high-glucose tissue.

**Diagnostic pearls worth memorising:** India ink for *Cryptococcus*; wet mount showing motile trophozoites with ingested red cells for *Entamoeba*; "strawberry cervix" for *Trichomonas*; thick and thin blood films for malaria; germ tube for *Candida*; 45° septate hyphae for *Aspergillus* versus broad non-septate right-angle hyphae for Mucorales.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Fungal cell walls are like plant cell walls" | Plants use **cellulose**; fungi use **chitin** — chemically unrelated and pharmacologically distinct. |
| "Yeast and mould are different organisms" | They are **morphological forms**; many fungi are **dimorphic** and switch between them with temperature. |
| "Fungal spores and bacterial endospores are the same" | Fungal spores are **reproductive and numerous**; an endospore is a **single-cell survival state** with no increase in cell number. |
| "Antibiotics treat fungal infection" | Fungi are eukaryotic — antibacterials (wall, 70S, folate) have no target. **Antifungals target ergosterol, glucan, or fungal-specific enzymes.** |
| "Amphotericin B is perfectly selective" | It prefers ergosterol but **binds cholesterol** → nephrotoxicity. Partial selectivity is why it is reserved for severe infection. |
| "A trophozoite in stool always means infection" | **Cysts indicate infection and carriage; trophozoites in liquid stool indicate acute disease** — but *E. dispar* trophozoites are indistinguishable and harmless. |
| "All protozoa have cysts" | *Trichomonas vaginalis* has **no cyst stage** — which is why it can only be transmitted sexually. |
| "Malaria is caused by a bacterium" | *Plasmodium* is an **apicomplexan protozoan**, eukaryotic, with 80S ribosomes — hence the difficulty of treatment and the failure of antibacterials. |
| "Intracellular means the same thing for bacteria and protozoa" | *Mycobacterium* blocks phagosome–lysosome fusion; *Plasmodium* digests haemoglobin in a food vacuole; *Toxoplasma* lives in a parasitophorous vacuole. Same location, different cell biology — and different drug requirements. |
| "Vaccines prevent all these infections" | There is **no licensed human malaria vaccine of broad efficacy** (RTS,S/AS01 is partial and geographically limited), and none for most protozoa; control is vector-, water-, and drug-based. |

## Key facts

- Fungi are **eukaryotes** with **chitin walls**, **ergosterol** membranes, glycogen stores, and **80S ribosomes** — unlike plants (cellulose, sitosterol, starch).
- **Yeast** = unicellular budding; **mould** = filamentous hyphae; **dimorphic** fungi switch yeast ↔ mould with temperature (37 °C ↔ 25 °C).
- **Septate** hyphae (Aspergillus, Candida) vs **coenocytic** hyphae (Rhizopus, Mucor); on histology, narrow 45° branching = *Aspergillus*, broad non-septate right-angle = Mucorales.
- Mycoses: **superficial/cutaneous** (dermatophytes, keratin-limited), **systemic/dimorphic** (inhaled, may disseminate), **opportunistic** (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*, *Cryptococcus* — immunocompromised hosts).
- Antifungals: **azoles** (ergosterol synthesis, CYP51), **polyenes** (bind ergosterol — amphotericin toxicity from cholesterol binding), **echinocandins** (β-1,3-glucan synthase — no human target), **flucytosine** (fungal-specific activation), **griseofulvin** (spindle).
- **80S ribosomes cannot be a fungal drug target** — they are the human machinery.
- Protozoa are classified by locomotion: **amoeboid, flagellate, ciliate, apicomplexan**.
- **Trophozoite = feeding, causes disease; cyst = resistant, transmits disease**; *Trichomonas* and *Toxoplasma* tissue stages have no environmental cyst in the same sense.
- The **apicoplast** — a relic plastid carrying fatty acid, isoprenoid, and haem pathways — is a validated drug target in *Plasmodium* and *Toxoplasma*.
- **Plasmodium**: liver schizogony → RBC cycle (48/72 h periodicity) → gametocytes → mosquito sexual stage; hypnozoites in *P. vivax*/*ovale* require **primaquine**.
- Protozoal disease is hard to treat because the target machinery is **eukaryotic, intracellular, and present in several stages** — including dormant ones.
- Cyst-mediated **faecal–oral transmission** makes water treatment and sanitation the decisive control measure — see [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](05-microbial-reproduction-and-transmission.md).

## Practice questions

**1. The most important difference between fungal and plant cell walls, from a therapeutic standpoint, is that fungi**

A. Contain cellulose instead of chitin
B. Contain chitin and β-glucan, which are absent in human cells, giving an antifungal target
C. Have no wall at all
D. Use cholesterol instead of ergosterol

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Fungal walls are chitin plus β-1,3-glucan covered by mannoproteins; neither is present in a human cell, so drugs such as echinocandins (β-1,3-glucan synthase inhibitors) are selectively toxic with a wide margin. Plants use cellulose, not chitin (A). Fungi definitely have a wall (C), and it is the fungal membrane that contains **ergosterol**, whereas human membranes contain cholesterol (D).

**2. Hyphae seen on a nasal biopsy are broad, non-septate, and branch at right angles. The most likely organism is**

A. *Aspergillus fumigatus*
B. *Candida albicans*
C. *Rhizopus* or another Mucorales fungus
D. *Trichophyton rubrum*

**Answer: C**

Explanation: The combination of broad, ribbon-like, aseptate hyphae with irregular right-angle branching is characteristic of the Mucorales (*Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Rhizomucor*), the cause of mucormycosis — a medical emergency in uncontrolled diabetes and neutropenia. *Aspergillus* has narrow, regular, septate hyphae branching at ~45°; *Candida* shows yeast and pseudohyphae; dermatophytes are septate and confined to keratin.

**3. Echinocandins are more selective than azoles mainly because they**

A. Inhibit fungal 80S ribosomes
B. Block β-1,3-glucan synthesis in the cell wall, a structure human cells do not possess
C. Bind ergosterol with perfect specificity
D. Are activated only by human enzymes

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The cell wall is absent from human cells, so inhibiting β-1,3-glucan synthase cannot damage the host — which is why echinocandins have such a favourable toxicity profile. Azoles act on ergosterol synthesis, which partially overlaps biochemically with human cholesterol synthesis and cytochrome P450 metabolism, producing drug interactions. Polyenes (amphotericin) bind cholesterol weakly as well as ergosterol, causing nephrotoxicity (C is wrong for that class), and no antifungal works by inhibiting 80S ribosomes because those ribosomes are shared with the host (A).

**4. In *Entamoeba histolytica*, the role of the cyst is to**

A. Cause tissue invasion and dysentery
B. Transmit infection — it survives outside the host and initiates disease after ingestion
C. Reproduce sexually in the human intestine
D. Escrow antibiotics during treatment

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The trophozoite is the feeding, invasive stage that causes symptoms (and ingests red blood cells), but it dies rapidly outside the body. The cyst is the resistant, non-feeding stage that survives chlorination, desiccation, and gastric acid, making it the form found in food and water and the reason faecal–oral transmission dominates. Cysts do not invade, do not reproduce in the environment, and are not a resistance structure against drugs.

**5. Why is the apicoplast considered an attractive drug target in malaria?**

A. It is a bacterial-type organelle unique to the parasite, carrying fatty acid, isoprenoid, and haem pathways that human cells lack in that form
B. It contains 70S ribosomes that human cells lack entirely
C. It makes chloroquine
D. It is present in human liver cells

**Answer: A**

Explanation: The apicoplast is a relic plastid from secondary endosymbiosis of a red alga. It runs type II fatty acid synthesis, the MEP/DOXP isoprenoid pathway, and haem biosynthesis — pathways either absent from humans or organised so differently that inhibitors such as fosmidomycin and apicoplast-targeting antibiotics can discriminate. Human cells do contain 70S ribosomes, in mitochondria (B is therefore wrong as stated), and apicoplasts are absent from human cells (D). Chloroquine targets heme detoxification in the parasite food vacuole, not the apicoplast (C).

**6. The periodic fever of malaria is generated by**

A. Bacterial toxin release during bacteraemia
B. Synchronous rupture of infected red blood cells releasing merozoites and heme with a TNF-driven response
C. Direct infection of hypothalamic neurons
D. Hypoglycaemia caused by parasite glucose consumption

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Sporozoites mature in the liver, then merozoites invade red cells in a synchronised cycle — every 48 hours for *P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, and *P. ovale*, every 72 hours for *P. malariae*. When the schizont ruptures, released parasites and toxic free heme drive a macrophage cytokine surge (TNF, IL-1) producing the classic rigor–fever–sweats paroxysm. The parasite does not infect the hypothalamus (C), this is not bacteraemia (A), and hypoglycaemia is a complication of severe malaria or of quinine therapy rather than the cause of periodicity (D).

**7. A patient with HIV and a CD4 count of 60 cells/µL develops headache, neck stiffness, and a positive India ink preparation. The organism is**

A. *Cryptococcus neoformans*
B. *Candida albicans*
C. *Trichomonas vaginalis*
D. *Histoplasma capsulatum*

**Answer: A**

Explanation: India ink outlines the polysaccharide capsule of *Cryptococcus neoformans* as a clear halo around the yeast cell — the classic finding in cryptococcal meningitis, an AIDS-defining infection typically occurring below 100 CD4 cells/µL. *Candida* causes mucosal and bloodstream disease but is not identified by India ink; *Trichomonas* is a flagellate of the urogenital tract; *Histoplasma* is a dimorphic mould causing pulmonary and disseminated disease and is identified in tissue as narrow-budding yeast inside macrophages.

**8. Why are fungal infections generally harder to treat than bacterial infections?**

A. Fungi have no genetic material
B. Fungi replicate faster than bacteria
C. Fungi are eukaryotic, so most of their cellular machinery is shared with the human host, leaving few selective targets
D. Fungi are not affected by any drug

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Antibacterial therapy exploits structures and pathways bacteria possess and we lack — peptidoglycan, 70S ribosomes, folate synthesis, DNA gyrase. Fungi have 80S ribosomes, mitochondria, nuclei, and cholesterol-adjacent membranes, so overlap is extensive. Only the wall (chitin/glucan), ergosterol itself, and a handful of fungal-specific enzymes remain — hence longer courses, fungistatic agents, and toxic drugs such as amphotericin B. A is absurd, B is false (fungi grow far more slowly), and D is disproved by echinocandins and azoles.

**9. Which statement about *Trichomonas vaginalis* is correct?**

A. It forms cysts that survive on fomites
B. It is transmitted by the faecal–oral route
C. It has no cyst stage and is transmitted sexually as a trophozoite
D. It is an apicomplexan

**Answer: C**

Explanation: *T. vaginalis* has no cyst — the trophozoite alone survives briefly in the genital tract and is transferred during sexual contact, which is why it causes urethritis and vaginitis rather than intestinal disease. Option A describes *Giardia* and *Entamoeba*; B describes those same faecal–oral organisms; D is wrong — *Trichomonas* is a flagellated mastigophoran, whereas apicomplexans (malaria, *Toxoplasma*) have an apical complex and no flagellum in the mature stage.

**10. Chloroquine resistance in *Plasmodium falciparum* is best explained by**

A. Loss of the parasite's mitochondrion
B. Mutation of a food-vacuole transporter that prevents chloroquine from accumulating where it blocks heme detoxification
C. Increased production of human haemoglobin
D. The parasite developing a cell wall

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Chloroquine concentrates in the parasite's digestive vacuole, where it binds free heme released from haemoglobin digestion and prevents its polymerisation into inert hemozoin; free heme is toxic, so blocking detoxification kills the parasite. Resistance most often involves *pfcrt* (and *pfmdr1*) transporter mutations that lower vacuolar drug concentration — the target is intact but the drug cannot reach it. This is the same principle as porin loss in Gram-negative bacteria: reduced uptake rather than target change.
