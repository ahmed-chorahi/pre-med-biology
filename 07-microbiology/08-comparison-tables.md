# Comparison Tables

## Why it matters

Chapters 01–06 explain each group, structure, and process separately. Exams — and clinical decisions — almost never ask about one of them in isolation: they ask you to **tell two things apart under pressure**, usually by a single feature that carries the answer. This chapter is the consolidation: every distinction that the earlier chapters built up slowly is set side by side here, with the one load-bearing difference highlighted in each row.

The method matters as much as the content. Almost every microbiology comparison resolves through one of four questions: **Does the structure exist? What is it made of? Is it alive and when? Does the drug have a target?** If you can ask those four questions of any pair of terms — spore or cyst, exotoxin or endotoxin, Gram-positive or Gram-negative — the correct row falls out of the mechanism rather than out of recall. The tables below are arranged exactly that way, and the final section explains how to use them in an exam.

## Table 1 — Prokaryote, archaeon, eukaryote, and virus

| Feature | Bacterium (prokaryote) | Archaeon | Eukaryotic cell | Virus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nucleus** | None; circular chromosome in nucleoid | None; circular chromosome | True nucleus, linear chromosomes with histones | No nucleus — genome inside the capsid |
| **Genome** | Usually one circular chromosome, genes mostly in operons; plasmids common | Circular chromosome, operons; plasmids and **multiple rRNA copies** (target of archaeal phylogenetics) | Several linear chromosomes, introns, histones | **DNA *or* RNA**, single or double strand, segmented or not — never both |
| **Ribosomes** | **70S** (target of many antibiotics) | **70S** but sequence and drug-binding features differ from bacterial ribosomes | **80S** cytosolic (70S in mitochondria/chloroplasts) | **None** — uses the host's ribosomes |
| **Cell wall** | **Peptidoglycan** (NAG–NAM with peptide cross-links) | **Pseudopeptidoglycan, S-layer proteins, or absent — no peptidoglycan** | None (cellulose, chitin, or no wall) | None — **n/a, which is why β-lactams cannot work on it** |
| **Membrane lipids** | Ester-linked fatty acids | **Ether-linked isoprenoid lipids**, often monolayer in thermophiles | Ester-linked fatty acids with cholesterol | Envelope (if present) **stolen from the host membrane** |
| **Division** | Binary fission; no spindle | Binary fission | Mitosis/meiosis with spindle | **Assembly** from components — no division at all |
| **Metabolism** | Full metabolism | Full metabolism, including **methanogenesis** (no other life does this) | Full metabolism | **None — obligate parasite, not even a metabolism** |
| **Response to antibiotics** | Cell-wall, 70S, folate, gyrase targets **work** | **No peptidoglycan → β-lactams useless**; ribosomal drugs differ | Not affected by antibacterials (mitochondrial caveat) | No antibacterial or antifungal target — needs **antivirals** acting on virus-specific enzymes |
| **Examples** | *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *M. tuberculosis* | Halophiles, thermophiles, methanogens, Asgard archaea | Your cells, fungi, protozoa, plants | Influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2, bacteriophages |

## Table 2 — Gram-positive vs Gram-negative

| Feature | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Peptidoglycan** | **Thick** (20–80 nm), multi-layered | Thin (7–8 nm), single layer |
| **Outer membrane** | **Absent** | **Present** — asymmetric bilayer with **LPS outside** |
| **Endotoxin (lipid A)** | No | **Yes — the source of endotoxic shock** |
| **Teichoic / lipoteichoic acids** | **Present** (anchoring, adhesion, cation control) | Absent |
| **Periplasmic space** | Minimal | **Well-defined** — site of β-lactamases and transport |
| **Porins** | None needed | **Outer-membrane porins** control what enters — loss = resistance |
| **Gram stain colour** | **Purple** (crystal violet retained by thick peptidoglycan) | **Pink** (decolourised, counterstained with safranin) |
| **Morphology typical** | Cocci in clusters/chains; rods | Rods (cocci: *Neisseria*, *Vibrio*) |
| **Exotoxin production** | Common (clostridia, streptococci, staphylococci) | Some (cholera, Shiga, diphtheria is a Gram-positive rod) |
| **Intrinsic antibiotic resistance** | Sensitive to **vancomycin** (too large to cross an outer membrane) | Resistant to large hydrophobic drugs, lysozyme, many penicillins — **porins and β-lactamases** |
| **Classic targets** | Wall synthesis (penicillin, vancomycin) | Wall synthesis + porin-dependent uptake; quinolones, aminoglycosides |
| **Examples** | *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Bacillus*, *Clostridium*, *Corynebacterium* | *E. coli*, *Pseudomonas*, *Salmonella*, *Neisseria*, *Bacteroides* |

**The Gram stain mechanism** is in [01 — Bacterial structure and physiology](01-bacterial-structure-and-physiology.md); the therapy rule that follows from this table is that **the first Gram stain result halves the antibiotic options before culture is even incubated.**

## Table 3 — Exotoxin vs endotoxin

| Property | Exotoxin | Endotoxin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chemistry** | Protein | **Lipid A** (portion of LPS) |
| **Released by** | Secretion from a living cell | **Lysis** of Gram-negative cells (and membrane vesicles) |
| **Produced by** | Mostly Gram-positive (also cholera, pertussis, Shiga toxin producers) | **All Gram-negative bacteria** |
| **Gene location** | Often **plasmid or bacteriophage**-carried (diphtheria toxin = phage gene; toxin genes move by transduction) | Chromosomal — part of core LPS synthesis |
| **Heat stability** | **Labile** (60–80 °C destroys) | **Stable** (survives 100 °C, 1 h) |
| **Potency** | Extreme — µg quantities lethal | Low — large amounts required |
| **Action** | Specific: A–B enzymatic modification, pore formation, superantigen cross-linking | General: **TLR4 → NF-κB → TNF/IL-1/IL-6** |
| **Clinical picture** | Disease-specific: paralysis, secretory diarrhoea, shock (TSS) | Uniform: **fever, hypotension, DIC, septic shock** |
| **Toxoid vaccine** | **Yes** (tetanus, diphtheria) | **No** |
| **Neutralising antibody** | Effective once given | Not clinically useful |
| **Effect of killing the bacterium** | Source stops | **Transient WORSENING** — lysis releases a bolus of lipid A |

## Table 4 — Survival structures: spore, cyst, oocyst, tissue stage, capsule

This is the comparison promised in [01 — Bacterial structure and physiology](01-bacterial-structure-and-physiology.md): five structures that are constantly confused because they all "protect" something, for completely different reasons.

| Structure | What it actually is | Made by | Formed when | Purpose | Survives heat/chemicals? | Increases cell number? | Control measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bacterial endospore** | A single cell **internally rebuilt** — dehydrated core, Ca²⁺–dipicolinic acid, SASPs, keratin coat | *Bacillus*, *Clostridium* | Starvation, overcrowding | **Survival** of the organism through hostile periods | **Yes — only autoclave (121 °C) or incineration reliably kills it** | **No** — 1 cell → 1 spore → 1 cell | **Sterilisation**, not disinfection |
| **Protozoan cyst** | Resistant dormant stage with a wall, no feeding, no division | *Giardia*, *Entamoeba*, *Balantidium* | Leaving the host | **Transmission** through water, food, surfaces | Resists **chlorination and gastric acid**; not heat in the laboratory sense | No | **Water treatment, sanitation, hand hygiene** |
| **Protozoan oocyst** | Sporulated environmental stage (often after sexual reproduction) | *Toxoplasma*, *Cryptosporidium*, *Cyclospora* | Shed in faeces, sporulates outside | Transmission; *Crypto*'s oocyst is **notably chlorine-resistant** | Variable — *Crypto* resists standard chlorination | No (sporulation yields sporozoites within one oocyst) | Filtration, advanced water treatment |
| **Tissue cyst / hypnozoite** | Dormancy **inside the host** — wall-bound, non-dividing | *Toxoplasma* tissue cysts; *P. vivax* hypnozoites | Chronic/latent infection | **Relapse reservoir** years later | n/a (in host) — requires drug that reaches tissue | No | **Radical cure** (e.g. primaquine for hypnozoites) |
| **Fungal spore** | **Reproductive/dispersal** cell (conidium, arthroconidium, sporangiospore) | Fungi, including yeasts and moulds | Reproduction or stress | Dispersal — and the **infectious form in the environment** | Moderate — desiccation-tolerant, **not** autoclave-resistant in the same league | **Yes — one spore → a new mycelium, numbers multiply** | Filtration of air; disinfection of surfaces |
| **Capsule** | **Extracellular surface layer on a living, dividing cell** — polysaccharide (or glucuronoxylomannan in *Cryptococcus*) | *S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*, *Klebsiella*, *Cryptococcus* | Continuously, during growth | **Immune evasion** — anti-phagocytic, prevents opsonisation | **No — killed like any vegetative cell** | Yes (it grows and divides) | Antibiotics, vaccines against the capsule |

**The one-line rules:** the **spore resists the environment** (heat, chemicals); the **cyst transmits between hosts**; the **tissue cyst relapses within a host**; the **fungal spore reproduces and multiplies**; the **capsule does not protect against anything physical — it protects against you, not against heat.**

## Table 5 — The four infectious agents: what each one is made of, and where the drug binds

| Feature | Bacteria | Fungi | Protozoa | Viruses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cell type** | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic | Eukaryotic | Acellular |
| **Genome** | DNA (usually one chromosome) + plasmids | DNA, linear chromosomes | DNA | **DNA *or* RNA only** |
| **Ribosomes** | **70S** | 80S | 80S | None (host's) |
| **Wall / coat** | **Peptidoglycan** | **Chitin + β-glucan** | **None** | Capsid (+ envelope) |
| **Membrane sterol** | None | **Ergosterol** | Various, often host cholesterol | Host-derived envelope lipids |
| **Replicates** | In the host (or environment) | In the host | In the host, often **intracellular** | **Only inside a host cell** |
| **Key drug targets** | Peptidoglycan, **70S**, folate, gyrase, RNA polymerase | Wall β-glucan, **ergosterol/CYP51**, squalene epoxidase | Apicoplast pathways, folate, heme detoxification, tubulin (varies by species) | Virus-specific **polymerases, protease, integrase, reverse transcriptase, neuraminidase** |
| **Representative drugs** | Penicillin, vancomycin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, rifampicin, co-trimoxazole | Echinocandins, azoles, amphotericin B, terbinafine | Chloroquine, artemisinin, metronidazole, atovaquone–proguanil | Acyclovir, tenofovir, protease inhibitors, oseltamivir |
| **Selectivity basis** | Structures/pathways **humans lack** | Wall (none in us) + ergosterol (partially overlaps cholesterol → toxicity) | Limited — hence narrow therapeutic index | Targets **do not exist in host cells** |
| **Why treatment fails** | Resistance (enzymes, efflux, porins, target change) | Few targets; 80S ribosomes unusable | Eukaryotic overlap + intracellular location + **dormant stages** | Mutations in viral targets; latent genome has nothing to target |

Helminths are omitted deliberately: they are multicellular eukaryotic parasites whose targets are even fewer (nicotinic receptors, tubulin — ivermectin, albendazole), and the same rule holds: **the more the parasite resembles the host, the fewer targets exist.**

## Table 6 — Killing and controlling microbes

| Method | Principle | Kills spores? | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Autoclave (121 °C, 15 psi, 15–20 min)** | Moist-heat protein denaturation under pressure | **Yes** | Surgical instruments, culture media, dressings |
| **Boiling (100 °C)** | Moist heat | **No** | Cleaning only — never claimed as sterilisation |
| **Pasteurisation (72 °C, 15 s)** | Mild heat | No | Milk and juice — kills pathogens, keeps flavour |
| **Hot-air oven (160–170 °C, 2 h)** | Dry-heat oxidation | **Yes** | Glassware, metal, powders |
| **Incineration** | Complete combustion | Yes | Loops, clinical waste |
| **UV (254 nm)** | Thymine dimers block replication | No | Air and surface only — **no penetration** |
| **Ionising radiation** | DNA strand breakage | Yes | Sterile packaging: syringes, sutures |
| **Filtration (0.22 µm)** | Physical removal | Yes (spores retained) | Heat-labile liquids, air (HEPA) |
| **Alcohol (60–80%)** | Protein/membrane denaturation | **No** | Hand rubs, skin — vegetative cells and envelopes |
| **Chlorhexidine / povidone–iodine** | Membrane and protein damage | No | **Antisepsis on living tissue** |
| **Hypochlorite, peroxides** | Oxidation | Yes (adequate contact time) | Spills, *C. difficile* cleaning, water |
| **Glutaraldehyde, OPA** | Alkylate proteins | Yes (prolonged) | Endoscope high-level disinfection |
| **Ethylene oxide** | Alkylation | Yes | Heat-sensitive plastics and electronics |
| **Quaternary ammonium** | Surfactant, membrane disruption | **No — not even mycobacteria** | Low-level disinfection of furniture and floors |

## Table 7 — The vocabulary of antimicrobial testing

| Term | Exact definition | Not to be confused with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **MIC** | Lowest concentration that **prevents visible growth** | MBC (which measures killing) |
| **MBC** | Lowest concentration that **kills ≥99.9%** of inoculum | MIC (inhibition) |
| **Bacteriostatic** | Inhibits growth at achievable concentrations; clearance requires host immunity | "Weak" — static is a strategy, not a failure |
| **Bactericidal** | Kills outright (MBC:MIC commonly ≤4) | Always clinically superior — not true in every infection (endocarditis yes, uncomplicated cystitis can be static) |
| **Spectrum (narrow / broad)** | Range of species affected | Selectivity — spectrum is about microbes, **selective toxicity is about host vs microbe** |
| **Empirical therapy** | Chosen from Gram stain, source, and local resistance data before culture | Directed therapy — narrowed once MICs return |
| **Susceptible / Intermediate / Resistant** | Breakpoint categories from zone diameter or MIC vs clinical outcome | Absolute properties — breakpoints are **drug-, site-, and organism-specific** |
| **Selective toxicity** | Harm the microbe, spare the host | Spectrum — a broad-spectrum drug can still be selective |

## Table 8 — Lytic vs lysogenic (and where latency sits)

| Feature | Lytic cycle | Lysogenic cycle | Latency (eukaryotic viruses) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Genome fate** | Replicated, host genome degraded | **Prophage/provirus integrated or episomal** — replicated with host | Viral genome persists in a specific cell type (neurons, resting CD4⁺ T cells) |
| **Host cell** | Lysed — progeny released | **Survives and divides** carrying the genome | Survives; may be non-dividing |
| **Immediate disease** | Yes — acute | **Usually none** — silent carriage | None while latent |
| **Danger** | Direct lysis | **Inducible**: UV/heat/stress → excision → lysis; toxin genes expressed (diphtheria toxin is a **phage gene**) | **Reactivation on immunosuppression** — zoster, CMV, HIV rebound, EBV |
| **Immune visibility** | Strong (viral proteins displayed early) | Minimal — few proteins made | Minimal until reactivation |
| **Clinical example** | Influenza, rabies, poliovirus | *C. diphtheriae* with tox+ phage, *C. botulinum* prophage, λ phage | HSV in sensory ganglia, HIV provirus, VZV in dorsal root ganglia |
| **Epidemiological consequence** | Cases track exposure | **Carriers** — the reservoir link of the chain of infection | Asymptomatic shedding escapes symptom screening |

The full replication cycles and the plaque assay are in [03 — Viruses](03-viruses.md); the carrier and reservoir consequences are in [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](05-microbial-reproduction-and-transmission.md).

## Table 9 — Transmission routes and the single interruption each requires

| Route | Mechanism | Typical examples | The link that breaks it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct contact** | Skin-to-skin, mucosal contact, droplets at close range | Impetigo, herpes, gonorrhoea, influenza (droplet) | **Hand hygiene, gloves, mask at <1 m** |
| **Indirect (fomites)** | Contaminated instruments, linen, equipment | *S. aureus* on devices, rotavirus on surfaces, endophthalmitis after surgery | **Sterilisation of instruments, surface disinfection, single-use items** |
| **Airborne** | Nuclei or dust <5 µm, suspended for hours, travels far | Tuberculosis, measles, varicella, *Legionella* (aerosolised water) | **Ventilation, HEPA filtration, N95 respirators** |
| **Faecal–oral (vehicle: water/food)** | Ingestion of cysts, bacteria, or viral particles shed in faeces | Cholera, *Giardia*, hepatitis A, typhoid | **Water treatment, sanitation, food hygiene, hand hygiene** |
| **Vector-borne** | Biological (replication in arthropod: mosquito, tick, tsetse) or mechanical (fly on food) | Malaria, Lyme disease, plague, dengue | **Vector control — insecticides, bed nets, elimination of breeding sites** |
| **Vertical** | Mother to child: transplacental, perinatal, breast milk | HIV, syphilis, rubella, hepatitis B, Zika | **Antenatal screening, treatment in pregnancy, neonatal prophylaxis** |
| **Sexual** | Mucosal exchange of secretions | HIV, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, *T. vaginalis* | **Barrier methods, screening, treatment of partners** |

Each row is one link of the **chain of infection** from [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](05-microbial-reproduction-and-transmission.md) — the point of the framework is that the intervention is chosen by naming the route.

## Medical relevance

**The first decision is always an identification question, and these tables are the decision tree.** Gram reaction narrows the antibiotic list within an hour of the stain; toxin type tells you whether killing the organism helps (endotoxin release risk) or whether neutralising the toxin matters (antitoxin, toxoid-primed immunity); the structure present tells you whether a disinfectant or an autoclave is required.

**The structures table is infection-control policy.** A spore on a surface means bleach and contact time, not alcohol; a spore in an instrument load means the autoclave cycle failed; an oocyst in a water supply means chlorination alone will not suffice — *Cryptosporidium* outbreaks are stopped by filtration, not by raising the chlorine. Misclassifying the structure means choosing an intervention that cannot work.

**Latency tables explain screening policy.** Lytic infections are controlled by isolating symptomatic cases; lysogenic and latent infections cannot be, because the reservoir is the asymptomatic carrier — which is why HIV and hepatitis screening, blood-supply testing, and contact tracing during reactivation exist at all.

**The testing table explains prescribing language.** When a report says "intermediate," it is not hedging — the breakpoint integrates tissue penetration, dosing, and clinical outcome data. Understanding that MIC is inhibition while MBC is killing explains why a static drug still cures an uncomplicated infection in an immunocompetent patient, and why a cidal drug is demanded when immunity cannot assist (endocarditis, prosthetic device infection, neutropenic sepsis).

## Common confusions this section retires

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Archaea are just bacteria that like heat" | They are a separate domain: **ether-linked isoprenoid membranes, no peptidoglycan, information machinery closer to eukaryotes** — and β-lactams fail for a structural reason, not a lifestyle one. |
| "A virus is a very small cell" | A virus has **no ribosomes, no metabolism, and no division** — it assembles. Size is the least important difference. |
| "Gram-negative means weak" | The thin wall is paired with an **outer membrane that blocks many drugs** — Gram-negative organisms are frequently the *harder* treat. |
| "Endotoxin is secreted like an exotoxin" | Endotoxin is **built into the wall** and released on lysis — which is why lyse-inducing antibiotics can worsen shock. |
| "Spores and cysts protect against the same things" | Spores resist **heat and chemicals**; cysts resist **chlorination, desiccation, and stomach acid**; the controls differ accordingly. |
| "A capsule is a dormant form" | A capsule is worn by a **living, dividing** cell; it confers immune evasion, not environmental survival. |
| "Killing the bacterium always improves the patient" | With endotoxin, rapid lysis can **transiently worsen** the inflammatory surge — the reason source control and fluids accompany antibiotics. |
| "Static drugs are inferior" | Static drugs work when the immune system finishes the job; **cidal drugs are required when it cannot** (valves, devices, neutropenia). |
| "Broad-spectrum means better" | It means **more organisms affected, including your flora** — with every cost of losing colonisation resistance. |
| "Disinfection is sterilisation, just weaker" | Not a matter of degree — disinfection **leaves spores**, so instruments must never be treated as disinfected. |
| "Fungal spores behave like bacterial endospores" | Fungal spores **reproduce and multiply**; endospores never increase cell number. Different purpose, different control. |
| "Transmission route is an academic detail" | It **determines the intervention** — a mask for droplets, HEPA for airborne, chlorination for faecal–oral, bed nets for vector-borne. |

## How to use these tables in an exam

1. **Identify what is being compared before recalling anything.** Structure pair (Gram+/−, spore/capsule), agent pair (fungus/virus), or process pair (lytic/lysogenic) — each has a different load-bearing column.
2. **Find the one row that cannot be shared.** Gram-negative has lipid A; fungi have ergosterol; viruses have no ribosome. Examiners write distractors that are true of the *other* row.
3. **Ask the four questions in order:** Does the structure exist? What is it made of? Is it alive, and when does it form? Does a drug have a target there?
4. **Convert the row into a mechanism.** Do not memorise "endotoxin = fever": lipid A → TLR4 → TNF/IL-1 → hypothalamic prostaglandin → fever. A mechanism survives an unfamiliar question; a fact does not.
5. **Check the clinical consequence.** Most comparison questions are clinical in disguise — "which disinfectant," "why did the patient worsen," "why is this drug chosen" — and the answer is the last column of the relevant table.

## Key facts

- Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and have **70S ribosomes**; eukaryotes have a nucleus and **80S ribosomes**; viruses have neither — they use yours.
- Archaea differ from bacteria in **membrane chemistry (ether-linked isoprenoids), wall (no peptidoglycan), and information machinery (eukaryote-like)** — which is why penicillin has no target.
- Gram-positive = thick peptidoglycan, no outer membrane, purple, vancomycin-susceptible; Gram-negative = thin wall, **outer membrane with LPS and porins**, pink, intrinsically resistant to large hydrophobic drugs.
- Exotoxins are **proteins, labile, specific, toxoidable**; endotoxin is **lipid A, heat-stable, chromosomal, non-toxoidable**, and released on lysis.
- **Endospore** = survival, heat-resistant, no multiplication → autoclave; **cyst** = transmission, chlorination-resistant → water treatment; **fungal spore** = reproduction, multiplies; **capsule** = anti-phagocytic layer on a living cell, no heat resistance.
- Drug targets divide by kingdom: bacteria → wall/**70S**/folate/gyrase; fungi → wall/ergosterol; protozoa → few, largely eukaryotic; viruses → **virus-specific enzymes only**.
- **MIC inhibits, MBC kills**; static vs cidal depends on concentration and on whether host immunity can finish the job.
- Sterilisation kills spores; the autoclave reference is **121 °C, 15 psi, 15–20 min**; UV does not penetrate; filtration is for heat-labile material.
- Lytic cycles destroy the host immediately; lysogenic and latent cycles create **carriers and reactivation reservoirs** that symptom-based screening cannot detect.
- Every transmission route maps to **one specific interruption** — naming the route names the control measure.
- Selective toxicity, not spectrum, is what makes a drug safe: **the target must exist in the microbe and not in the host.**

## Practice questions

**1. Which single feature most reliably distinguishes an archaeon from a bacterium?**

A. Archaea are larger and visible under a light microscope
B. Archaea have ether-linked isoprenoid membranes and lack peptidoglycan
C. Archaea possess a nucleus
D. Archaea are sensitive to all antibiotics that work on bacteria

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The domain-defining characters are **ether-linked isoprenoid lipids** (often monolayers) and the absence of peptidoglycan — the structural reason β-lactams fail against them. Archaea are prokaryotic in cell plan, so C is wrong; size does not define the domains (A); and D inverts the logic — resistance follows from whether the target exists, not from a blanket rule (neither universal sensitivity nor universal resistance is true).

---

**2. The critical structural difference between a bacterial endospore and a protozoan cyst is that**

A. Only the cyst contains DNA
B. The endospore is an internal survival state resistant to heat, while the cyst is a transmission stage resistant to chlorination and stomach acid
C. The cyst is formed by *Bacillus* and the endospore by *Giardia*
D. The cyst multiplies the organism while the endospore does not

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The endospore is a whole-cell rebuild (dehydrated core, Ca²⁺–dipicolinic acid, SASPs) that survives autoclaving — which is why sterilisation is required; the cyst is the environmentally resistant, non-feeding stage that carries protozoal infection between hosts through water and survives chlorination and gastric acid. Both contain DNA (A), the genera are swapped in C, and neither multiplies cell number (D).

---

**3. A patient's Gram-negative sepsis temporarily worsens hours after starting a bactericidal antibiotic. The best explanation is**

A. The patient developed an allergy to the antibiotic
B. Lysis of the bacteria released lipid A, amplifying the cytokine response
C. The antibiotic is pyrogenic
D. The organism changed to a resistant strain

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Endotoxin is a structural component of the outer membrane, so rapid killing releases it in bulk → TLR4 → TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6 surge → transiently worse fever, hypotension, and coagulopathy. This is managed by supportive care alongside antibiotics, not by stopping them. Allergy presents with rash or bronchospasm (A); antibiotics are not pyrogens (C); resistance does not emerge in hours as an explanation for this pattern (D).

---

**4. Which row correctly pairs an organism with its drug target?**

A. Fungus — peptidoglycan synthesis
B. Virus — 70S ribosome
C. Bacterium — folate synthesis pathway absent from humans
D. Protozoan — chitin wall

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Bacteria synthesise folate from PABA; humans lack the pathway entirely, which is the complete basis of sulfonamide–trimethoprim selectivity. Fungi have **chitin** walls (A); viruses have no ribosomes of their own at all (B); protozoa have no wall (D) — and each distractor confuses the kingdoms' structures.

---

**5. The reason disinfection is unacceptable for surgical instruments is that disinfectants**

A. Work too slowly for clinical schedules
B. Do not reliably destroy endospores, which sterilisation must
C. Damage metal surfaces
D. Cannot penetrate water

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The definition that separates the two processes is spores: sterilisation destroys **all** forms including endospores, disinfection may leave them alive. Instruments entering sterile tissue therefore require an autoclave (121 °C, 15 psi, 15–20 min) or validated equivalent. Speed and corrosion are engineering considerations, not the defining criterion (A, C), and penetration is a property of the method, not the objection (D).

---

**6. A disc diffusion report shows a 22 mm zone for drug X and 8 mm for drug Y against the same organism. This means**

A. Drug X is bactericidal and drug Y is static
B. Drug X produced the larger zone under these test conditions — interpretation requires the breakpoint table for that drug–organism pair
C. Drug Y should never be used
D. The organism is resistant to both

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Zone size depends on diffusion rate, potency, and breakpoints — a larger zone usually but not automatically means susceptibility, and only comparison against the published breakpoint for that specific drug and organism gives *S/I/R*. Cidal vs static cannot be read from zone diameter (A); resistance or efficacy cannot be concluded without the breakpoints (C, D).

---

**7. Which statement about lysogenic viruses is correct?**

A. They always lyse the host cell immediately
B. Their genome can persist with the host and be induced later, and can carry toxin genes such as diphtheria toxin
C. They cannot be transmitted between hosts
D. They require a high fever to replicate

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Lysogeny integrates or maintains the viral genome through host division — silent carriage — and induction (stress, UV) can switch to lysis; in *C. diphtheriae* the toxin itself is a **phage-encoded gene**, so the strain is only pathogenic when lysogenic. Immediate lysis defines the lytic cycle (A); carriers transmit (C); fever is a host response, not a replication requirement (D).

---

**8. A waterborne outbreak of cryptosporidiosis in a chlorinated municipal supply is best explained by**

A. Failure of the chlorination to kill bacteria
B. The oocyst's resistance to standard chlorination, requiring filtration or advanced treatment
C. The presence of a bacterial endospore
D. Direct person-to-person transmission only

**Answer: B**

Explanation: *Cryptosporidium* oocysts resist the chlorine levels used in ordinary water treatment, so outbreaks occur even when bacterial indicators are controlled — the intervention that works is **filtration or UV of the water**, not more chlorine. A misses the point (bacteria are killed); C misidentifies the stage; D ignores the faecal–oral water route that defines the outbreak.

---

**9. Which transmission route requires HEPA filtration and N95 respirators rather than simple hand hygiene?**

A. Faecal–oral
B. Direct contact
C. Airborne
D. Vector-borne

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Airborne transmission uses droplet nuclei or dust that remain suspended, travel beyond 1 m, and are inhaled — so the intervention must clean the *air* (ventilation, HEPA) and protect the *respiratory tract* (N95). Hand hygiene breaks faecal–oral and contact routes (A, B); vector-borne transmission requires insect control (D).

**10. The most efficient way to distinguish a fungal spore from a bacterial endospore in an exam question is to ask**

A. Which one is larger
B. Whether it increases the number of organisms — fungal spores reproduce, endospores do not
C. Whether it contains DNA
D. Whether it forms in the presence of oxygen

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Purpose follows from behaviour: a fungal spore **germinates into a new individual and multiplies numbers**, which is also why it is the environmentally infectious form (inhaled conidia cause histoplasmosis); an endospore is a **survival state of the same cell** — one cell in, one cell out — and never increases the population. Both contain DNA (C); size and oxygen conditions do not define the distinction (A, D).
