# Characteristics of Life

## Core idea

Biology is the study of life, and the first real work of the subject is agreeing on what life is. It is harder than it sounds. A crystal grows reproducibly; a fire consumes fuel and spreads; a virus reproduces only inside a host. Both look alive in places, and neither is.

Biology handles this by defining life **operationally** — as a set of properties that living things share. No single property is sufficient on its own. A mule is sterile and cannot reproduce; a single-celled amoeba does not have tissues. What matters is that living things as a group are distinguished by a cluster of characteristics.

## The characteristics of life

### 1. Organization

Every living thing has an ordered internal structure, and its parts depend on one another.

```
ATOM → MOLECULE → MACROMOLECULE → ORGANELLE → CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM → ORGANISM
```

Remove any level and the level above it loses its function. This is the definition of **open systems** — the opposite of a closed system: living things maintain internal order by taking in energy and matter and discarding what they cannot use. Entropy increases in the universe as a whole; a living thing maintains local order by spending energy to export entropy.

**Medical relevance:** an injury that destroys the organization of a tissue — a cut through the layers of the intestinal wall, a fracture through a bone's trabeculae — impairs function not because of what was added, but because of what was lost.

### 2. Metabolism

**Metabolism** is the sum of all chemical reactions in an organism. It has two halves:

| Type | Also called | What it does | Energy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anabolism** | Assimilation | Builds complex molecules from simple ones | Requires energy |
| **Catabolism** | Dissimilation | Breaks complex molecules into simpler ones | Releases energy |

Catabolism and anabolism are not separate processes that take turns — they are coupled. The energy released by breaking down glucose pays for the building of proteins, and the same coupling runs inside a single cell for every reaction that needs coupling.

**Medical relevance:** the metabolic rate defines how many calories a body needs per day. A thyroid hormone problem changes the rate of essentially every metabolic reaction in the body, which is why it produces symptoms across several systems at once.

### 3. Homeostasis

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal condition despite a changing external environment. It is covered properly in [09 — Homeostasis](09-homeostasis.md), and the full control loop in 09-human-biology/03-homeostasis.md.

**Medical relevance:** most of the "vital signs" measured in a clinic — temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, blood glucose, oxygen saturation — are homeostasis being reported on. The values are not arbitrary; each is the set point a control system is defending.

### 4. Growth and development

Living things increase in size (**growth**) and change from one form to another over their lifetime (**development**). Both are controlled by information and by hormone signalling, not by simple addition of matter.

**Common confusion:** growth and development are separable. An infant grows and develops at once. A person of normal height with an untreated growth-hormone deficiency in childhood grows little but still develops normally in every other respect.

### 5. Reproduction

Life derives from life. Cellular reproduction (mitosis) allows growth and repair; organismal reproduction (meiosis followed by fertilization) allows continuation of a lineage. An individual need not reproduce for life to continue — but the species cannot persist for many generations without it.

**Common confusion:** sterile organisms are alive. Reproduction is a property of the species, not a requirement for individual life.

### 6. Response to the environment

Living things detect and respond to stimuli — changes in light, temperature, chemicals, gravity, sound, and mechanical force.

```
STIMULUS → RECEPTOR DETECTS → SIGNAL TRANSDUCED → RESPONSE
```

A hand on a hot surface is the standard example, and it is worth tracing all the way through, because it contains the whole reflex arc in miniature: skin receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → (no time to consult the brain) → motor neuron → muscle contraction → withdrawal. A plant's response is slower but uses the same logic: a shoot bends toward light because cells on the shaded side elongate faster than cells on the lit side.

### 7. Adaptations and evolution

Populations of living things have **adaptations** — inherited traits that improve function in a particular environment. Adaptations arise and spread through **evolution** across generations, not within an individual's lifetime.

That last clause carries more weight than it looks like it should. A bacterium developing resistance to an antibiotic is not the individual bacterium adapting; it is a resistant variant already existing and multiplying. See 06 — Evolution.

### 8. Energy processing

Living things acquire energy and transform it. Plants and some bacteria capture light. Others capture chemical energy from compounds. All of them convert that energy into a form they can spend — almost always **ATP**, the molecule covered in [01-biochemistry/05-enzymes.md](../01-biochemistry/05-enzymes.md) and 03 — Cellular Processes.

## What is not life

The test is more useful applied to cases than to definitions.

| Thing | Why it seems alive | Why it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A crystal | Grows in ordered patterns | No metabolism; growth is a physical precipitation process, not chemistry the crystal performs |
| A flame | Consumes fuel, grows, dies out | No information processing, no boundary, no self-repair |
| A virus | Has genetic material; reproduces inside a host | Cannot perform metabolism or ribosome-based protein synthesis on its own; replication is an instruction handed to a host |
| A computer | Stores information and responds to input | No metabolism, no growth from nutrients, no independent reproduction |
| A seed | Appears inert; can produce a whole plant | Metabolism is greatly reduced but not absent — it respires slowly and repairs cellular damage |

That last row is the useful one. Dormancy is a *reduction* in activity, not the absence of life. A dormant seed still runs repair metabolism and still holds ion gradients.

## Medical relevance

The characteristics of life are not exam trivia; they are the definitional basis of how medicine reasons.

**Disease is a failure of a life characteristic.** That framing is more precise than "disease is when something goes wrong."

| Failed characteristic | Example of what it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Organization | Tissue architecture destroyed — ulcerated intestinal wall, fractured bone |
| Metabolism | Diabetes: cells cannot take up glucose effectively; metabolism does not stop, it is misrouted |
| Homeostasis | Fever, electrolyte disturbance, blood pressure collapse |
| Growth/development | Cancer — cells grow without the normal developmental constraints; failure of development produces congenital anomalies |
| Response | Neuropathy — an abnormal or absent response to a normal stimulus |
| Energy processing | Mitochondrial disease — a defect in ATP generation, felt in every high-demand tissue |
| Reproduction | Infertility |

The two items worth memorizing as a pair: **failure of development → congenital anomaly**, **uncontrolled growth → cancer**.

## Key facts

- Life is defined by a **set of properties**, not a single defining feature.
- Living things are **open systems** that maintain internal order by consuming energy.
- Metabolism has two coupled halves: **anabolism** (builds, costs energy) and **catabolism** (breaks down, releases energy).
- **Homeostasis** — stable internal conditions — is a defining feature of life.
- **Adaptations** belong to populations across generations, never to individuals within a lifetime.
- A virus is not a cell and not independently alive; it lacks ribosomes and metabolism.
- A dormant organism is alive: metabolism is reduced, not absent.

## Practice questions

**1. A feature that all living things share is**

A. An ability to maintain a relatively stable internal environment
B. The ability to move independently to find food
C. A cell wall made of cellulose
D. The ability to reproduce sexually

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Homeostasis is universal — even a bacterium maintains its internal pH and ion balance. B excludes plants and fungi, which do not move, and many organisms that never relocate. C applies only to plants. D excludes bacteria, which divide by binary fission, so it describes only a subset of eukaryotes.

---

**2. Which statement about an adaptation is correct?**

A. All adaptations are permanent and irreversible
B. An adaptation is an inherited trait that improves function in a particular environment
C. Adaptations only increase organism fitness, never decrease it
D. An individual organism adapts over its lifetime by developing resistance and passing that resistance on

**Answer: B**

Explanation: An adaptation is an inherited (or in microbe terminology, heritable) trait shaped by natural selection and suited to a specific environment. A is wrong because environments change, so adaptations are context-dependent rather than permanent. C is wrong because the value of a trait is relative to the environment, and what is adaptive in one setting can be neutral or costly in another. D is the classic Lamarckian error — individuals do not hand down acquired traits; populations do.

---

**3. Crystals can grow into highly ordered, geometric structures. What is the key difference between a crystal and a living organism?**

A. Crystals cannot grow at high temperatures
B. Crystals are made of atoms from living things
C. Crystals always contain carbon
D. Crystals do not perform metabolism to build themselves from nutrients

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Both can produce ordered structure, but a living organism builds that structure by running chemical reactions, obtaining matter and energy from its environment and discarding waste. Crystal growth is a physical precipitation process driven by the surroundings' chemistry; the crystal does not control it.

---

**4. An organism is sterile and cannot reproduce. Which statement follows from this?**

A. It cannot be a living thing
B. It must lack DNA
C. It is alive, because reproduction is a property of the species rather than a requirement for individual life
D. It must be an eukaryote

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Reproduction maintains a species across generations, but no single characteristic is sufficient on its own to define life. Sterile individuals are alive.

---

**5. An amoeba engulfs a particle, moves toward a chemical gradient, and converts the particle's chemical energy into ATP. Which characteristic of life is being demonstrated?**

A. Reproduction
B. Development
C. Growth
D. Metabolism and response

**Answer: D**

Explanation: The described behaviours involve acquiring matter, transforming it, and responding to a chemical stimulus — catabolism plus response. Growth would mean an increase in overall size; development would mean a change in form over time.

---

**6. A fire spreads, consumes fuel, and eventually goes out. Which comparison with living tissue is most accurate?**

A. A fire lacks metabolism, information processing, and cellular boundaries, so it is not alive
B. Living tissue is alive because it produces heat, which is the same reason a fire is alive
C. A fire and living tissue both build ordered structure, so they are both alive
D. A fire is alive because it reproduces

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Heat production is a byproduct of metabolism in living tissue; it is not what makes something alive. A fire has no capacity to process information, build itself from nutrients, or maintain a membrane that separates an internal environment.

---

**7. Which of the following is the best example of growth without development?**

A. A seed germinating into a seedling
B. A unicellular organism increasing in size without changing which structures it contains
C. A teenager developing secondary sexual characteristics
D. A child becoming taller over one year

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Growth is increase in size; development is a change in form or internal organization. B shows size increasing with no qualitative change in what the organism is made of. A, C, and D all involve change in form.

---

**8. Why are living things described as open systems?**

A. Their cell membranes contain pores
B. They are not completely sealed by a skin or shell
C. They exchange both matter and energy with their surroundings
D. Their atoms are not permanently bonded

**Answer: C**

Explanation: A living thing takes in nutrients and energy and expels waste and heat — a sustained flow of both matter and energy across a boundary. That exchange is what funds the internal order it maintains. B confuses the biological term with a physical observation; D is true of the chemical bonds in molecules but does not describe system-level behaviour.
