# Atoms and Elements

## Core idea

Every biological molecule is built from a small set of elements. Understanding what those elements are and why carbon dominates is the difference between memorizing a formula and understanding why biochemistry works the way it does.

## What is an atom?

An **atom** is the smallest unit of an element that retains that element's chemical properties.

```
SUBATOMIC PARTICLES
 ├── PROTONS     in the nucleus; positive charge; defines the element
 ├── NEUTRONS    in the nucleus; no charge; defines the isotope
 └── ELECTRONS    outside the nucleus; negative charge; involved in bonding
```

An element is defined entirely by its number of protons, called its **atomic number**. Change the proton count and you have a different element — that is the only thing that makes iron iron. A neutron count changes the **isotope** but not the element; a change in electron count produces an **ion** but does not change the element either.

This is worth stating precisely because all three are routinely confused:

| Change | Result | Element still the same? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton count | Different element | No |
| Neutron count | Different isotope | Yes |
| Electron count | Ion (cation if lost, anion if gained) | Yes |

## The six elements that make up biology

Living things are mostly a handful of elements by mass, plus a long tail of trace elements required in trace amounts.

| Element | Symbol | % of human body mass | Primary roles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen | O | ~65% | Water, carbon dioxide, the O₂ in carboxyl and phosphate groups |
| Carbon | C | ~18% | Backbone of all organic molecules |
| Hydrogen | H | ~10% | Bridges and fills valences; carries protons |
| Nitrogen | N | ~3% | Amino groups, nucleotide bases |
| Calcium | Ca | ~1.5% | Bone mineral, blood clotting, muscle contraction, cell signalling |
| Phosphorus | P | ~1% | Phosphate groups, ATP, nucleic acids, phospholipids |

The rest — sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, sulfur, iron, iodine, and others — are present in small amounts, and several are functionally essential in traces. Iron sits at roughly 0.006% of body mass and is required for haemoglobin and the electron transport chain; iodine at well under 0.001% is required to make thyroid hormones. **A trace element is not an unimportant element.** It is an element required in small amounts.

## Carbon

Carbon deserves separate attention because every other biomolecule is a consequence of its properties.

Carbon has four valence electrons in its outer shell, and it can use all four to form covalent bonds.

| Property | Consequence in biology |
| --- | --- |
| Forms 4 covalent bonds | Builds 3D structures rather than only chains; permits branching |
| C–C bonds are strong | Large stable molecules are possible; energy is required to break them |
| Bonds to itself in chains, branches, and rings | Skeletons for every class of organic molecule |
| Bonds easily to C, H, O, N, S | Functional groups; and crucially, O and N are more electronegative than C, so bonds to them are polar |

That last row is the one to slow down on. Carbon is only weakly electronegative, so it does not strongly attract the shared electrons in a C–O or C–N bond. This leaves those bonds **polar**, and polar bonds are what allow chemistry to be directed rather than random:

```
C=O     carbon δ+ , oxygen δ−     → dipole, water-hydrogen-bonding, partial charges
C–N     nitrogen δ−              → hydrogen bonding, the basis of DNA base pairing
C–H     nearly nonpolar           → allows long hydrophobic chains, e.g. fatty acid tails
```

**Medical relevance:** the polarity of C–O and C–N bonds is the reason molecules have three-dimensional shapes, and three-dimensional shape is what a protein must have to work. When a mutation changes one amino acid, it does not usually damage the cell directly — it changes a shape, and the shape is the function.

### Isotopes

Two atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons are **isotopes**. Chemically they behave almost identically; what differs is nuclear stability and mass.

| Isotope | Used in medicine because |
| --- | --- |
| Carbon-14 | Radioactive; used to date once-living material |
| Cobalt-60 | Gamma-emitting; used in radiotherapy and to sterilize equipment |
| Iodine-131 | Traced through thyroid hormone production to image and treat the thyroid gland |
| Technetium-99m | Short half-life; emits gamma only; the workhorse of diagnostic nuclear imaging |

All isotopes with unstable nuclei are **radioactive** — their nuclei decay spontaneously, emitting radiation. Two terms get mixed up and should not be:

- **Isotope** = atoms of the same element differing in neutron count. Can be stable or radioactive.
- **Radioactivity** = the decay process itself. A property of unstable isotopes.

## Structure of the atom

```
          NUCLEUS (protons + neutrons)
          ↓
     electron shells hold electrons
     ↓
only the VALENCE electrons participate in bonding
```

Electrons fill shells from the inside out, and the number of **valence electrons** in the outermost shell determines chemical behaviour. Carbon has 4; oxygen has 6 and needs 2 more to reach a full shell.

That "reach a full shell" drive is what bonding *is*. Atoms bond because a filled outer shell is a lower-energy arrangement. Share a pair of electrons between two atoms and each one moves closer to a full shell.

## Elements to know beyond the six

| Element | Biological role |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Chloride (Cl) | The ion gradients that drive membrane potentials, nerve impulses, and muscle contraction. See 10 — Nervous |
| Magnesium (Mg) | Cofactor for hundreds of enzymes; required for ATP to be usable |
| Sulfur (S) | Cysteine and methionine in proteins; disulfide bonds that stabilize protein structure |
| Iron (Fe) | Haemoglobin oxygen transport; iron–sulfur clusters in the electron transport chain |
| Iodine (I) | Thyroid hormones, which set metabolic rate |

## Medical relevance

Elements are where clinical nutrition and toxicology actually operate.

**Deficiency vs excess is often the same element with opposite signs.** Iodine deficiency causes hypothyroidism — low metabolic rate, fatigue, impaired development in children. Excess iodine can also disturb thyroid function in the opposite direction. The element is not the problem; the concentration is. This is a recurring pattern.

**Metals poison enzymes by binding them.** Lead inhibits two enzymes in the heme synthesis pathway, which is why it produces anaemia *and* neurological symptoms at the same time. That pattern — one cause, several systems — recurs in heavy metal toxicity, because many metals act on the same ubiquitous chemistry rather than on one particular organ.

**Clinical labs measure elements for concrete reasons.** Electrolyte panels measure Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ because those are what the ion gradients and fluid balance depend on. Serum calcium is measured because it affects nerve and muscle excitability directly. An ion level that is only slightly abnormal can produce a serious arrhythmia, because cardiac electrical activity depends on those gradients.

**Elements in imaging and treatment.** Iodine is used as a contrast agent in CT scanning because it absorbs X-rays strongly. Gadolinium is used in MRI, but by a different mechanism: it is strongly paramagnetic and shortens the relaxation time of nearby hydrogen nuclei, changing local signal intensity. Two imaging agents can exploit the same element for entirely different physical reasons.

## Key facts

- **Atomic number** (protons) defines the element. Neutron count gives the **isotope**. Electron count gives the **ion**. None of the latter two changes the element.
- The six major elements by mass: **O, C, H, N, Ca, P**.
- Carbon forms four covalent bonds, bonds to itself in chains and rings, and makes weak bonds that form **polar** bonds to O and N.
- Polar C–O and C–N bonds are the source of molecular shape, and therefore of protein function.
- **Ion** = charged atom, from gaining or losing electrons. Cation = positive; anion = negative.
- Trace elements are required in small amounts and are not unimportant.
- **Radioactivity** is spontaneous decay of an unstable nucleus; **isotope** is simply a different neutron count.

## Practice questions

**1. A particle has 17 protons, 20 neutrons, and 18 electrons. Which statement about it is correct?**

A. It is a chlorine atom carrying a $2+$ charge
B. It is a neutral atom of chlorine
C. It is an isotope of argon
D. It is a chloride ion, and an atom with the same proton count but fewer neutrons would also be a chloride ion

**Answer: D**

Explanation: 17 protons identifies the element as chlorine — argon would require 18 protons, which rules out C. With 17 protons and 18 electrons the net charge is $17 - 18 = -1$, so the particle is negatively charged: a **chloride ion**, which rules out A and B. And because only the proton count defines the element, changing the neutron count produces a different *isotope* of chloride rather than a different element — which is what the second half of D states.

---

**2. Why is carbon the basis of organic chemistry?**

A. It forms four covalent bonds, bonds to itself in chains and rings, and forms polar bonds to other elements
B. It is the most abundant element in the human body
C. It has the highest atomic mass of any common element
D. It can form ionic bonds with metals

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Oxygen is the most abundant element by mass, not carbon. Carbon's four bonding sites allow 3D and branching structures, its C–C bonds are strong enough to make large stable molecules, and its relatively low electronegativity means bonds to O and N are polar — which is what gives biomolecules their shapes. C and D are false: carbon's mass is modest and carbon forms covalent, not ionic, bonds with metals.

---

**3. Which statement about isotopes is correct?**

A. An isotope has a different number of neutrons from the same element
B. All isotopes are radioactive
C. An isotope has a different number of protons from the same element
D. Isotopes have different chemical properties from each other

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Isotopes differ in neutron count and therefore in mass. B changing the proton count would produce a different element, not an isotope. C is false — ${}^{12}\mathrm{C}$ and ${}^{14}\mathrm{C}$ are both carbon, and only the second is radioactive. D is false — because chemical behaviour depends on electrons, isotopes of the same element behave almost identically chemically, which is why radioactive tracers work.

---

**4. Two atoms have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. They are**

A. Ions of the same element
B. Different compounds
C. Isotopes of the same element
D. Different elements

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Same element by definition, since proton count is fixed. The differing neutron count makes them isotopes. They are not ions, because an ion differs in electron count.

---

**5. A patient has an abnormally low haematocrit. Which element is most directly implicated, and in which pathway?**

A. Sodium — membrane potential generation
B. Iron — haemoglobin synthesis
C. Calcium — coagulation cascade
D. Iodine — thyroid hormone synthesis

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Haemoglobin requires iron at its centre. Low iron impairs haemoglobin production and lowers haematocrit. Iodine deficiency causes hypothyroidism, not anaemia. Calcium affects coagulation and muscle contraction. Sodium affects membrane potentials. Most importantly, trace iron content shows that a tiny mass of an element can be functionally indispensable.

---

**6. Why is the fact that C–O and C–N bonds are polar biologically important?**

A. It prevents molecules from dissolving in water
B. It allows atoms to form ionic bonds with each other
C. It makes molecules heavier
D. It creates partial charges that allow hydrogen bonding and directed molecular shape

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Because carbon is less electronegative than oxygen and nitrogen, the shared electrons are drawn toward those atoms, leaving a dipole. Dipoles drive hydrogen bonding, which is how water interacts with biomolecules, how DNA holds its two strands together, and how proteins fold into specific three-dimensional shapes. B is backwards — polarity generally increases water solubility. C is wrong; polar covalent bonds are not ionic bonds.

---

**7. Why can radioactive isotopes be used as biological tracers?**

A. Radioisotopes react faster than stable isotopes
B. Isotopes of the same element have nearly identical chemical behaviour, so a radioactive one follows the same routes as a stable one but can be detected
C. Radioactive isotopes are always absorbed by cells
D. Radioisotopes can be substituted for other elements in metabolic reactions

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Chemical behaviour depends on electrons, and an isotope has the same electrons. So a labelled atom is handled by enzymes exactly like the unlabelled version, but its decay can be measured. This is the principle behind tracking iodine through the thyroid. D is false — an isotope cannot be substituted for a different element, since the enzyme only recognizes the correct element.

---

**8. An atom loses two electrons. Which statement is correct?**

A. It becomes an isotope
B. It becomes a different element
C. It becomes a cation with a $2+$ charge, and the element is unchanged
D. It becomes an anion with a $2-$ charge

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Losing negatively charged electrons leaves a net positive charge — a cation. Losing electrons does not change the proton count, so the element is unchanged and the particle is not an isotope. B describes the opposite event. D would require a change in proton count.
