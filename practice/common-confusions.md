# Common Confusions

The specific mix-ups behind most wrong answers. These are not knowledge gaps — you know the material and reach for the wrong piece of it. Each entry names the error, explains why it feels right, and gives the correction.

If you got a question wrong and *didn't* know the answer, go back to the chapter. If you knew it and picked the plausible distractor, you are in the right place.

---

## Foundations

### 1. "Homeostasis means the body keeps everything constant"

**Why it feels right:** the word "stasis" suggests no change at all.

**The correction:** homeostasis keeps variables within a **narrow range**. They change constantly — that is what the correcting is for. Blood glucose rises after every meal and is brought back; body temperature varies with the time of day. The defended variable is stable; the process is dynamic.

**Also wrong:** homeostasis is not only about temperature and blood sugar. pH, water balance, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and ion concentrations are all regulated. Every system in [10 — Anatomy and Physiology](../10-anatomy-and-physiology/) exists to serve this.

---

### 2. "Individual organisms adapt"

**Why it feels right:** it is how the word is used in everyday speech, and it is how it was described before Darwin.

**The correction:** **populations evolve; individuals do not.** A bacterium that acquires resistance to an antibiotic is not adapting — a resistant variant already existed and multiplied. An individual that develops a callus does not pass the callus on. See [01 — Characteristics of life](../00-foundations/01-characteristics-of-life.md).

**The exam version:** the question will give you a resistant bacterium and ask what happened. Look for which variant increased in frequency.

---

### 3. "A virus is alive because it reproduces"

**Why it feels right:** reproduction is one of the characteristics of life, and viruses do it.

**The correction:** reproduction is one characteristic, not the definition, and viruses cannot reproduce independently. They lack ribosomes and cannot synthesise protein or generate energy. They reproduce only by handing instructions to a host cell. No cell arises from anything other than a pre-existing cell, and viruses are not cells.

---

### 4. "Metabolism is how the body releases energy"

**Why it feels right:** the word "metabolism" is often used loosely for energy expenditure.

**The correction:** metabolism is **all** chemical reactions in an organism — both directions. **Catabolism** breaks things down and releases energy; **anabolism** builds things up and consumes energy. They are coupled. Eating builds tissue, which is metabolic work.

---

### 5. "Acid = something with hydrogen in it"

**Why it feels right:** every acid does contain hydrogen, so the pattern holds — and fails to discriminate.

**The correction:** what defines an acidic solution is its raised hydrogen ion concentration. Water contains two hydrogen atoms per molecule and is neutral. Proteins are full of hydrogen and are basic at physiological pH. Ask about $[\mathrm{H}^+]$, not the formula.

---

### 6. "pH 8 is twice as basic as pH 7"

**Why it feels right:** it reads like arithmetic.

**The correction:** the scale is logarithmic, so a change of one unit is a **tenfold** change in $[\mathrm{H}^+]$. pH 8 has one tenth the hydrogen ions of pH 7, and pH 6 has ten times as many. This is why a one-unit pH difference is a large difference.

**The other half of the confusion:** pH runs *inversely* to $[\mathrm{H}^+]$. Higher pH means fewer hydrogen ions. So "lower pH" and "more acidic" go together.

---

### 7. "Neutral pH is always 7"

**Why it feels right:** you learn "7" and it sticks.

**The correction:** neutral means $[\mathrm{H}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-]$, and the value where that happens depends on temperature. It is 7.0 at 25 °C and slightly lower at 37 °C. So blood plasma at 7.35–7.45 is very slightly **basic** — which is normal and not a problem. The concept is the equality; the number is temperature-dependent.

---

### 8. "Buffers keep pH exactly constant"

**Why it feels right:** that is what "resists change" sounds like.

**The correction:** a buffer resists and limits, but it is finite. Once either the weak acid or its conjugate base is exhausted, pH moves sharply. This matters clinically: a buffer system under sustained stress eventually fails, and the failure is abrupt.

---

## Chemistry and bonds

### 9. "Water repels nonpolar molecules"

**Why it feels right:** it is the standard simplification, and "hydrophobic" sounds like aversion.

**The correction:** water fails to interact *favourably* with nonpolar molecules — it does not repel them. Exposing a nonpolar surface requires surrounding water molecules to adopt an ordered arrangement, which costs energy. Nonpolar molecules therefore cluster together to minimise the region of ordered water. That is the **hydrophobic effect**, and it is what drives bilayer formation and protein folding. A close paraphrase that is also wrong: "water pushes them away."

---

### 10. "Hydrogen bonds are strong"

**Why it feels right:** they hold DNA together and hold proteins folded, so they must be strong.

**The correction:** a single hydrogen bond is roughly **5% of the strength** of one covalent bond — far too weak to survive thermal motion alone. DNA separates at 70–80 °C while covalent bonds survive hundreds of degrees. What makes hydrogen bonding decisive is the **number** of them acting together.

**Why it still matters:** their strength is not negligible — it is the right order for reversible, specific processes. That is exactly what DNA strand separation, enzyme-substrate binding, and proofreading need. Weakness and importance are compatible.

---

### 11. "Breaking bonds releases energy"

**Why it feels right:** burning fuel feels like breaking bonds releasing heat.

**The correction:** **breaking a bond always requires energy.** Burning wood is exothermic because *forming* the new bonds in CO₂ and H₂O releases more energy than breaking the bonds in cellulose required. This matters for metabolism: catabolism is net exergonic only because new bonds form in the products. See [high-yield distinction #6](high-yield-distinctions.md).

---

### 12. "Ionic bonds are covalent bonds where one atom is greedier"

**Why it feels right:** both involve attraction between atoms, and both involve electrons.

**The correction:** the mechanism is different — electrons are **transferred**, not shared. A transferred electron leaves a **cation** and an **anion**. Covalent bonds share and produce neutral molecules. In water, ionic compounds dissociate entirely; the ions are then separately hydrated and move independently.

---

### 13. "Weak bonds are unimportant because they are weak"

**Why it feels right:** "weak" sounds like "irrelevant."

**The correction:** what is individually weak is collectively decisive — collagen's tensile strength, a protein's folded shape, DNA's double helix. And weakly bound is exactly what *reversible* processes require. Biology uses strong covalent bonds for permanent architecture and weak interactions for everything that must be undone: proofreading, signal transduction, membrane fusion, replication.

---

## Cell biology and biochemistry

### 14. "Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell"

**Why it feels right:** the phrase is correct about the ATP and wrong about everything else.

**The correction:** it tells you the output and none of the mechanism. The mechanism is:

```
NADH/FADH₂ → ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN → PROTON GRADIENT across the inner membrane
→ ATP SYNTHASE → ATP
```

Mitochondria do not simply "make energy." They move electrons, build a proton gradient, and let the flow of protons back through ATP synthase drive ATP synthesis. Learn the chain, not the label. See [03 — Cellular Processes](../03-cellular-processes/).

---

### 15. "Ribosomes make proteins"

**Why it feels right:** it is accurate and completely uninformative.

**The correction:** the mechanism is:

```
mRNA → RIBOSOME reads codons → tRNAs deliver the matching amino acids → peptide bonds form → polypeptide
```

The three components each have a job: the ribosome holds everything in position and catalyses the bond, the mRNA carries the sequence, and the tRNA both decodes the codon and carries its amino acid. "Ribosomes make proteins" would not let you predict what happens if any one of the three failed.

---

### 16. "Smooth ER has no function because it has no ribosomes"

**Why it feels right:** rough ER is defined by its ribosomes, so it seems smooth ER has none of that.

**The correction:** the two ERs are continuous and divide the work differently. Rough ER synthesises and folds secreted, membrane, and lysosomal proteins. **Smooth ER** synthesises lipids and phospholipids, metabolises drugs and toxins via cytochrome P450, stores and releases calcium, and contributes to steroid hormone synthesis. No ribosomes is the reason it *doesn't* do the rough ER's job, not the reason it has none.

---

### 17. "The nucleus controls everything the cell does"

**Why it feels right:** it holds the DNA, and DNA is the instruction manual.

**The correction:** it holds the *instructions*, not the controls. Actual regulation happens at many points: transcription, mRNA processing and stability, translation, protein folding and degradation, and post-translational modification. A cell with a perfect genome but defective protein folding is not functional. See 05 — Gene regulation.

---

### 18. "Denatured means broken down"

**Why it feels right:** denatured protein is visibly changed and often insoluble.

**The correction:** **denaturation** destroys tertiary and secondary structure and **leaves the peptide bonds intact**, which is why it is usually reversible. Breaking peptide bonds is **proteolysis**, and it is not reversible. Boiled collagen becomes gelatin and can be re-formed; collagen hydrolysed into peptides cannot. See [high-yield distinction #8](high-yield-distinctions.md).

---

### 19. "Proteins denature at high temperature and high pH, so denaturation is caused by extreme conditions only"

**Why it feels right:** both extremes do denature proteins.

**The correction:** denaturation can follow **misfolding**, where the sequence is normal but the protein folds wrongly and aggregates — as in prion disease and several neurodegenerative conditions. That is not extreme heat or pH, and it is not reversed by returning the protein to normal conditions. Denaturation and misfolding are separate problems with separate mechanisms.

---

### 20. "A change in pH breaks peptide bonds"

**Why it feels right:** pH changes do damage proteins.

**The correction:** pH changes the **ionisation** of acidic and basic side chains, which changes charge, which changes hydrogen bonding and shape, which changes function. The peptide backbone survives. Peptide bonds need hydrolysis — a protease — to break.

---

### 21. "Nonpolar side chains go to the outside because they're bigger"

**Why it feels right:** size seems like it should matter.

**The correction:** it is about water, not size. Nonpolar groups cannot hydrogen-bond with water, so exposing them requires an energetically unfavourable ordered shell of water molecules. Folding buries them to minimise that. Charged and polar side chains stay on the surface for the same reason in reverse — they interact favourably with water.

---

### 22. "All amino acids are chiral"

**Why it feels right:** all amino acids except glycine have a stereocentre.

**The correction:** **glycine is achiral** — its side chain is a hydrogen, so its central carbon has only three different groups. Glycine is also why collagen can pack three chains into a tight helix: it is small enough to fit. This is a useful exception, because "all amino acids except glycine" is a standard exam item.

---

### 23. "Enzymes make reactions happen that couldn't occur otherwise"

**Why it feels right:** enzymes obviously govern whether chemistry happens.

**The correction:** enzymes only speed up reactions that are **already thermodynamically favourable**. They lower activation energy, not ΔG, and do not change the equilibrium. If a reaction cannot proceed at all, no amount of enzyme helps. Enzyme failure produces slow reactions and accumulating substrates — not reactions that become impossible.

---

### 24. "Adding more substrate always increases reaction rate"

**Why it feels right:** rate rises with substrate across the entire range of a textbook curve in most simple diagrams.

**The correction:** **saturation**. Once every active site is occupied, further substrate changes nothing — the enzyme is running at Vmax. This is why drug and substrate doses have diminishing effects past a point, and it is why an enzyme's capacity is finite.

---

### 25. "Competitive inhibitors lower Vmax"

**Why it feels right:** the inhibition lowers the measured rate, so the maximum looks lower too.

**The correction:** raise the substrate and you can outcompete a competitive inhibitor, so the enzyme **can still reach Vmax**. What increases instead is **Km** — more substrate is needed to reach half the maximum. Only non-competitive inhibition lowers Vmax, because more substrate cannot dislodge an inhibitor bound elsewhere. See [high-yield distinction #9](high-yield-distinctions.md).

---

### 26. "Enzymes are used up in the reaction"

**Why it feels right:** food and substrates are consumed, so why not enzymes?

**The correction:** that is what *makes* them catalysts. An enzyme is returned unchanged at the end of each cycle, so a small quantity can process a large quantity of substrate. Enzymes are consumed only by irreversible degradation.

---

### 27. "Competitive inhibition can be overcome by adding more inhibitor"

**Why it feels right:** the inhibitor is fighting for the site, so more of it should be more effective.

**The correction:** more inhibitor displaces more substrate and **lowers** the rate. Overcoming a *competitive* inhibitor means adding **substrate**. Overcoming a *non-competitive* inhibitor means removing the inhibitor — more substrate never works.

---

## Macromolecules

### 28. "Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids are all polymers"

**Why it feels right:** they are all called macromolecules.

**The correction:** **lipids are not true polymers.** They have no repeating monomer unit and no defined chain length. They are defined by **hydrophobicity**, which is why steroids, waxes, and triglycerides all belong despite looking unrelated. This is also why lipids have the most varied shapes of the four classes.

---

### 29. "DNA is more stable than RNA because it is double-stranded"

**Why it feels right:** two strands feels more robust than one.

**The correction:** the real reason is the **missing 2′-OH group** on deoxyribose. That hydroxyl can attack a neighbouring phosphodiester bond and cause hydrolysis; without it, DNA persists for millions of years. RNA, having the group, degrades within minutes. That difference in chemical stability is exactly what allows DNA to be the archival molecule.

---

### 30. "DNA and RNA differ in that DNA has two strands and RNA has one"

**Why it feels right:** this is usually true, so it gets generalised.

**The correction:** it is a true **consequence**, not the **cause**. The primary differences are chemical — the sugar and one base — and those differences are what permit the single strand to fold, and permit the double helix to form and to store information. RNA does have double-stranded forms in some viruses; DNA can be single-stranded in some viruses. The chemistry is the reliable distinction.

---

### 31. "Uracil is in RNA because it's less stable than thymine"

**Why it feels right:** it's the opposite of the usual "more stable is better" reasoning, which makes it memorable — and backwards.

**The correction:** thymine's role is to make cytosine deamination **detectable**. Cytosine spontaneously converts to uracil; since uracil is not a normal DNA base, its presence in DNA signals damage. Thymine is 5-methyluracil, so it is distinguishable from uracil. The selection is for **error detection**, not stability.

---

### 32. "Nucleotide and nucleoside are the same thing"

**Why it feels right:** both are base-plus-something, and the words rhyme.

**The correction:** nucleotide = base + sugar + **phosphate**. Nucleoside = base + sugar, **no phosphate**. It is functional, not cosmetic: a nucleoside cannot be incorporated into a nucleic acid chain without phosphorylation, and nucleosides are what nucleic acid degradation produces.

---

### 33. "Urine/excretion removes waste from the body, so the kidneys excrete urea"

**Why it feels right:** urea is a waste product, and the kidney makes urine.

**The correction:** the kidneys **filter** urea from the blood into the filtrate, and most of it is passively **reabsorbed** back. Urea is eliminated because a small fraction escapes reabsorption at the end of the nephron, not because the kidney actively secretes it. Active tubular secretion targets substances the body needs to eliminate more efficiently — hydrogen ions, potassium, creatinine, and drugs. This is covered in 10 — Urinary.

## Structure, function, and level of organisation

### 34. "An emergent property belongs to the cell"

**Why it feels right:** the cell is the smallest unit of life, so it seems to be where function begins.

**The correction:** some properties are cellular — metabolism, homeostasis, reproduction. Others need an **arrangement**: the heart pumps blood, the lung does not; oxygen transport is a property of the arrangement, not of any cell. Naming the level at which a function emerges is a required clinical skill, because it determines the specialty involved.

---

### 35. "Blood is a tissue because it circulates"

**Why it feels right:** it flows, and flowing things seem like fluids rather than tissues.

**The correction:** blood **is** a connective tissue — cells (erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets) dispersed in a liquid extracellular matrix called plasma. The defining criterion is a group of cells plus extracellular material performing a shared function, not whether the material is solid. See 09 — Human Biology.

---

### 36. "Arteries carry oxygenated blood; veins carry deoxygenated blood"

**Why it feels right:** in the body it is almost always true, so it becomes the rule.

**The correction:** judge vessels by **direction**, not oxygen content. The **pulmonary artery** carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the **pulmonary vein** carries oxygenated blood back to the heart. Arterial means away from the heart; venous means toward it. See [high-yield distinction #21](high-yield-distinctions.md).

---

### 37. "The cell membrane is a protein layer with lipids stuck to it"

**Why it feels right:** proteins do the work and are the more functionally interesting component.

**The correction:** the membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer** with proteins embedded in it. That arrangement is why it is fluid and self-sealing, and why selective permeability works. The proteins can move laterally within a continuous lipid phase. See 02 — Cell Biology.

---

### 38. "Genotype and phenotype are the same thing for a dominant trait"

**Why it feels right:** dominant traits appear reliably, so they seem fixed by genotype.

**The correction:** phenotype = genotype **plus environment**. Dominance only describes the relationship between two alleles in a heterozygote. The environment can shift a phenotype without any change in genotype — nutrition affecting body mass, sunlight affecting pigmentation, oxygen availability affecting haemoglobin saturation. See [high-yield distinction #12](high-yield-distinctions.md).

---

### 39. "A dominant allele is always more common in a population"

**Why it feels right:** the word "dominant" sounds like "better" and "more."

**The correction:** dominance is a **within-individual** relationship between two alleles; frequency is a **population** property. There is no requirement that the dominant allele be the common one. It is also not a statement about advantage.

---

### 40. "Positive feedback is a stronger form of negative feedback"

**Why it feels right:** "positive" sounds like an intensified version of "negative."

**The correction:** they do opposite things. **Negative** feedback opposes a change and stabilises; it is the basis of homeostasis. **Positive** feedback amplifies a change and takes the system further from where it was; it is used for childbirth, clotting, the action potential, and ovulation. Neither is stronger than the other — they are opposite.

---

## How to use this file

When you get a question wrong, identify which of these patterns produced it. Then:

- **A knowledge gap** — you did not know the fact. Return to the chapter.
- **A confusion** — you knew the fact and reached for the wrong one. This file is for that.
- **A reasoning error** — you understood the principle and applied it wrongly. Redo the question from scratch, slowly, writing out the causal chain.

Only the first is fixed by reading more. The other two are fixed by identifying the specific error, and that is what this file is for.
