# High-Yield Distinctions

Comparisons that appear repeatedly in exams and that students most often get wrong by blending the two categories together. Each entry gives the distinction, the reason it exists, and a note on the mix-up.

---

## 1. Acid vs base

| | Acid | Base |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brønsted–Lowry | Donates a proton ($\mathrm{H}^+$) | Accepts a proton |
| Arrhenius | Raises $[\mathrm{H}^+]$ in water | Raises $[\mathrm{OH}^-]$ in water |
| pH | Below 7 at 25 °C | Above 7 at 25 °C |
| Concrete example | Hydrochloric acid, lactic acid | Sodium hydroxide, ammonia, bicarbonate |

**Why it matters:** a buffer needs a weak acid *and* its conjugate base together. Strong acids dissociate completely and cannot buffer — there is no undissociated reserve to re-form.

**Common mix-up:** calling a substance an acid because it contains hydrogen. Water contains two hydrogen atoms and is neutral. What defines an acidic solution is a raised hydrogen ion concentration, not the presence of the element.

---

## 2. Isotope vs radioisotope vs ion

| Change | Result | Element changed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton count | Different element | **Yes** |
| Neutron count | Different **isotope** | No |
| Electron count | **Ion** (cation if lost, anion if gained) | No |

**Radioactivity** is not a third particle type — it is the spontaneous decay of an unstable nucleus. An isotope may be stable or radioactive.

**Why it matters:** two atoms of the same element with different neutron counts behave identically chemically, which is exactly what makes radioactive tracers work.

---

## 3. Strong vs weak acid (or base)

| | Strong | Weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionisation | Complete | Partial — an equilibrium |
| Equilibrium | Entirely to the products | Both sides populated |
| pK_a | Low | Higher |
| Comparable at equal molarity | No — **strength is not concentration** | — |

**Why it matters:** biological carboxyl and amino groups are weak acids and weak bases. Their ionisation depends on pH, which determines their charge, which determines their function.

**Common mix-up:** confusing strength with concentration. A very dilute solution of a weak acid can have a lower $[\mathrm{H}^+]$ than a concentrated solution of another.

## 4. Covalent vs ionic bond

| | Covalent | Ionic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Electrons **shared** | Electrons **transferred** |
| Particles | Nonmetals | Metal + nonmetal |
| Particles formed | Neutral molecules | Cation and anion |
| Attraction | Shared electron pair | Electrostatic attraction between opposite charges |
| Strength | Very strong | Strong |
| In water | Intact | Dissociate; ions become **hydrated** |

**Why it matters:** in solution, ionic compounds do not really exist as bonds — the ions are separated and each is surrounded by oriented water. This is why ions cross membranes and set up gradients.

**Common mix-up:** calling any electrostatic attraction an ionic bond. The attraction between a nucleus and its own electrons is not an ionic bond; "ionic bond" refers to inter-ion attraction after electron transfer.

---

## 5. Hydrogen bond vs van der Waals interaction

| | Hydrogen bond | van der Waals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | H covalently bonded to O or N, attracted to a nearby O or N | Temporary dipoles in electron clouds |
| Directional | **Yes** | **No** |
| Saturating | Yes | No |
| Sums over | One specific direction | **All atom pairs** |
| Strength | ~20 kJ/mol | ~2–5 kJ/mol |
| Function | Determines molecular **shape** | Packs the **interior** |

**Why it matters:** hydrogen bonds are strong enough to be specific and weak enough to be reversible — which is what DNA strand separation and enzyme binding require. Van der Waals contacts are individually negligible and collectively substantial.

**Common mix-up:** claiming hydrogen bonds are "strong." Individually they are about 5% of a covalent bond. They are numerous.

## 6. Exothermic vs endergonic

| | Exothermic / exergonic | Endergonic / energonic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free energy | Released ($\Delta G < 0$) | Required ($\Delta G > 0$) |
| Spontaneity | Spontaneous | Not spontaneous on its own |
| Needs | — | Coupled to an exergonic reaction |

**Why it matters:** **an enzyme cannot make an endergonic reaction spontaneous.** It lowers activation energy. Cell metabolism works by coupling endergonic reactions to exergonic ones — see 03 — Cellular Processes.

**Common mix-up:** using "exothermic" where "exergonic" is meant. Bond-breaking is always endothermic; net energy release depends on whether forming new bonds releases more than breaking the old ones cost.

## 7. Primary vs secondary vs tertiary vs quaternary structure

| Level | What it is | Stabilised by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | Amino acid **sequence** | Covalent peptide bonds |
| **Secondary** | Local arrangement: α-helix, β-sheet | **Backbone** hydrogen bonds |
| **Tertiary** | 3D fold of one chain | Side chains: hydrophobic effect, H-bonds, ionic, van der Waals, disulfides |
| **Quaternary** | Arrangement of **multiple** chains | Same as tertiary, between chains |

**Why it matters:** denaturation destroys tertiary and secondary structure and leaves primary intact. The classification tells you what stage of damage has occurred.

**Common mix-up:** assuming side chains form α-helices. Side chains do not participate in secondary structure at all.

---

## 8. Denaturation vs proteolysis vs misfolding

| | Denaturation | Proteolysis | Misfolding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | Existing structure unfolds | Peptide bonds are broken | Folding goes wrong from the start |
| Primary structure | **Intact** | Destroyed | Intact — the sequence is normal |
| Cause | Heat, pH, solvents, metals | Protease enzymes | Defect in the sequence or in folding control |
| Reversible | Usually | No | Usually not |
| Example | Boiled egg white | Pepsin digesting a meal | Aggregates in prion disease |

**Why it matters:** the three produce different clinical pictures, and the therapeutic implications differ — denaturation is often recoverable, proteolysis is not.

**Common mix-up:** using "denatured" to mean "broken down." Collagen denatured becomes gelatin and can be re-formed; collagen hydrolysed becomes absorbable peptides and cannot.

---

## 9. Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition

| | Competitive | Non-competitive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binds | **Active site** | **Allosteric site** |
| Competes with substrate | **Yes** | No |
| Overcome by more substrate | **Yes** | **No** |
| Vmax | **Unchanged** | **Decreased** |
| Km | **Increases** | Unchanged |

**Why it matters:** this is the mechanism behind a large fraction of drug classes. Competitive inhibition can be outcompeted and depends on relative concentrations; non-competitive cannot.

**Common mix-up:** expecting Km to change in both. If you are unsure which parameter moves, work out which one would let the enzyme still reach its maximum — that one is unchanged.

---

## 10. Osmotic effects: osmosis, tonicity, water potential

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Osmosis** | Diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane |
| **Hypertonic** | Solution with a **higher non-penetrating solute** concentration — water leaves the cell, which shrinks |
| **Hypotonic** | Lower non-penetrating solute concentration — water enters the cell, which swells |
| **Isotonic** | Equal non-penetrating solute concentration — no net movement |
| **Tonicity** | Effect on **cell volume**; depends on non-penetrating solutes only |

**Why it matters:** tonicity predicts what happens to cell volume; osmolarity is a total concentration measure. They differ when a solute can cross the membrane.

**Common mix-up:** treating osmolarity and tonicity as synonyms. They are not, and the difference is exactly what makes a non-penetrating solute necessary for the definition.

---

## 11. Passive vs active transport

| | Passive | Active |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy (ATP) | Not required | Required directly or indirectly |
| Direction | Down the gradient | **Against** the gradient |
| Requires a protein | Sometimes — facilitated diffusion uses channels or carriers | Always — a pump or cotransporter |
| Examples | Simple diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion | Na⁺/K⁺ pump, Ca²⁺ pump, glucose–Na⁺ cotransport |

**Why it matters:** glucose enters some cells by facilitated diffusion and others by secondary active transport. The gradient that powers the secondary case is itself maintained by ATP — so it is **not** passive, even though no ATP is spent at the transporter.

**Common mix-up:** calling secondary active transport passive because ATP is not consumed at that membrane. The energy was spent building the gradient, elsewhere.

## 12. Genotype vs phenotype

| | Genotype | Phenotype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | The allele combination | The **observable** trait |
| When it exists | In principle from conception | Only when expressed |
| What determines it | Inheritance | Genotype **plus environment** |

**Why it matters:** identical genotypes can produce different phenotypes if the environment differs. This is why a recessive trait may never appear in a genotype that carries it.

**Common mix-up:** using them interchangeably, especially for dominant and recessive — those describe the *relationship* between alleles, not the trait itself.

---

## 13. Dominant vs recessive

| | Dominant | Recessive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expression in a heterozygote | Expressed | Masked |
| Requires | One copy | Two copies |
| Typical gene function | Often produces a functional product | Often produces a defective product |

**Why it matters:** the reason recessive traits tend to produce functional defects is that a single working copy often makes enough product. So "recessive" predicts a mechanism.

**Common mix-up:** assuming dominant traits are always more common or always beneficial. Neither follows.

---

## 14. Incomplete dominance vs codominance

| | Incomplete dominance | Codominance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heterozygote phenotype | **Intermediate** | **Both** alleles expressed |
| Example | Red × white snapdragon → pink | AB blood type |
| Alleles | Blended | Independently detectable |

**Why it matters:** these are different phenomena and the distinction is always examinable. Codominance produces a third, distinguishable phenotype; incomplete dominance produces one that looks halfway.

## 15. DNA vs RNA

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose (no 2′-OH) | Ribose (2′-OH) |
| Pyrimidines | Cytosine, **thymine** | Cytosine, **uracil** |
| Strands | Two, antiparallel, complementary | Usually one |
| Location (eukaryote) | Nucleus | Nucleus and cytoplasm |
| Function | Long-term storage | Transfer, expression, regulation, catalysis |
| Stability | High | Lower |
| Replicates | Yes | No |
| Typical length | Very long | Short |

**Why it matters:** every difference follows from the missing 2′-OH — more stable sugar chemistry, different helical geometry, and single-stranded folding for catalysis and recognition.

**Common mix-up:** the 3′/5′ direction. Each strand has a 5′ and a 3′ end, the two strands run in opposite directions, and synthesis only ever proceeds toward a 3′ end.

---

## 16. Nucleotide vs nucleoside

| | Nucleotide | Nucleoside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contains | Base + sugar + **phosphate** | Base + sugar |
| Can be polymerised | Yes | No — needs phosphorylation first |
| Also called | — | Produced by nucleic acid breakdown |

**Why it matters:** ATP is a nucleotide; nucleosides are the products of degradation. It is a genuine functional distinction, not a naming convention.

---

## 17. Prokaryote vs eukaryote

| Feature | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleus | No membrane-bound nucleus | Membrane-bound nucleus |
| DNA | Usually a single circular chromosome | Linear chromosomes |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytosol; 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts |
| Size | 0.1–5 μm | 10–100 μm |
| Cells per organism | Usually unicellular | Unicellular or multicellular |
| Example | *E. coli* | Yeast; a human cell |

**Why it matters:** this distinction is the basis of antibacterial selectivity — most antibiotics target structures bacteria have and human cells do not.

**Common mix-up:** treating the prokaryote/eukaryote line as a ladder of complexity. Both are successful solutions to different problems, and both have been evolving for the same length of time.

---

## 18. Plant cell vs animal cell

| Feature | Plant | Animal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell wall | Cellulose | **None** |
| Large central vacuole | Yes, usually large | Small or absent |
| Chloroplasts | In photosynthetic cells | **Absent** |
| Storage carbohydrate | **Starch** | **Glycogen** |
| Structural carbohydrate | **Cellulose** | **Chitin** in external skeletons only |
| Shape | Regular, fixed by the wall | Irregular |

**Why it matters:** the storage carbohydrate difference explains why humans cannot digest cellulose — no human enzyme cleaves β(1→4) bonds — and it is also why plant storage starch and animal storage glycogen differ in branching despite sharing the same linkage.

**Common mix-up:** attributing a plant's rigidity to glycogen, or to turgor pressure alone. It is the cellulose wall, held rigid by turgor pressure acting against it.

---

## 19. Diffusion vs osmosis

| | Diffusion | Osmosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What moves | Any substance | **Water** |
| Requirement | None | A **selectively permeable** membrane |
| Down a gradient | Yes | Yes — from lower to higher solute concentration |

**Why it matters:** osmosis is a specific case of diffusion. Diffusion needs no membrane; osmosis does.

**Common mix-up:** describing water moving "down its own concentration gradient" without saying it moves **toward the higher solute concentration**. Water's gradient is the inverse of the solute gradient, and this inversion is why osmosis swells a cell in hypotonic solution.

---

## 20. Mitosis vs meiosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Divisions | One | **Two** |
| Daughter cells | **2** | **4** |
| Chromosome number | Same as parent (2n → 2n) | **Halved** (2n → n) |
| Genetic identity | Genetically identical to parent | Genetically **different** |
| Crossing over | **No** | **Yes**, in prophase I |
| Independent assortment | No | **Yes** |
| Occurs in | All eukaryotic cells for growth and repair | Germ cells only, for gamete production |
| Purpose | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Producing gametes for sexual reproduction |

**Why it matters:** meiosis is what makes offspring genetically different from parents and from each other. Its two contributions are **recombination** and **independent assortment**.

**Common mix-up:** that meiosis produces four cells from one — true — but the defining feature is **halving the chromosome number**, not the cell count.

## 21. Artery vs vein vs capillary

| Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries blood | Away from the heart | Toward the heart | Between them |
| Thick wall | **Yes** — withstands pressure | Thin | One cell thick |
| Lumen | Narrow, round, no valves | Wide, collapsed, **valves** | Narrowest |
| Blood flow | Pulsatile | Continuous | Slowest — maximises exchange area |
| Valves | Absent | **Present** | Absent |

**Why it matters:** wall thickness and valving follow directly from the job — pressure resistance versus return against gravity.

**Common mix-up:** arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated. That is true **only for the systemic circuit**; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood. Judge them by direction, not oxygen content.

## 22. Innate vs adaptive immunity

| Feature | Innate | Adaptive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Immediate | Days — requires clonal expansion |
| Specificity | Recognises broad, shared patterns | Recognises **specific** antigens |
| Memory | **No** | **Yes** |
| Cells | Neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells | B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes |
| Molecules | Complement, acute phase proteins, cytokines | Antibodies, T cell receptors |
| Second response | Same as first | Faster and stronger |

**Why it matters:** memory is the whole basis of vaccination — the primary response establishes memory cells, so the secondary response is faster and greater.

**Common mix-up:** treating innate immunity as unimportant because it is nonspecific. It is the first line and the one that decides whether an adaptive response is even needed.

---

## 23. Catabolic vs anabolic

| | Catabolic | Anabolic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | Complex → simple | Simple → complex |
| Energy | **Released** | **Required** |
| Main purpose | Make ATP | Build molecules |
| Coupled to | Oxidations | Reductions |
| Examples | Glycolysis, β-oxidation | Protein synthesis, gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis |

**Why it matters:** they are coupled. The energy released by catabolism funds anabolism — which is why an organism must keep breaking things down in order to keep building.

**Common mix-up:** using "metabolism" for one of the two halves. Metabolism is both.

## 25. Solution vs suspension vs colloid

| | Solution | Colloid | Suspension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Molecules or ions, < 1 nm | 1–1000 nm | > 1000 nm |
| Settles on standing | No | No | **Yes** |
| Tyndall effect | No | **Yes** | Yes |
| Filters through | Ordinary paper | Partly | No |
| Example | Salt in water | Starch in water, milk | Blood cells in water |

**Why it matters:** it determines whether a mixture needs filtering, whether it settles, and whether it scatters light — the basis of several laboratory tests.

## 26. Exocytosis vs endocytosis

| | Endocytosis | Exocytosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | Into the cell | Out of the cell |
| Vesicle forms | Membrane invaginates and pinches off inward | Vesicle fuses with the membrane | 
| Bulk transport | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Requires ATP | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Used for | Large particles, nutrients, pathogens | Hormones, enzymes, neurotransmitters |

**Why it matters:** both move material that cannot cross the membrane by diffusion or transport proteins.

**Common mix-up:** including passive transport as endocytosis. Endocytosis requires energy because membrane rearrangement is work.

## 27. Structure and function: the pattern to recognise everywhere

Whenever a structure is described, the function follows from the geometry or the chemistry — not the other way round.

| Structure | Feature | Function that follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artery wall | Thick, elastic | Withstands and smooths high pulsatile pressure |
| Capillary wall | One cell thick | Minimises diffusion distance |
| Alveolus | Very large surface area, thin | Maximises gas exchange rate |
| Proximal tubule | Microvilli → brush border | Maximises reabsorption area |
| Small intestine | Villi and microvilli | Maximises absorptive surface |
| Saturated fatty acid | No kinks | Tight packing, high melting point, fluid membrane at low temperature |
| Cis double bond | Produces a kink | Loose packing, lower melting point |
| Muscle spindle | Arranged in parallel with muscle fibres | Detects **length** |
| Golgi tendon organ | Arranged **in series** | Detects **tension** |

**Why it matters:** being able to derive function from structure is what makes the anatomy chapters predictable rather than a list to memorise.
