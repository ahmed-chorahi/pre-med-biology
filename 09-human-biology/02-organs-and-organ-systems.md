# Organs and Organ Systems

## Why it matters

This chapter is the map for section 10: [10 — Anatomy & Physiology](../10-anatomy-and-physiology/) gives each organ system its own chapter, and every one of them assumes two definitions fixed here — an **organ** is a recurring recipe of the four tissue types, and an **organ system** is a set of organs sharing one physiological problem.

The recipe claim is literal. Oesophagus, stomach, intestine, ureter, bladder and airway are built from the same layers in the same order — **epithelium** facing the lumen, **connective tissue** beneath it, **muscle**, **nerve plexuses** — with only the proportions changed. Position follows function, so pathology follows position: ulcers are graded by depth, and a perforation is an emergency because of which layer was breached. The ingredients are introduced in [01 — The four tissue types](01-four-tissue-types.md).

Two threads run forward from [02 — Biological organization](../00-foundations/02-biological-organization.md): cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form systems. And because systems are defined by a shared *problem*, none works alone — the heart cannot pump oxygen it does not have, and the kidney cleans what the gut and lungs disturbed. Control runs through receptor → control centre → effector → response ([09 — Homeostasis](../00-foundations/09-homeostasis.md), [03 — Homeostasis — the full control loop](03-homeostasis-control-loop.md)).

## What counts as an organ

Cells of one kind form a **tissue** — similar cells plus their extracellular material doing one job ([01 — Cell theory and cell types](../02-cell-biology/01-cell-theory-and-cell-types.md)). An **organ** is a recognisably shaped mass in which **at least two tissue types are arranged so that a function emerges that none could perform alone** — the heart needs cardiac muscle for force, endocardium for a smooth lining, connective tissue for its valves, and nervous tissue for its timing. An **organ system** is a set of organs that together solve **one physiological problem**. Failures are read at whichever rung they occur: channel → epithelium → mucus → infection → systemic illness.

Borderline cases are settled by five criteria; no single one is sufficient:

| Criterion | What it means | Example or exception |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Distinct vital function** | An overriding physiological job | The kidney filters plasma; a blood vessel only conducts |
| **Recognisable architecture** | Dissectable shape with internal organisation | Renal cortex and medulla; hepatic lobules |
| **Encapsulation** | A connective-tissue capsule defining its edge | Kidney, liver, spleen; **brain and spinal cord** have meninges instead |
| **Dedicated blood supply** | Named vessels, so ischaemia has a predictable territory | Renal artery, coronary arteries |
| **Named innervation** | Specific nerves or intrinsic plexuses | Vagus to the gut, myenteric plexus in its wall |

Organs are either hollow — a lumen plus the layered wall described next — or solid, parenchyma threaded with a connective-tissue stroma; the distinction decides whether they fail by obstruction and perforation or by infarction and mass lesion, and whether they are examined by endoscopy or by biopsy.

## The organ recipe: the same four layers

Essentially every hollow organ uses the same plan, and **each layer sits where it does because of what it must touch.** Epithelium faces the material being handled; connective tissue carries the blood supply and gives strength; muscle surrounds the lumen so it can squeeze; nerve plexuses lie inside the submucosa and between the muscle layers so they can coordinate secretion and motion locally.

### Worked example 1 — the gut wall

| Layer (lumen outward) | Tissue composition | Job | Why it is *here* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mucosa**: epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae | Simple columnar epithelium with absorptive cells and goblet cells; loose connective tissue with capillaries and lacteals; thin muscle sheet | Secretion, digestion, absorption, immune sampling | It must **touch the lumen** and stay **one cell thick** so nutrients cross a minimal diffusion distance ([02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md)); the lamina propria collects what crosses |
| **Submucosa** | Dense irregular connective tissue with large vessels, lymphatics, and the **submucosal (Meissner's) plexus** | Strength; blood supply; local secretion control | **Between lining and motor layer**, so one vascular bed serves both and nerves reach epithelium and muscle without crossing muscle |
| **Muscularis externa**: inner circular, outer longitudinal, **myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus** between | Two smooth-muscle layers | **Peristalsis** and segmentation | Layers at right angles turn contraction into *directional* movement; the plexus between them sequences the wave |
| **Serosa** (or **adventitia** where no cavity abuts) | Mesothelium over thin connective tissue | Low friction; anchoring | Outermost because it must **slide against or attach to** neighbours — the oesophagus attaches instead |

```
LUMEN (chyme, digestate)
   ↓ contact
MUCOSA — epithelium digests and absorbs; lamina propria capillaries collect the products
   ↓ plumbing and coordination
SUBMUCOSA — vessels feed both neighbours; Meissner's plexus adjusts secretion and flow
   ↓ motor
MUSCULARIS EXTERNA — inner circular layer NARROWS the lumen,
                     outer longitudinal layer SHORTENS the segment
   ↓ sequenced by the myenteric plexus
PERISTALSIS propels contents; SEGMENTATION mixes them for absorption
   ↓ outermost
SEROSA lets the loop slide against its neighbours without abrasion
```

The layer order predicts symptom and spread: mucosal disease bleeds into the lumen, muscular disease obstructs without bleeding, serosal disease causes peritonitis. An ulcer that stops in the mucosa heals; one that breaches the serosa empties gut contents into a sterile cavity.

### Worked example 2 — skin: the organ with no lumen

Skin faces the environment rather than a lumen, yet uses the same four ingredients specialised against abrasion, desiccation, ultraviolet light and microbes.

| Layer | Tissue composition | Job | Why it is *here* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Epidermis** | **Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium**; melanocytes in the basal layer | Abrasion-resistant, nearly waterproof barrier; absorbs ultraviolet radiation; renews from below | It must **face the world**; stratification lets cells shed from the top without exposure, and keratin plus lipids prevent water loss |
| **Dermis** | **Dense irregular connective tissue** — collagen woven in many directions, carrying vessels, nerves, glands, follicles | Multi-directional tensile strength; anchors the epidermis; vascular and sensory bed | Beneath the epithelium because it must **hold it against shear in any direction**, and because avascular epithelium is fed by diffusion |
| **Muscle** | **Arrector pili** smooth muscle on hair follicles | Raises hairs, expresses sebum, conserves heat | Embedded in the dermis so it can act on follicles |
| **Nerves** | Meissner's and Pacinian corpuscles, free endings; autonomic fibres to arterioles | Touch, pressure, temperature, pain; reflex control of blood flow | Distributed through the dermis so the whole surface reports inward |

```
ABRASION, UV, MICROBES, HEAT at the surface
   ↓
KERATINISED STRATIFIED SQUAMOUS EPITHELIUM — dead, keratin-filled layers shed:
   barrier first, because the interior must not be exposed
   ↓
DENSE IRREGULAR DERMIS — collagen in all directions resists stretch whichever
   way it arrives; vessels and nerves run here
   ↓
ARRECTOR PILI + CUTANEOUS ARTERIOLES — smooth muscle raises hairs and
   redirects heat loss; sensory nerves report touch, temperature and pain
   ↓
INTEGRATION → behaviour and autonomic reflexes
```

All four tissue types are present, with the epithelium keratinised and the connective tissue dense and irregular precisely because the job is protection rather than absorption. Skin is the body's largest organ, and the subcutaneous fat beneath it (hypodermis) is padding, not part of the skin proper.

Variations keep the logic: the heart adds connective-tissue valves and a pacemaker of nervous tissue, the stomach adds an inner oblique layer for churning, the bladder uses stretchable transitional epithelium, and the brain has meninges rather than a capsule.

## The organ systems

An **organ system** is defined by the problem it solves, which is why boundaries are physiological rather than anatomical.

| System | Principal organs | Primary problem it solves | Covered in section 10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Skeletal** | Bones, cartilage, joints, ligaments, marrow | Frame that bears load against gravity; protects brain, cord, heart and lungs; mineral reservoir; haematopoiesis | Skeletal |
| **Muscular** | Skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle, tendons | Converts ATP into force — posture, locomotion, movement of blood, air and food | Muscular |
| **Nervous** | Brain, spinal cord, nerves, sense organs | Rapid electrochemical integration: detect, decide, command — in milliseconds | Nervous |
| **Endocrine** | Hypothalamus, pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, islets, gonads | Slow chemical control of long-term set points: growth, metabolism, reproduction | Endocrine |
| **Cardiovascular** | Heart, arteries, capillaries, veins | **Bulk transport** of oxygen, nutrients, hormones, heat, immune cells and wastes | Cardiovascular |
| **Respiratory** | Lungs, bronchial tree, trachea, diaphragm | **Gas exchange** — oxygen in, carbon dioxide out — plus acid–base control | Respiratory |
| **Digestive** | Mouth, stomach, intestine, liver, gallbladder, pancreas | Reduce food to absorbable molecules, kill swallowed microbes, recover water | Digestive |
| **Urinary** | Kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra | **Selective excretion** of wastes, water and electrolytes; acid–base control | Urinary |
| **Immune / lymphatic** | Lymph nodes, spleen, thymus, lymphatics, diffuse lymphoid tissue | Distinguish self from non-self; destroy pathogens; drain interstitial fluid | Immune |
| **Reproductive** | Gonads, ducts, glands, uterus, placenta | Produce gametes, achieve fertilisation, support foetal development | Reproductive |
| **Integumentary** | Skin, hair, nails, sweat and sebaceous glands | Outer barrier, thermoregulation, sensation, vitamin D | The one system **folded into the tissue and skin coverage** |

Section 10 treats each of these systems in turn, chapter by chapter — see [10 — Anatomy & Physiology](../10-anatomy-and-physiology/).

An organism is an open system exchanging matter and energy with its environment, exactly as in [12 — Ecology](../12-ecology/), and only four systems do that exchanging: respiratory (gas), digestive (food and water), urinary (waste and water), integumentary (surface and heat). The rest support them — cardiovascular transports, nervous and endocrine control, skeletal and muscular anchor the exchange surfaces, immune defends, reproductive repeats the arrangement.

## Why systems are never truly separate

The box diagram is a bookkeeping device: physiologically every system is embedded in every other, and what kills patients is usually failure of a relationship rather than of one box.

### The two control arms

The **nervous system** and the **endocrine system** are the two control arms, and **both act on every other system** — they differ in speed and specificity, not in reach.

| Property | Nervous | Endocrine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Signal** | Impulse, then neurotransmitter at a synapse | Hormone carried in blood |
| **Speed / reach** | Milliseconds, brief, targeted to defined cells | Seconds to hours, sustained, broadcast to any cell with the receptor |

```
STIMULUS → RECEPTOR → CONTROL CENTRE
                 ╲          ╱
     milliseconds  ╲        ╱  seconds to hours
                    ▼      ▼
          NERVOUS SYSTEM  ENDOCRINE SYSTEM
                    ╲      ╱
                     ▼    ▼
    BOTH ACT ON: muscle · gut wall · kidney tubule · vessel wall
                 · airway · bone · immune cells
                     ▼
          EFFECTOR RESPONSE → set point restored
```

The same target usually receives both: the heart by sympathetic nerves *and* adrenaline, gut motility by the vagus *and* gastrin, blood glucose by nerves *and* by insulin.

### Digestion and respiration both depend on circulation

Absorption without transport is not nutrition:

```
NUTRIENT IN GUT LUMEN → DIGESTED to glucose, amino acids, fatty acids
   ↓ absorbed across ONE cell layer (diffusion, channels, carriers)
LAMINA PROPRIA CAPILLARIES → hepatic portal vein → liver first-pass
   ↓
CARDIOVASCULAR DISTRIBUTION → every tissue receives fuel, removes waste
```

Cut the supply and the wall itself fails: a blocked mesenteric artery starves the gut, and a failing heart empties it of blood so the mucosal barrier breaks. Total parenteral nutrition drips nutrients into veins instead — absorption without transport does not feed a patient.

Likewise, the lung exists to load oxygen onto blood the heart can move:

```
OXYGEN IN ALVEOLUS → diffusion across a ~0.5 µm respiratory membrane
   ↓
PULMONARY CAPILLARY → binds haemoglobin → LEFT HEART pumps it onward
   ↓
SYSTEMIC CAPILLARY → oxygen diffuses into cells; CO₂ returns to be exhaled
```

Either half alone is useless: anaemia gives plenty of alveolar oxygen with too few carriers, while heart failure moves carriers poorly however well the lungs work.

### The urinary system corrects what the others disturb

The gut takes in a variable acid and electrolyte load with every meal, and the lung exhales CO₂, an acid anhydride in blood — so both **disturb the variables the body must hold constant**.

```
DIGESTIVE INPUT (acid/base, Na+, K+, water)  +  RESPIRATORY OUTPUT (CO₂)
        ↓ both perturb blood pH, volume, osmolality
KIDNEY: filtration → selective REABSORPTION of water, glucose, HCO3-, Na+
                  → selective SECRETION/EXCRETION of H+, K+, urea, drugs
        ↓
pH, volume, osmolality restored to set point
```

So an anxious hyperventilator blows off CO₂ and the kidney excretes bicarbonate over a day, while chronic lung disease makes the kidney retain bicarbonate ([09 — Homeostasis](../00-foundations/09-homeostasis.md)). Respiration is the fast lever on pH, the kidney the slow one.

### The immune system patrols all of them

Immune tissue is not confined to lymph nodes: skin carries dendritic cells, the gut carries gut-associated lymphoid tissue and secretory IgA, the airway has mucus and alveolar macrophages, blood carries leucocytes that exit wherever infection starts, and the liver's Kupffer cells filter portal blood. When containment fails, infection becomes a systemic inflammatory response with organ dysfunction — a disease of every system at once ([06 — Host–pathogen interaction](../07-microbiology/06-host-pathogen-interaction.md)).

### Organs that belong to several systems at once

| Organ | Systems | Why the classification splits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Liver** | Digestive, cardiovascular, immune, endocrine | Processes nutrients, handles portal blood, filters microbes, signals hormonally |
| **Pancreas** | Digestive, endocrine | Enzymes via a duct; insulin via capillaries — two delivery routes |
| **Kidney** | Urinary, endocrine, cardiovascular | Filters blood, then sets cardiovascular variables through renin and volume |

## Structure predicts function at the organ level

At tissue level, function follows composition. At organ level it follows **geometry** — area, flow, and barrier thickness.

### Surface area: absorption scales with area

Absorption is a flux across a surface, so it obeys the arithmetic of passive transport:

```
FLUX ∝ (SURFACE AREA × CONCENTRATION GRADIENT) ÷ BARRIER THICKNESS
        ↓
two moves: INCREASE AREA, DECREASE THICKNESS, or both
gut:   folds × villi × microvilli raise area; thickness = ONE cell
lung:  hundreds of millions of alveoli raise area; thickness ≈ 0.5 µm
```

Because the intestinal amplifications **multiply rather than add**, the gain compounds:

| Feature | Gain | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Smooth tube (~6 m long, ~6 cm across) | ×1, about 1 m² | Starting geometry |
| Circular folds (plicae circulares) | ×3 | Ridges that distension cannot flatten |
| Villi | ×10 | Mucosal projections, each with its own capillary loop |
| Microvilli (brush border) | ×20 | Membrane extensions carrying disaccharidases and carriers |
| **Compounded** | **several hundred-fold** | Estimates of functional area span roughly 30–250 m² — the mechanism, not the figure, is the point |

The lung repeats the trick: ~300–500 million alveoli of about 0.2 mm radius give roughly 70–100 m² of gas-exchange surface, folded into the chest because a flat sheet that size would not fit; intestinal loops turn length into area inside a small cavity. The examinable insight is arithmetic — **rate scales with area**, so coeliac disease flattens villi and causes malabsorption without removing tissue, and ileal resection causes deficiency once enough area is lost.

### Flow: branching trees deliver and collect

| Tree | Trunk → finest branches | What happens at the finest level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Airway** | Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveolar ducts (~23 generations) | Gas exchange; mucus and cilia move debris outward |
| **Arterial** | Aorta → arteries → arterioles → capillaries | Exchange; cross-sectional area rises, so flow slows and diffusion gains time |
| **Renal** | Renal artery → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → peritubular capillaries | Filtration first, then reabsorption and secretion |
| **Biliary** | Ducts → canaliculi (ran backwards) | Secretion into a lumen rather than delivery to tissue |

Wide trunks branch into narrow ones so wall material and pumping cost stay low while coverage stays total, and **exchange happens only where branches are finest**. A system with no branching — a single duct such as the ureter — must move material by muscular propulsion instead, which is what ureteric peristalsis does.

### Barrier: selective walls

| Barrier | Construction | Selects against | Clinical echo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Skin** | Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium with surface lipids | Water loss, abrasion, microbes | Burns destroy it → fluid loss and infection |
| **Gut lining** | One-cell epithelium with tight junctions, mucus and IgA | Luminal enzymes and microbes | Inflamed junctions → bacterial translocation |
| **Blood–brain barrier** | Endothelial tight junctions, no pinocytosis, astrocyte end-feet | Charged and polar molecules, most antibiotics | Explains hard-to-treat central nervous system infections and sterile CSF |
| **Respiratory membrane** | Fused alveolar and capillary basement membranes, ~0.5 µm thick | Nothing — minimised for diffusion | Fibrosis thickens it → gas exchange fails |

The tension is explicit: **the thinner the barrier, the better it transports and the more easily it fails** — which is why the organs that exchange most efficiently break down first under stress.

## Medical relevance

**Symptom localisation is organ-system reasoning at the bedside.** Examination proceeds system by system because systems are the natural units of dysfunction: chest pain is cardiovascular first, then respiratory, then gastrointestinal — left-arm radiation with sweating suggests ischaemia, pleuritic pain with unilateral breath sounds suggests pulmonary, relation to meals suggests oesophageal. Visceral pain is vague because gut afferents converge on the same spinal segments as somatic structures, which is why cardiac ischaemia is felt in the arm and jaw. Markers follow the same logic: troponin to myocardium, creatinine to kidney, lipase to pancreas.

**Sepsis is the demonstration that systems fail together.** Infection provokes a systemic inflammatory response with organ dysfunction, and multi-organ failure is the terminal common pathway: cytokine-driven vasodilation and capillary leak lower blood pressure, poorly perfused kidneys sustain acute injury, the ischaemic gut mucosa breaches and translocates bacteria that feed the loop, the liver stops making clotting factors, the lungs stiffen, and disseminated intravascular coagulation consumes platelets while infarcting tissue. The cascade is set out in [06 — Host–pathogen interaction](../07-microbiology/06-host-pathogen-interaction.md); its clinical scoring is an organ-by-organ ledger, because poor perfusion injures cells, injury releases more inflammatory signal, and the next system fails in turn.

**Transplantation exposes all three layers of the recipe at once.** A graft must succeed at the **vascular** level (artery and vein sewn to the recipient's; minutes of ischaemia decide how much tissue survives reperfusion), at the **parenchymal** level (nephrons, hepatocytes or cardiomyocytes must remain viable and resume their barrier and transport roles), and at the **immune** level (without HLA matching and immunosuppression the recipient's lymphocytes destroy a graft bearing foreign antigens — hyperacute in minutes, acute in days, chronic over years). A skin graft survives initially by diffusion because it is a thin sheet; a kidney is useless until blood flow is restored.

**Redundancy and single points of failure are distributed unevenly by design.** Two kidneys, two lungs, two ureters, paired vessels and hepatic lobes mean one can be lost while the problem is still solved — a kidney donor loses half their renal mass and the remainder hypertrophies to carry the load. Other structures have no partner: one heart, one spinal cord, one brain, one trachea, one common bile duct. So nephrectomy is survivable, occlusion of a single coronary artery kills a territory of myocardium, and transection of the spinal cord is permanent.

**Reading a wall tells you which disease you have.** Because hollow organs share their layers, pathology is named by depth: gastritis is mucosal, a penetrating ulcer reaches the muscularis and may scar into obstruction, and a perforated ulcer breaches the serosa — chemical peritonitis first, infectious emergency once luminal bacteria enter. Glomerular disease is detected as protein in the urine rather than pain, because the layer that fails dictates both the symptom and the test.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| A tissue and an organ are the same thing at different magnifications | A **tissue** is one cell type plus matrix doing one job; an **organ** integrates **at least two tissue types** so a new function emerges — pumping, peristalsis, filtration. |
| An organ system is just a list of organs | It is defined by **one shared physiological problem**, not proximity — which is why liver, pancreas and skin each belong to several systems. |
| Serosa and adventitia are different tissues | Same job, different setting: **serosa** has a mesothelial surface and lubricating fluid and faces a cavity; **adventitia** anchors the organ to its neighbours. |
| Mucosa and submucosa are interchangeable | **Mucosa** faces the lumen (epithelium + lamina propria + muscularis mucosae); **submucosa** is the scaffolding beneath, carrying larger vessels and Meissner's plexus. |
| The hypodermis is part of the skin | Skin proper is **epidermis + dermis**; the hypodermis is adipose and loose connective tissue beneath it. |
| Epithelium is only found on the outside of the body | It lines every hollow organ, vessel, alveolus and kidney tubule — and **every epithelium is avascular**, fed by diffusion from the connective tissue below. |
| Nervous tissue exists only in brain and spinal cord | The **enteric nervous system** holds ~500 million neurons in the gut wall and can drive peristalsis alone, which is why a severed spinal cord does not stop gut motility. |
| Organ systems operate independently | They are coupled: nervous and endocrine control everything; absorption needs transport; the kidney corrects what lung and gut disturb; immune tissue patrols all of them. |
| The immune system is just lymph nodes and spleen | Most defence is **diffuse** — mucus and IgA, alveolar macrophages, skin dendritic cells, gut-associated lymphoid tissue, Kupffer cells; organs are only assembly points. |

## Key facts

- An **organ** integrates **at least two tissue types** into one structure with one overriding job; an **organ system** is a set of organs solving **one shared physiological problem**.
- Borderline organs are settled by five criteria: **vital function, architecture, encapsulation, dedicated blood supply, named innervation**.
- **Hollow organs share one recipe**: mucosa → submucosa → muscularis externa (**inner circular, outer longitudinal**) with myenteric plexus between → serosa or adventitia.
- Layer position follows function — epithelium **faces the lumen**, connective tissue **supplies**, muscle **moves**, nerves **coordinate** — so the depth of a lesion predicts the symptom and the spread.
- **Skin uses the same four ingredients**: keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, dense irregular dermis, arrector pili smooth muscle, sensory and autonomic nerves; the hypodermis is not part of it.
- Only **four systems exchange matter with the environment** (respiratory, digestive, urinary, integumentary); the rest control, transport, support or defend that exchange.
- **Nervous and endocrine are the two control arms**, and both act on every other system — speed and specificity differ, reach does not.
- **Absorption needs cardiovascular transport; oxygen uptake needs cardiovascular delivery; the kidney corrects what respiration and digestion disturb; immune tissue patrols all of them.**
- **Flux ∝ area × gradient ÷ thickness**: folds × villi × microvilli multiply the gut's surface several hundred-fold; ~300–500 million alveoli give ~70–100 m² of gas exchange.
- **Branching trees** (airway, arterial, renal, biliary) deliver or collect over a whole volume; exchange happens only at the thinnest, finest branches.
- **Barriers trade transport for safety**: one-cell epithelia and a ~0.5 µm respiratory membrane diffuse brilliantly and fail easily; blood–brain barrier tight junctions exclude most drugs.
- **Redundancy** (two kidneys, two lungs, paired vessels) makes many losses survivable; **single points of failure** (heart, spinal cord, trachea) are immediately life-threatening.

## Practice questions

**1. Sectioned from the lumen outward, the stomach wall shows columnar epithelium over loose connective tissue, then dense connective tissue with large vessels, then two smooth-muscle layers, then a thin mesothelial covering. In order these are:**

A. Submucosa, mucosa, muscularis externa, adventitia
B. Mucosa, serosa, submucosa, muscularis externa
C. Mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa
D. Epidermis, dermis, arrector pili, adventitia

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Mucosa → submucosa → muscularis externa → serosa is fixed, and mucosa comes first because it contacts the lumen. A reverses the inner two layers, B puts the outer covering second, and D lists skin.

---

**2. The inner circular and outer longitudinal arrangement of the muscularis externa produces propulsion because**

A. The circular layer narrows the lumen, the longitudinal layer shortens the segment, and the myenteric plexus sequences them
B. The circular layer is skeletal while the longitudinal layer is smooth
C. Both layers contract together to seal the lumen against leakage
D. The longitudinal layer anchors to bone and pulls the organ along

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Peristalsis needs narrowing and shortening at right angles, sequenced by the myenteric plexus between the layers. Both are smooth (B); simultaneous contraction occludes rather than propels (C); gut muscle attaches to no bone (D).

---

**3. Which set of criteria best decides whether a structure qualifies as an organ?**

A. Cell density, colour, and microscopic size
B. A distinct vital function, recognisable architecture, encapsulation, dedicated blood supply and named innervation, with at least two tissue types integrated
C. Being composed of epithelium alone
D. Containing a lumen through which material passes

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Borderline cases turn on architecture, supply and innervation, plus the requirement that two or more tissue types produce a function no single tissue could. A describes cells, C a tissue, and D excludes solid organs such as the liver.

---

**4. Skin resists stretching in any direction principally because of**

A. The keratinised stratified squamous epithelium of the epidermis
B. The smooth muscle of the arrector pili
C. The adipose tissue of the hypodermis
D. The dense irregular connective tissue of the dermis, whose collagen runs in many directions

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Dense irregular connective tissue has fibres woven in many planes — exactly the geometry that resists multidirectional shear, which is why it forms the dermis. Keratin resists abrasion (A), arrector pili produces piloerection (B), and the hypodermis is padding (C).

---

**5. A substance produces a response within milliseconds in one defined pathway. The control arm involved is most likely**

A. Endocrine, because hormones broadcast to every cell with a receptor
B. Nervous, because neural signalling is electrical, targeted and fast
C. Immune, because cytokines act on every organ simultaneously
D. Urinary, because the kidney regulates all effectors

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Neural impulses travel in milliseconds to a specified target; hormones act more slowly, last longer, and broadcast to any cell with the receptor. Both arms reach every system — speed and specificity differ, reach does not. The kidney is an effector, not a control arm.

---

**6. Glucose absorption from the small intestine is greatly increased mainly because**

A. The epithelium is stratified, packing more cells onto the surface
B. Folds, villi and microvilli multiply together while the barrier stays one cell thick
C. Glucose uses active transport, which is independent of surface area
D. The muscularis externa grinds food into molecules before absorption

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Flux is proportional to area × gradient ÷ thickness, so the gut compounds folds, villi and microvilli while keeping the path to a single cell. The epithelium is simple columnar (A), active transport still scales with area (C), and digestion is enzymatic (D).

---

**7. A patient with severe chronic lung disease retains CO₂ and, over days, the kidneys retain bicarbonate. This demonstrates that**

A. The urinary system corrects disturbances created by the respiratory system
B. The respiratory system directly controls renal blood flow
C. The digestive system is the chief regulator of blood pH
D. Organ systems operate independently of one another

**Answer: A**

Explanation: CO₂ is an acid anhydride, so hypoventilation lowers pH, and the kidney is the slow compensatory lever that reclaims bicarbonate. Anxiety hyperventilation shows the same principle in reverse. The demonstrated regulation is renal (C), and B and D deny the interdependence shown.

---

**8. Penicillin often fails to clear a central nervous system infection because**

A. Neurons metabolise the drug before it can act
B. Blood–brain barrier endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions that exclude most polar molecules
C. The meninges are made of bone rather than connective tissue
D. Cerebrospinal fluid is alkaline and inactivates the drug

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Brain capillaries lack fenestrations and pinocytic vesicles; tight junctions, basement membrane and astrocyte end-feet restrict charged and polar molecules, which is why special formulations are needed for meningitis. Neurons do not degrade penicillin (A), the meninges are membranes (C), and pH is not the barrier (D).

---

**9. The pancreas is classified under both the digestive and the endocrine systems because**

A. It is too large to belong to one system
B. Its acini secrete enzymes into a duct while its islets secrete hormones directly into capillaries
C. It contains both muscular and epithelial tissue
D. It is glandular tissue rather than a true organ

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The pancreas has two delivery routes — a duct carrying enzymes into the duodenum, and capillaries carrying insulin and glucagon into the blood — so one organ serves two systems. Classification follows the problem solved and the route of delivery, not size (A) or tissue type (C); D is wrong.

---

**10. A transplanted kidney has excellent surgical blood flow but is rejected within days. The mechanism most directly involved is**

A. Thrombosis of the anastomosed artery causing total ischaemia
B. Loss of epithelial integrity in the ureteric lining
C. Immune recognition of foreign histocompatibility antigens by the recipient's lymphocytes
D. Invasion of the graft by gut flora entering the peritoneal cavity

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Acute rejection is an immune attack on mismatched HLA antigens, which is why matching and immunosuppression accompany good surgery — a graft must succeed at the vascular, parenchymal **and** immune levels. Arterial thrombosis (A) gives immediate non-function, a ureteric leak (B) causes urinary leakage, and infection (D) is later.
