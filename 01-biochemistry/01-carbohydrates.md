# Carbohydrates

## Why they matter

Carbohydrates are the immediate fuel of life and the main energy storage form of plants and animals. They are also the most structurally varied of the four macromolecule families: the same elements arranged into a linear chain, a branch, or a helix produce something that dissolves instantly, resists digestion, or forms load-bearing fibre.

That range comes from one design choice — which of two carbon positions forms a bond to the oxygen of the hydroxyl group. Changing it flips the ring from one shape to another, and everything downstream changes with it.

## The four major macromolecules

Carbohydrates belong to a family of four organic molecules, all built from C, H, and O, all containing many hydroxyl groups:

| Class | Monomer | Polymer | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Carbohydrates** | Monosaccharide | Polysaccharide | Approx. $(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$; energy, storage, structure |
| **Lipids** | Fatty acid | — (not true polymers) | Hydrophobic; energy storage, membranes, signalling |
| **Proteins** | Amino acid | Polypeptide | 20 types; enzymes, structure, transport, signalling |
| **Nucleic acids** | Nucleotide | DNA, RNA | Information storage and expression |

Carbohydrates are defined by the general formula $(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$ — but this is a useful shortcut with a real limitation. Some carbohydrates do not match it exactly. **Deoxyribose** in DNA is $(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_4})$, with one oxygen fewer, which is exactly why it is called *deoxy*. **Amino sugars** have a nitrogen replacing a hydroxyl.

## Monosaccharides

**Monosaccharides** (mono = single) are the simplest carbohydrates. They cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrates. Several isomers exist with the same molecular formula, and the structural differences matter enormously.

### The three important monosaccharides

| Property | Glucose | Fructose | Galactose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Molecular formula | $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ | $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ | $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ |
| Carbon atoms | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| Ring size formed | 6-membered (pyranose) | 5-membered (furanose) | 6-membered (pyranose) |
| Key use | Main fuel; blood glucose | Sweetest; component of sucrose | Component of lactose |
| Where it goes | Cellular respiration | → glucose after metabolism | → glucose after metabolism |

Same formula, different structure, different behaviour. Glucose, fructose, and galactose are all C₆H₁₂O₆, and none can be converted into another without changing the arrangement of atoms — which is why each requires its own enzyme.

### Ring formation and the α/β designation

In water, a five- or six-carbon sugar reacts with itself: a hydroxyl group on one carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon, and a **cyclic** structure forms. This happens because the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.

```
LINEAR FORM              CYCLIC FORM
   CHO                       OH
    |                        |
 H—C—OH                   H—C——O
    |                      /    \
 H—C—OH                  OH     CH—OH
    |                    |      |
 H—C—OH                  H—C——O  H
    |                      \    /
 H—C—OH                    OH CH—OH
    |                        |
  CH₂OH                    H—C—OH
                                  |
                                 CH₂OH
```

Two consequences:

**1. The α and β anomers.** The new stereocentre at the anomeric carbon can face either direction. The anomer with the hydroxyl **trans** (on the opposite face) to the terminal CH₂OH group is the **α-anomer**; **cis** to it is the **β-anomer**.

**2. The α and β forms are different molecules with different biological effects** — because the enzymes that act on them are chiral and distinguish the two. This is not a chemical nicety:

| Sugar | Consequence of the distinction |
| --- | --- |
| **Glucose** | The majority of glucose in solution is the β-anomer. Cells store it as **α-glucose** in glycogen and starch — the α form is what glycogen phosphorylase and starch synthase recognise |
| **Galactose** | In lactose, galactose is **β(1→4)**-linked to glucose |
| **Fructose** | In sucrose, fructose is **α(1→2)**-linked to glucose |

Maltase hydrolyses α(1→4) bonds; lactase hydrolyses β(1→4) bonds. This is the mechanism behind lactase deficiency and lactose intolerance, and it is a clean illustration of why stereochemistry carries biological meaning.

### Monosaccharides worth naming

| Sugar | Where it appears |
| --- | --- |
| **Ribose** | 5-carbon; RNA, ATP, NAD, coenzymes |
| **Deoxyribose** | 5-carbon, missing one oxygen; DNA |
| **Galactose** | Lactose; myelin |
| **Fructose** | Sucrose, honey, seminal fluid |
| **Lactose** | Milk sugar — glucose + galactose |
| **Sucrose** | Table sugar — glucose + fructose |

Note the recurring pattern: **a five-carbon sugar in nucleic acids, a six-carbon sugar in fuel.** See [04 — Nucleic acids](04-nucleic-acids.md).

## Disaccharides

**Disaccharides** are formed by **dehydration synthesis** (condensation): two monosaccharides join, releasing one water molecule and forming one glycosidic bond.

```
   CH₂OH                              CH₂OH
     |                                  |
     C — OH    +    H — C — OH   →     C — O — C      +    H₂O
     |                |                 |       |
   CH₂OH             CH₂OH            CH₂OH    CH₂OH

        dehydration synthesis:   —OH  +  H—   →   H₂O   (released)

        the C—O—C linkage is the glycosidic bond
```

| Disaccharide | Monosaccharides | Bond | Found in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sucrose** | Glucose + fructose | α(1→2) | Sugarcane, table sugar |
| **Lactose** | Glucose + galactose | β(1→4) | Milk |
| **Maltose** | Glucose + glucose | α(1→4) | Germinating grains; malt |

The importance of that table extends beyond nutrition. The α-versus-β and the 1→4-versus-1→2 designation determine which enzyme can cleave the bond, and therefore whether a carbohydrate is digestible at all.

## Polysaccharides

**Polysaccharides** are long chains of monosaccharides, usually glucose. The chains differ in the linkage type, the branching, and the stereochemistry of the monomers — and these three variables produce entirely different properties.

### Starch — plant storage

Found in potatoes, rice, wheat, and maize.

| Property | Amylose | Amylopectin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bonds | α(1→4) | α(1→4) with α(1→6) branches |
| Structure | Unbranched helix | Branched |
| Proportion of starch | ~20–30% | ~70–80% |
| Digestibility | Slow | Faster (more ends for enzymes to attack) |

Human enzymes that cleave **α**-bonds cannot cleave β-bonds. That single fact explains why starch is digestible and cellulose is not.

### Glycogen — animal storage

The animal equivalent of starch, and structurally more efficient.

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Bonds | α(1→4) with α(1→6) branches |
| Highly branched | Yes |
| Compared with amylopectin | Branches are shorter and more frequent |
| Storage site | Liver (blood glucose buffer), muscle (local fuel) |
| Overall α-glucose | Almost exclusively |

The high branching is a design solution, and it is worth understanding why:

```
AMYLOPECTIN:  branches every 24–30 residues
GLYCOGEN:     branches every 8–12 residues
```

Every branch point is an **end** for the enzyme. A single glycogen molecule may have hundreds of branches, so hundreds of glucose residues can be released simultaneously when the need arises. It allows glucose to be mobilised in under a minute — which is the requirement. A linear chain with only two ends would release glucose far too slowly.

**Common confusion:** glycogen is not "animal starch." The linkage is the same α(1→4), but the branching pattern is deliberately different, and the storage forms are not interchangeable in their release kinetics.

### Cellulose — plant structure

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Bonds | β(1→4) |
| Structure | Straight, unbranched chains |
| Arrangement | Parallel chains packed with hydrogen bonds into **microfibrils** |
| Function | Structural — cell wall |

**Why cellulose resists digestion.** Humans lack **β(1→4) glycosidase** — cellulase. Ruminants do not produce it either; they host bacteria that do, in the rumen. Humans do carry cellulose-degrading bacteria in the large intestine, but they ferment rather than absorb the products, so the energetic yield is small.

The cell wall is not merely a cellulose network. It also contains **pectin** and **hemicellulose**, and the matrix it forms resists tension — which is the basis of the distinction between plant and animal connective tissue described in 09 — Human Biology.

### Chitin

β(1→4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine. Forms the **exoskeleton** of arthropods and the cell wall of fungi. Its presence in both groups, and its chemical relationship to cellulose, is one of the more striking pieces of evidence for how metabolism is shared across life.

### Other important polysaccharides

| Polysaccharide | Location | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Glycogen** | Liver, muscle | Energy storage |
| **Heparin** | Mast cells, basophils | Anticoagulant |
| **Glycosaminoglycans** | Ground substance of connective tissue; cartilage | Water retention, lubrication, shock absorption |
| **Cellulose** | Plant cell walls | Structure |
| **Chitin** | Arthropod exoskeleton; fungal walls | Structure |

Heparin and the glycosaminoglycans are the reason connective tissue holds water. That property is covered in 09 — Human Biology and becomes clinically important in 10 — Cardiovascular, where a fall in plasma protein changes tissue fluid dynamics.

| Function | Example |
| --- | --- |

## Medical relevance

**Blood glucose regulation is the central carbohydrate physiology.** Every cell takes up glucose, but insulin-dependent tissues — chiefly skeletal muscle and adipose tissue — require the transporter GLUT4 to be moved from intracellular vesicles to the membrane. Without insulin, GLUT4 stays sequestered and glucose stays outside the cell. The result is that glucose accumulates in the blood while cells effectively starve — the paradox at the centre of diabetes mellitus.

```
EATING → BLOOD GLUCOSE RISES
   ↓
BETA CELLS OF PANCREAS DETECT THE RISE
   ↓
INSULIN RELEASED INTO BLOOD
   ↓
GLUT4 MOVED TO CELL MEMBRANE in muscle and adipose tissue
   ↓
GLUCOSE ENTERS CELLS; LIVER AND MUSCLE STORE IT AS GLYCOGEN
   ↓
BLOOD GLUCOSE RETURNS TOWARD NORMAL
```

The counter-regulatory hormone **glucagon** works in the opposite direction, and the reason it must exist is that blood glucose cannot be allowed to fall. Brain tissue depends almost entirely on glucose, and between meals the liver must release it — which is what glucagon arranges. This control loop is worked through fully in 09-human-biology/03-homeostasis.md.

**Glycogen structure is directly clinical.** The highly branched architecture means release is fast, which is what makes an immediate response to falling glucose possible. This is why glycogen storage diseases — inherited defects in the enzymes that break down glycogen — present as a restricted tolerance of fasting, with accumulation of abnormal glycogen in liver and muscle.

**Lactase deficiency is a stereochemical problem.** Because lactase hydrolyses **β(1→4)** bonds and no human enzyme handles the β(1→4) linkage, the β-galactosidase activity present in most people declines after weaning and persists at low levels in many adults. The unabsorbed lactose then reaches colonic bacteria, and their fermentation produces the gas and osmotic symptoms.

**Glycosaminoglycans explain the behaviour of connective tissue.** Their negatively charged chains repel neighbouring chains and attract water, so cartilage is firm yet resilient, and tissue fluid is retained in the matrix. Ageing reduces glycosaminoglycan content, which is part of why joints become stiffer and why the matrix holds less water.

**Blood group antigens are carbohydrates.** ABO antigens on red blood cells are carbohydrate chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids. This is the basis of the ABO system and of transfusion reactions, in 10 — Immune.

**Why fasting and prolonged exercise use different fuels.** During the first ~20 minutes of exercise at high intensity, ATP and creatine phosphate cover the demand. Anaerobic glycolysis then uses muscle glycogen to make lactate. Only later does aerobic metabolism of fatty acids and glucose predominate. So glycogen is the short-term reserve and triglyceride stores are the long-term one — see 03 — Cellular Processes.

## Key facts

- Carbohydrates are C, H, O with many **hydroxyl (–OH)** groups; roughly $(\mathrm{CH_2O})_n$.
- **Monosaccharides** (glucose, fructose, galactose) cannot be hydrolysed further. Same formula ≠ same structure.
- Ring formation creates the anomeric carbon → **α** and **β** anomers, which are **different molecules** to enzymes.
- **Disaccharides** form by dehydration synthesis: sucrose (α1→2), lactose (β1→4), maltose (α1→4).
- **Starch** = plant storage, α(1→4) plus α(1→6); **glycogen** = animal storage, same bonds, **more heavily branched**; **cellulose** = plant structure, **β(1→4)**, indigestible to humans.
- Lack of **β(1→4) glycosidase** (cellulase) is why cellulose resists human digestion.
- Highly branching means many chain ends → fast simultaneous release. Glycogen branches every 8–12 residues; amylopectin every 24–30.
- Cellulose's β-linkage produces straight chains that pack into microfibrils via hydrogen bonding.

## Practice questions

**1. Why is cellulose indigestible to humans?**

A. It is insoluble in water and therefore cannot reach the digestive enzymes
B. It contains a different monosaccharide than starch
C. Humans lack β(1→4) glycosidase, so the bond linking its glucose residues cannot be hydrolysed
D. It is broken down in the stomach before reaching the intestine

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Cellulose consists of glucose joined by β(1→4) bonds. Human digestive enzymes cleave α bonds, so no human enzyme can hydrolyse that linkage. Ruminants solve this by hosting cellulase-producing bacteria in the rumen. A is not the reason — enzymes act at surfaces, and insolubility reduces but does not prevent digestion, as starch is also largely insoluble. B and D are incorrect: cellulose and starch are built from the same monomer, glucose, and stomach acid acts on protein rather than carbohydrate.

**2. A food label lists glucose and fructose, both with the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆. What does this indicate about them?**

A. They are structural isomers with different properties and different enzymes acting on them
B. One is a monosaccharide and the other is a disaccharide
C. They have the same energy content and therefore the same biological effect
D. They are chemically identical

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Same molecular formula but different arrangement of atoms — structural isomers. Their ring sizes, their α/β forms, and their enzyme specificity all differ. Both are monosaccharides, so D is wrong. C fails because equal chemical formulas do not imply equal metabolic handling — each isomer requires its own enzymes.

**3. Glycogen is described as more highly branched than starch. What is the functional advantage?**

A. Branching provides many chain ends, so multiple glucose residues can be released simultaneously by enzymes
B. Branching allows glycogen to store more water
C. Branching increases the energy content per gram
D. Branching makes glycogen more soluble

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Enzymes act at chain ends. A highly branched glycogen molecule has hundreds of ends, so a large number of residues can be liberated at once — essential when blood glucose must be corrected within minutes. Branching does not change the energy per gram, since the residues are identical glucose, and it has nothing to do with solubility or water storage.

**4. Lactase hydrolyses β(1→4) bonds. Which of the following explains why lactose intolerance is a problem for many adults?**

A. Most adults produce no lactase at all
B. Adults cannot digest disaccharides
C. Lactase is destroyed by stomach acid
D. Lactase activity declines after weaning, so β(1→4) bonds in lactose are not cleaved and lactose reaches the colon

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Lactase activity declines sharply after weaning in most humans and remains low in many adults, so lactose is not cleaved in the small intestine. It reaches the colon, where bacterial fermentation produces gas and the osmotic effect produces the other symptoms. A overstates it — most adults retain some lactase. B is false: lactase is a brush-border enzyme of the small intestine, so its activity depends on where the enzyme sits, not on general digestive capacity. C is false: lactase is not destroyed by stomach acid, and monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides are all digestible given the right enzymes.

**5. Which statement correctly pairs a carbohydrate with its function?**

A. Glycogen — plant cell wall structural component
B. Chitin — structural component of arthropod exoskeletons
C. Cellulose — long-term energy storage in animals
D. Starch — blood glucose regulation

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Chitin is a β(1→4)-linked polymer of N-acetylglucosamine and forms arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. A is wrong — cellulose forms plant cell walls; glycogen is animal storage. C is wrong — cellulose is structural, and animals store energy as triglycerides. D is wrong — starch is plant storage; regulation of blood glucose is by insulin and glucagon.

**6. Maltase can hydrolyse maltose but not lactose. What distinguishes the two bonds?**

A. Both are α(1→4); the difference is the monosaccharides involved
B. Maltose has a β(1→4) bond and lactose has an α(1→4) bond
C. Maltose has an α(1→4) bond and lactose has a β(1→4) bond
D. Maltose has an α(1→2) bond and lactose has a β(1→4) bond

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Maltose is two glucose units joined α(1→4); lactose is glucose and galactose joined β(1→4). Enzyme specificity is for the spatial arrangement of the glycosidic centre, not only for the sugars, so the stereochemistry alone determines whether maltase acts. This is the same distinction that separates starch from cellulose.

**7. A patient has a high blood glucose concentration and a glucose concentration inside their cells that is lower than expected. Which mechanism most directly explains this?**

A. Insulin-stimulated translocation of GLUT4 to the cell membrane has failed
B. Too much glucose is being lost in the urine
C. The cells are producing glucose from amino acids
D. Glucose cannot pass through cell membranes

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Muscle and adipose tissue depend on insulin to move GLUT4 transporters from intracellular vesicles to the membrane. Without that step, glucose cannot enter efficiently even though the extracellular concentration is high — the paradox of high blood glucose alongside cellular glucose deprivation. B is false; glucose crosses membranes readily via facilitated diffusion. C describes gluconeogenesis, which raises blood glucose but does not explain low intracellular glucose. D is a consequence, not the cause.

**8. The enzymes that produce starch and glycogen form glycosidic bonds. Why can the human enzyme make glycogen but not cellulose?**

A. Cellulose has weaker bonds than glycogen
B. Human glycogen synthase is stereospecific for α(1→4) linkages, and the human body lacks a cellulose synthase
C. Human enzymes cannot form glycosidic bonds at all
D. Cellulose is produced only by bacteria

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Enzymes are chiral and act on a specific three-dimensional arrangement at the glycosidic carbon. The human enzyme that polymerises glucose forms the α linkage found in glycogen; cellulose synthase, which forms the β linkage, is absent in animals. Bond strength is not the issue, and cellulose is produced by plants — as well as by some bacteria and algae.

**9. A disaccharide is formed from two monosaccharides. Which statement about this reaction is correct?**

A. Two water molecules are released
B. The reaction is a hydrolysis
C. Water is consumed from the environment
D. A water molecule is released as the glycosidic bond forms

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Dehydration synthesis joins two sugars covalently and releases one water molecule — the OH from one monosaccharide and an H from the other. The reverse reaction, hydrolysis, consumes water to break the bond. This is why digesting a disaccharide requires water to be present in the digestive tract.

**10. Cartilage is firm but able to absorb compression. Which structural feature best explains this?**

A. Elastin fibres that stretch and recoil
B. Negatively charged glycosaminoglycan chains that repel each other and attract water
C. Dense collagen bundles arranged in parallel
D. A calcified matrix

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Glycosaminoglycans carry many negative charges along the chain, so they repel one another and expand the matrix, drawing in water. Water in a pressurised matrix resists compression, which is what allows cartilage to be firm yet resilient. Parallel collagen bundles provide tensile strength, which is a different property — that is the arrangement in tendon. Elastin provides recoil in skin and arteries, and calcification would make the matrix rigid.
