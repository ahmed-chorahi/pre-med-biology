# Water Transport and Transpiration

## Why it matters

A mature oak stands 30–40 m tall and a coast redwood exceeds 100 m, yet no litre of the water they lift is pumped. The whole chapter is one mechanism — **cohesion–tension**: water is **pulled from above**, never pushed from below, and the energy comes from two free sources, **evaporation at the leaf surface** and **hydrogen bonds between water molecules**. Wilting is that chain slackening, guttation is it idle, cavitation is it snapping, drought death is it failing for good, and annual rings are its historical record. Two supporting ideas carry the argument: **water potential (Ψ)**, the quantitative vocabulary of water movement — the same quantity that governs osmosis in [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md), red-cell lysis and IV fluid choice — and the **stoma**, the valve that sets how hard the chain is pulled and trades CO₂ gain against water loss.

## Water potential: the vocabulary of the mechanism

**Water potential, symbol Ψ (psi), is the potential energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure**, measured in **megapascals (MPa)**; 0.1 MPa = 1 bar ≈ 1 atmosphere. It is written:

```
Ψ = Ψs + Ψp   (+ Ψg and Ψm, when height or drying matters)
```

| Component | Name | Sign and meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ψs** | Solute (osmotic) potential | **Always ≤ 0**; pure water = 0; solute makes it *more negative* |
| **Ψp** | Pressure potential | Positive when water is compressed (turgor, root pressure); **negative under tension** (normal xylem) |
| **Ψg** | Gravitational potential | **+0.01 MPa per metre** — higher water is *less negative* |
| **Ψm** | Matric potential | Water bound to walls and soil particles; negative, important in drying soil |

**Two rules govern every arrow in this chapter:**

1. **Pure water at atmospheric pressure = Ψ 0.**
2. **Water moves spontaneously from higher Ψ to lower Ψ** — as heat flows hot to cold, and as osmosis moves water toward more solute.

Solute particles bind water into hydration shells, so the concentration of **free** water molecules falls, and Ψ falls with it:

```
dissolve solute → water bound into hydration shells → fewer FREE water molecules
               → Ψs falls (more negative) → water moves toward that solution
```

This is the "water follows solute" rule of [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md) written as a potential. Ψs is **colligative** — particle number, not identity — so 1 M sucrose and 1 M glucose both give ≈ −2.7 MPa, while a dissociating salt gives roughly twice that per mole.

| Compartment | Typical Ψ (MPa) | Dominant component |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wet soil | **−0.01 to −0.03** | Slightly negative Ψs |
| Root hair / cortex cytoplasm | −0.2 to −0.5 | Ψs |
| Xylem sap in the stem | **−0.5 to −2.0** | **Ψp negative** (tension) |
| Leaf mesophyll cell wall | −1.0 to −3.0 | Ψm and Ψs |
| Atmosphere at 50 % humidity | **≈ −100** | Ψ of unsaturated water vapour |

**The plant supplies a wet surface in a leaf and lets dry air do the pulling**: soil (−0.01) → root (−0.3) → xylem (−1.0) → leaf wall (−2.0) → air (−100 MPa). The leaf–atmosphere interface is the steepest water potential gradient in biology — the mechanism's energy source.

## The pathway: soil to leaf

```
SOIL WATER
   │  enters the ROOT HAIR by OSMOSIS, down the gradient the epidermis
   │  and cortex created by accumulating solutes
   ▼
CORTEX — mostly via the APOPLAST (cell walls + intercellular spaces):
   │  water crosses walls without touching a membrane, so with no selectivity
   ▼
ENDODERMIS — CASPARIAN STRIP blocks the apoplast dead ahead
   │  water is FORCED across a plasma membrane → SYMPLAST, then cell to
   │  cell through PLASMODESMATA: the selective checkpoint
   ▼
PERICYCLE → XYLEM (dead, hollow, lignified vessels and tracheids),
   │  one continuous column held under tension, cross-flow through PITS
   ▼
UP THE STEM → LEAF bundle sheath → mesophyll CELL WALLS (apoplast again)
   ▼
EVAPORATION into the SUBSTOMATAL air space, then DIFFUSION down a vapour
   gradient through the OPEN STOMA to the atmosphere
```

### Root hair and cortex

A **root hair** is a tubular extension of a single epidermal cell — no cross-wall, cytoplasm continuous with the parent cell — multiplying absorptive surface perhaps tenfold. Entry is **osmotic**: the epidermis and cortex accumulate solutes, so their Ψ falls below soil Ψ and water follows. As soil dries, soil Ψ falls too and uptake stops — the origin of drought stress.

Across the **cortex** most water travels in the **apoplast**, the continuous system of cell walls, intercellular air spaces and adsorbed wall water — a low-resistance shortcut precisely because **no membrane is crossed** and therefore **no selectivity is applied**.

### The Casparian strip: the checkpoint

The endodermis is a ring of cells whose radial and transverse walls are impregnated with **suberin** and **lignin** — the **Casparian strip**. Suberin is waterproof, so the shortcut terminates there:

```
APOPLAST ───────── ✕ BLOCKED by the Casparian strip ─────────
SYMPLAST ── across the plasma membrane, then plasmodesmata ──▶ STELE
```

**This is the decisive step of the pathway**, because the membrane crossing is where selectivity happens: every solute must traverse a lipid bilayer of the kind described in [01 — Membrane structure](../03-cellular-processes/01-membrane-structure-and-fluid-mosaic.md), so the plant can refuse toxins, exclude pathogens and regulate which ions reach the xylem — and since water follows solute, **controlling ions means controlling water**. The root anatomy is in [02 — Roots, stems and leaves](02-roots-stems-and-leaves.md).

Past the endodermis, water enters **xylem**: dead, hollow, lignified **vessels** and **tracheids** joined by perforation plates and **bordered pits** (anatomy in [03 — Xylem and phloem](03-xylem-and-phloem.md)). Nothing living lies between root and leaf — one continuous phase in narrow tubes, **which allows the whole column to be pulled as a single piece**, and makes any air bubble in it a problem.

## Cohesion–tension theory

**Cohesion–tension theory states that water is pulled up the plant by negative pressure generated at the leaf and transmitted down a continuous column held together by hydrogen bonds.**

### The full arrow chain

```
SUNLIGHT warms the leaf; the air outside is far drier than the wall surface
        ↓
WATER EVAPORATES from mesophyll CELL WALLS into substomatal air spaces,
and vapour diffuses out through the OPEN STOMA (wall −2 MPa → air −100)
        ↓
menisci recede into tiny wall pores → capillary curvature makes wall water
MORE NEGATIVE → Ψ of the leaf xylem falls
        ↓
TENSION (negative Ψp) develops at the top of the column and is TRANSMITTED
down the continuous water column
        ↓
COHESION (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) resists the column being
pulled apart; ADHESION (water ↔ lignified wall) + capillarity STABILISE it
        ↓
water is PULLED stem-ward, root-ward, soil-ward
        ↓
NET RESULT: evaporation at the leaf lifts water 100 m with no pump
```

### Why it works against gravity for 100 m

| Quantity | Value | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravitational cost of 100 m | Ψg = 0.01 × 100 = **+1 MPa** | The hill to climb |
| Pressure needed to push 100 m from below | ρgh ≈ **1 MPa (≈10 atm)** plus friction | What a root pump would need, continuously |
| Largest root pressure recorded | **≤ 0.3–0.5 MPa**, rarely ~1 MPa | **Not enough for a tall tree** |
| Tension routinely developed at the leaf | **−1 to −5 MPa** (−10 MPa in conifers) | Comfortably pays the 1 MPa bill |
| Tensile strength of pure water | about **−30 MPa** | Far more than normal operation demands |

**The gradient, not the height, supplies the energy.** The atmosphere is an infinite sink at ≈ −100 MPa, so the +1 MPa gravitational hurdle is trivial: **pushing needs > 1 MPa and root pressure maxes at ~0.3–0.5; pulling routinely reaches −1 to −5**. Height limits trees through path resistance and the mechanical cost of wood, not through the thermodynamics of water transport.

### Why the xylem must be under tension, not pressure

The intuitive picture — roots pumping water up a straw — fails on physics:

| | **Pushed from below** (positive pressure) | **Pulled from above** (negative pressure) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driving force | Root pressure ≤ 0.3–0.5 MPa, mainly at night | Transpiration, scaling with demand |
| Load on conduit walls | Tension — thin-walled living cells would burst | **Compression**, which lignified tissue resists |
| Behaviour with height | Fails after a few metres | No limit while the column stays continuous |
| Response to dry air | Cannot increase | Tension deepens automatically as VPD rises |
| Characteristic failure | Pressure cannot be generated at all | **Cavitation** — a bubble nucleates under excess tension |

Negative pressure loads the **wall in compression**, and lignin is a compression-resistant material: the xylem is built for exactly the sign of force the mechanism applies. This is why the conduits are dead — a living cell could not tolerate internal stretch.

### Cavitation and embolism: the Achilles' heel

When tension exceeds the local strength of the column, or air is seeded through a pit membrane (**air seeding**), dissolved gas comes out of solution and a bubble expands:

```
tension rises / air seeds through a PIT MEMBRANE
        ↓
gas nucleates → CAVITATION → bubble fills the conduit
        ↓
EMBOLISM: an air-filled conduit with conductivity = 0;
        water diverts to neighbours through their PITS
```

**Four overlapping defences let plants live with it:**

| Defence | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Pit aspiration** | In conifers the torus of a bordered pit membrane seals the aperture when a neighbour cavitates |
| **Redundancy, narrow conduits** | Cross-flow around blockages via pits; small conduits cavitate less, so the network degrades gracefully |
| **Compartmentalisation** | Embolised or infected conduits are sealed by tyloses and gels — the logic that confines decay in wood |
| **Refilling** | Root pressure and ion/sugar loading by xylem parenchyma make the conduit positive again → water enters osmotically → the bubble redissolves |

In drought, embolisms accumulate faster than they are refilled: conductance falls, stomata close, photosynthesis stops and reserves deplete. **Hydraulic failure plus carbon starvation is the accepted mechanism of drought-induced tree death.** The same blockage logic explains **vascular wilt disease** — *Ralstonia*, *Fusarium*, *Verticillium* and *Xylella* occlude xylem vessels, so the plant wilts from a blocked conduit **even in wet soil**.

### Transpiration and guttation are different processes

| | **Transpiration** | **Guttation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driving force | **Evaporation** → negative pressure (tension) | **Root pressure** → positive pressure |
| Form and route | Water **vapour** through **stomata** | Liquid **xylem sap** through **hydathodes** at leaf margins and tips |
| When | **Daytime**, while stomata are open | **Night/early morning**, roots absorbing but transpiration near zero |
| Composition | Essentially pure H₂O | **Ions, sugars, amino acids**; dries to a residue |
| In a tall tree | Dominant — cubic metres per tree per year | Usually negligible; conspicuous in grasses at dawn |

**Guttation droplets on a lawn at dawn are root pressure made visible; dew is atmospheric water condensing on the leaf.** Neither is transpiration — both occur while the cohesion–tension chain is idle.

## Stomata: the control valve

Every CO₂ molecule a leaf fixes enters through a **stoma** (plural *stomata*), and every water molecule lost by transpiration exits through one. Two fluxes, one aperture — the plant's carbon-versus-water economy is a question about two cells.

### Guard cell anatomy

| Feature | **Dicots (and most gymnosperms)** | **Grasses (Poaceae)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guard cell shape | **Kidney-shaped** (bean-shaped) | **Dumb-bell shaped** — swollen ends, constricted middle |
| Neighbouring cells | Two similar subsidiary cells | Two large, distinct subsidiary cells |
| Cellulose microfibrils | **Radial** — around the circumference | Mainly **longitudinal** — along the axis |
| Opening movement | Turgid cells **bow outward**, separating the inner walls | Swollen ends press apart, straightening the mid-regions |
| Character | Broader, somewhat slower | Faster, more sensitive control |

The fact that makes both work: **microfibrils resist radial stretching, so a turgid guard cell can only lengthen, and that geometry bows the pair apart to open the pore.** Lose turgor and elastic walls restore the closed position.

### Opening: the arrow chain

```
DAWN: BLUE LIGHT via PHOTOTROPINS (PHOT1, PHOT2),
      reinforced by CO₂ drawdown and the CIRCADIAN clock
        ↓
H⁺-ATPase PUMPS H⁺ OUT of the guard cell (ATP-driven)
        ↓
membrane HYPERPOLARISES → voltage-gated INWARD K⁺ CHANNELS open → K⁺ IN
        ↓
Cl⁻ follows to balance charge; malate²⁻ is made from stored starch
(often plus imported sucrose) → internal solute concentration RISES
        ↓
Ψs falls markedly → WATER ENTERS BY OSMOSIS (aquaporins accelerate it)
        ↓
TURGOR RISES to about +2 to +4 MPa
        ↓
radial microfibrils force the pressurised cells to BOW APART
        ↓
PORE OPENS → CO₂ diffuses in for photosynthesis
```

**Closure runs the chain backwards:** solute efflux → water leaves → turgor falls → the pore seals. The first step is a **pump**, so guard-cell movement is the one part of this chapter that spends ATP directly.

### Closure by abscisic acid (ABA) in drought

**Abscisic acid (ABA)** is the drought hormone; its synthesis and general physiology are in [05 — Plant hormones](05-plant-hormones.md). At the guard cell:

```
soil dries → roots (and shoots) synthesise ABA
        ↓
ABA binds PYR/PYL RECEPTORS → these inhibit PP2C PHOSPHATASES
        ↓
SnRK2 KINASES are released from inhibition and become ACTIVE
        ↓
SLAC1 anion channels open → Cl⁻ and malate²⁻ EFFLUX
        ↓
membrane DEPOLARISES → outward K⁺ channels (GORK) open → K⁺ EFFLUX
        ↓
water leaves osmotically → turgor collapses → STOMATA CLOSE
        ↓
transpiration falls sharply, leaf Ψ is defended, CO₂ supply is cut
```

**Closing for minutes is far cheaper than permanent embolism:** a shut stoma costs a day's carbon, a cavitated column can cost a branch or the tree. ABA often acts before leaf Ψ has fallen appreciably — root signalling precedes hydraulic failure.

### Circadian and day/night behaviour

Stomata run on an **endogenous circadian programme** as well as on light and humidity: they open before dawn, are widest in morning light, narrow at midday when the vapour pressure deficit is extreme (**midday depression**), and close at dusk — and they keep oscillating in continuous light or darkness, which is how the clock was shown to be endogenous.

Night closure is what makes **guttation** possible: the valve is shut, roots keep loading ions, and the positive pressure exits at the hydathodes instead. **CAM plants invert the strategy** — opening at night when it is cool and humid, storing CO₂ as malate, then refixing it by day with the pores shut ([09 — Photosynthesis](../03-cellular-processes/09-photosynthesis.md)).

### The fundamental trade-off: CO₂ in, water out

| Flux through one open stoma | Typical magnitude |
| --- | --- |
| CO₂ gained | 6 molecules |
| H₂O lost | ≈ 400 molecules |
| Molar ratio | **≈ 1 : 100** — water-use efficiency ≈ 1–2 g dry matter per kg water |

CO₂ diffuses **in** through the same aperture that water vapour diffuses **out**, so transpiration is the unavoidable tax on photosynthesis. Stomata are therefore **optimised rather than simply open or closed**: aperture widens only while the marginal carbon gain justifies the marginal water cost — which is why stomata narrow as temperature and **vapour pressure deficit (VPD)** rise even in bright light.

## Factors affecting transpiration rate

Transpiration is driven by the **vapour pressure gradient between the saturated leaf interior and the drier air**, set by aperture plus the boundary layer of still air clinging to the leaf.

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Light** | **Increases**, sharply at dawn | Blue light activates H⁺-ATPase → stomata open; photosynthesis lowers internal CO₂, reinforcing opening |
| **Temperature** | **Increases** | Saturation vapour pressure of the leaf interior rises steeply → steeper gradient to the air |
| **Humidity / VPD** | **Falls as humidity rises** | High RH means small **vapour pressure deficit**; **VPD, not relative humidity alone, is the physical driver** |
| **Wind** | **Increases** up to a point | Sweeps away the saturated **boundary layer** and replaces it with drier air; very strong wind can trigger closure |
| **Soil water** | **Decreases** uptake → closure | Soil Ψ falls → uptake slows → ABA rises → stomata close |
| **Leaf surface features** | Modulate | Cuticle limits non-stomatal loss; sunken stomata and trichomes thicken the boundary layer (xerophyte adaptations) |

A **potometer** measures **water uptake** by a leafy shoot cut beneath water (so no air enters the xylem), connected to a capillary tube; transpiration drags a bubble or meniscus a measurable distance per unit time. **The critical caveat: it measures uptake, not strictly transpiration** — some absorbed water is retained in growth, a fraction is used in photosynthesis, and the excised shoot may not behave like the intact plant. Correcting for these gives reliable **relative** rates and clean demonstrations of the effects of light, wind, humidity and temperature.

## Factors affecting water uptake

Uptake occurs down a Ψ gradient, and the plant controls that gradient three ways.

1. **Active ion loading sets the gradient.** H⁺-ATPases and ion channels in the endodermis and xylem parenchyma pump K⁺, Ca²⁺, NO₃⁻ and other solutes into the stele. **Ions accumulate → Ψs of the sap falls → water follows osmotically** across the Casparian-strip checkpoint. The pump is [active transport](../03-cellular-processes/03-active-transport.md) — ATP-powered movement against a gradient — and the water entry it triggers is passive, driven by the gradient it created.
2. **Mycorrhizal fungi extend the absorptive surface.** In **arbuscular mycorrhiza**, hyphae explore soil volumes root hairs cannot reach and trade phosphate and water for plant carbon; **ectomycorrhizae** sheathe the root tip. Being far finer than roots, hyphae exploit smaller pores and sustain uptake as soil Ψ falls. **This adds surface area without changing the mechanism** — entry is still osmosis.
3. **Transpiration is the dominant pulling force in most conditions.** In a transpiring tree, leaf-generated tension transmitted through the xylem sets the flow rate; root pressure matters only in small plants, mainly at night. Stop transpiration (darkness, saturated air, ABA) and uptake falls within minutes.

## Relevance to medicine and agriculture

**Wilting is turgor loss, and it is reversible.** When soil Ψ falls below cell Ψ, cells lose water, the vacuole shrinks and the wall stops pressing back — **loss of turgor** — so herbaceous tissue collapses visibly. Re-water, and osmosis restores turgor within hours: the plant was unpressurised, not dead. The **permanent wilting point** (≈ −1.5 MPa soil Ψ) is the threshold beyond which roots can no longer extract water — a *soil* property used to define available water and to schedule irrigation.

**Water potential is the same quantity as clinical tonicity.** The Ψ framework here underlies IV fluid selection, red-cell lysis in hypotonic solution and the kidney's concentrating mechanism — all worked through in [02 — Passive transport](../03-cellular-processes/02-passive-transport.md). Dehydration, hyponatraemia and oral rehydration therapy are the clinical face of "water follows solute", with the ATP cost of such gradients set by [04 — ATP and metabolism](../03-cellular-processes/04-atp-and-metabolism.md).

**Gas in a tension system behaves like gas in a vascular system.** Cavitation is dissolved gas coming out of solution because local pressure fell below the gas's solubility limit — the same physics as **decompression sickness** in divers and **venous air embolism** when a line is not primed. Relatedly, **vascular wilts are the plant analogue of a vascular occlusion**: *Ralstonia*, *Fusarium*, *Verticillium* and *Xylella* block the transpiration stream so the plant wilts in wet soil, spreading through contaminated soil, water, pruning tools and insect vectors — the environmental-reservoir pattern framed in [05 — Microbial reproduction and transmission](../07-microbiology/).

**Xylem-mobile and phloem-mobile agrochemicals behave exactly as their transport streams do.** Compounds moving in the **transpiration stream** travel **acropetally** (root → shoot), so soil- or foliage-applied **systemic** insecticides and fungicides accumulate in leaves — the basis of neonicotinoid seed treatments. Compounds entering the **phloem** travel **source → sink** to roots, tubers, fruits and growing points, which is how sulfonylurea herbicides and aphid-transmitted viruses reach their targets (streams in [03 — Xylem and phloem](03-xylem-and-phloem.md)).

**Drought-resistance breeding is largely stomatal and root engineering.** Selection targets include **lower stomatal density**, faster ABA-induced closure (**higher ABA sensitivity**), a thicker cuticle and **deeper rooting** to reach subsoil water. Excessive closure cuts yield, so breeders aim at *optimised* rather than *maximal* closure.

**Cavitation is how drought kills trees.** Embolism accumulates → conductance falls → leaf Ψ drops → stomata close → photosynthesis ceases → reserves deplete → hydraulic failure plus starvation. Each year's conduit dimensions are written into the ring — wide **earlywood** in wet spring, narrow **latewood** in late summer — and vessel size, density and stable isotopes (δ¹⁸O, δ¹³C) are read back as a **hydrological archive** of past droughts (rings in [02 — Roots, stems and leaves](02-roots-stems-and-leaves.md)). **The water cycle is biological too**: transpiration returns on the order of 7 × 10⁴ km³ of water to the atmosphere each year — several times the annual discharge of all the world's rivers — and stomata also admit pollutants, since **ozone, SO₂ and NO₂ diffuse in through the same pores that admit CO₂** and damage mesophyll.

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Roots push water up the tree like a pump" | The dominant mechanism is **pull**: evaporation creates tension at the leaf. Root pressure (≤ 0.3–0.5 MPa) matters only in small plants at night and cannot lift water 100 m against ~1 MPa of gravity. |
| "Transpiration and guttation are the same" | Transpiration is **vapour lost through stomata**, driven by evaporation and tension; guttation is **liquid sap exuded from hydathodes**, driven by root pressure at night, and it contains solutes. |
| "Adding solute raises the water potential" | Solute **lowers** Ψ: Ψs is always **≤ 0**, so more solute means a *more negative* Ψ and water moves toward it — never from low Ψ to high. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is the zero. |
| "Cohesion means water sticking to the xylem wall" | **Cohesion = water–water** (hydrogen bonds) and holds the column together; **adhesion = water–wall** and, with capillarity, stabilises it. Neither drives the flow. |
| "Xylem sap sits under positive pressure like a drinking straw" | Xylem is normally under **negative pressure (tension)**, loading walls in compression. Positive pressure occurs only in root pressure, guttation and embolism refilling. |
| "An embolism permanently destroys the plumbing" | Water **cross-flows around blockages through pits**, damaged segments are **compartmentalised**, and conduits can be **refilled** osmotically. Only when embolism outpaces repair does tissue die. |
| "Wilting means the plant is dying" | Wilting is **reversible turgor loss** — re-water and posture returns. It becomes permanent only beyond the **permanent wilting point** or after prolonged cavitation. |
| "Stomata open because photosynthesis makes sugar there" | Opening begins with **blue-light activation of H⁺-ATPase**, then K⁺ and anion import and osmotic water entry, modulated by circadian and CO₂ signals — not by guard-cell photosynthesis. |
| "A potometer measures transpiration" | It measures **water uptake**, which approximates transpiration only after correcting for growth, chemical use of water and cuticular loss. |
| "Stomata should just stay fully open" | CO₂ entry and water loss share one aperture, and over-opening risks runaway cavitation. Aperture is **optimised against water availability and VPD** — full open can destroy the xylem, full closed starves the plant. |

## Key facts

- **Ψ = Ψs + Ψp** (plus Ψg, Ψm): **Ψs always ≤ 0**, pure water at atmospheric pressure = **0**, water moves **high Ψ → low Ψ**, units **MPa** (0.1 MPa = 1 bar ≈ 1 atm).
- The gradient runs **soil (−0.01) → root (−0.3) → xylem (−0.5 to −2) → leaf wall (−1 to −3) → atmosphere (≈ −100 MPa)** — the steepest water potential gradient in biology.
- Pathway: **soil → root hair (osmosis) → cortex apoplast → Casparian strip forces symplastic passage through the endodermis → xylem → stem → leaf mesophyll walls → evaporation → diffusion out through the stoma**.
- The **Casparian strip** (suberin/lignin) blocks the apoplast, so every solute and the water following it must cross a membrane before entering the xylem — the selective checkpoint.
- **Cohesion–tension**: evaporation lowers Ψ → **tension (negative pressure)** forms → transmitted down the continuous column → **cohesion (H-bonds)** holds it together, **adhesion** stabilises it → water is pulled up with no pump and no ATP.
- Lifting 100 m costs only **+1 MPa** of Ψg, while the leaf-to-air drop is ≈ 100 MPa; **root pressure (≤ 0.3–0.5 MPa) cannot do it**, so tall trees must pull rather than push.
- **Cavitation** leaves an **embolism**; defences are **pit aspiration, redundant pit-paired pathways, compartmentalisation and osmotic refilling**. **Transpiration** (vapour via stomata) ≠ **guttation** (liquid sap via hydathodes, root pressure at night) ≠ dew.
- Guard cells are **kidney-shaped in dicots, dumb-bell in grasses**; opening = **blue light → H⁺-ATPase → hyperpolarisation → K⁺/Cl⁻/malate²⁻ in → water in → turgor up → pore bows open**; closure = **ABA** via PYR/PYL → SnRK2 → SLAC1 → K⁺ efflux → water out.
- Stomata run on a **circadian** programme as well as light and humidity, and the core trade-off is **CO₂ in vs H₂O out at about 1 : 100** — aperture is optimised, not maximised.
- Transpiration rises with **light, temperature, wind and VPD**, falls with **high humidity and drying soil**; the **potometer measures uptake, not strictly transpiration**.
- Uptake depends on **active ion loading into the xylem** (lowering Ψs so water follows), on **mycorrhizal hyphae** extending surface area, and above all on **transpiration** as the pulling force.
- **Wilting = reversible turgor loss** (permanent wilting point ≈ −1.5 MPa); **annual rings are a hydrological record**; drought death proceeds by **hydraulic failure (cavitation) plus carbon starvation**.

## Practice questions

**1. Pure water is assigned Ψ = 0. Dissolving sucrose in water makes the solution's water potential**

A. More positive, because solute adds potential energy
B. More negative, because solute binds water and lowers free water concentration
C. Unchanged, because pressure potential cancels the effect
D. Zero, because water potential is only meaningful inside cells

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Ψs is always ≤ 0 and grows more negative as solute rises, because hydration shells reduce free water molecules — the effect that drives osmosis toward the concentrated solution. Solute cannot make Ψ positive (A); pressure is a separate component (C); Ψ is referenced to pure water, not cells (D).

---

**2. Water moves from the soil to the atmosphere through a plant. The correct description of this flow is that it**

A. Runs from lower to higher Ψ, powered by root pressure
B. Runs from higher to higher Ψ along a positive pressure gradient
C. Runs from higher to lower Ψ, powered ultimately by evaporation at the leaf
D. Runs from lower to higher Ψ, powered by active transport in the xylem

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Water always moves down the Ψ gradient, and evaporation at the leaf creates that gradient (soil ≈ −0.01 MPa to air ≈ −100 MPa). Root pressure is far too small for tall trees (A), xylem holds no living pumps (D), and flow toward higher Ψ would need energy (B).

---

**3. The Casparian strip matters mainly because it**

A. Blocks the apoplast, forcing water and solutes across a membrane so xylem entry is selective
B. Prevents all water reaching the xylem
C. Generates root pressure by pumping water
D. Seals pit membranes to stop embolism spreading

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Suberin blocks apoplastic flow, so every solute and the water with it must cross a plasma membrane — the point where the plant controls what enters the xylem. It does not block water (B), has no pump (C), and pit sealing is a separate anti-embolism defence (D).

---

**4. A tree is 100 m tall, and gravitational potential rises by 0.01 MPa per metre. Which statement best explains how its water is lifted?**

A. Root pressure of about 10 MPa pushes the water up from below
B. Living xylem parenchyma pressurise the sap to about 1 MPa
C. Water becomes denser with height, removing the gravitational cost
D. Evaporation creates −1 to −5 MPa of tension at the leaf, and the ≈ 100 MPa leaf-to-air drop dwarfs the +1 MPa gravitational cost

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Lifting 100 m costs +1 MPa, easily covered by leaf tension of −1 to −5 MPa, while the drop from leaf wall to atmosphere is ≈ 100 MPa. Root pressure maxes at ~0.3–0.5 MPa (A); xylem is dead and under tension (B); water density is effectively constant (C).

**5. Blue light falls on a closed stoma at dawn. The first event in the opening sequence is**

A. Abscisic acid binding to PYR/PYL receptors in the guard cell
B. Activation of the guard cell H⁺-ATPase, pumping protons out and hyperpolarising the membrane
C. Efflux of K⁺ and malate²⁻ from the guard cell
D. Hydrolysis of the cell wall so the pore can widen mechanically

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Phototropins activate the H⁺-ATPase; hyperpolarisation then opens inward K⁺ channels, and solute plus water entry raises turgor to bow the cells apart. ABA-driven efflux (A, C) is closure, and the pore opens by turgor, not wall hydrolysis (D).

**6. In drought the plant produces abscisic acid, which closes stomata by**

A. Increasing solutes inside guard cells so water enters
B. Blocking the H⁺-ATPase so hyperpolarisation cannot occur
C. Triggering anion and K⁺ efflux so water leaves osmotically and turgor falls
D. Depositing suberin over the pore permanently

**Answer: C**

Explanation: ABA binds PYR/PYL receptors, releases SnRK2 kinases and opens SLAC1 anion channels, then outward K⁺ channels; solutes and water leave and the pore seals. Solute entry (A) is opening, B mislocates the signal, and D would be irreversible rather than hormonal.

**7. At dawn a student finds liquid droplets along the tips of grass leaves. The best explanation is that overnight**

A. Root pressure pushed xylem sap out through hydathodes while transpiration was low
B. Transpiration continued at a high rate through the night
C. Water vapour pumped out of the stoma condensed on the leaf edge
D. Mesophyll cells secreted water through the cuticle

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Overnight the stomata close while roots keep absorbing and loading ions; the resulting positive pressure forces xylem sap out of hydathodes — guttation. The solute-rich residue distinguishes it from dew (C), and it is pressure-driven, not evaporative (B).

**8. A potometer is used with a leafy shoot. The main caution is that it measures**

A. Transpiration alone, with no other process contributing
B. Cuticular water loss and nothing else
C. Water movement through the phloem
D. Water uptake, which differs from transpiration because some water is retained in growth, used in photosynthesis and lost cuticularly

**Answer: D**

Explanation: The instrument records volume absorbed by the shoot, so it approximates transpiration only after correcting for growth, chemical water use and non-stomatal loss. The flow measured is in the xylem, not the phloem (C), and cuticular loss alone (B) is minor.

**9. After a long drought a branch loses conductivity, and gas bubbles are found filling vessel lumens. The branch is suffering from**

A. Excess root pressure forcing water out of the vessels
B. Over-activity of the Casparian strip blocking ion entry
C. Cavitation that has produced embolised, non-conducting conduits
D. Increased cohesion preventing evaporation from the leaves

**Answer: C**

Explanation: When tension exceeds the column's local strength, often by air seeding through pit membranes, dissolved gas comes out of solution and fills the conduit: an embolism with zero conductance. Root pressure would relieve tension (A); B and D cannot reduce conductivity.

**10. A systemic insecticide applied to soil appears in the leaves within hours but never accumulates in the roots. The best explanation is that it**

A. Is phloem-mobile and therefore moves only toward sugar sinks
B. Is xylem-mobile and is carried acropetally in the transpiration stream
C. Is pumped into the phloem by guard cells
D. Cannot cross the Casparian strip and so never enters the vascular tissue

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Xylem-mobile compounds ride the transpiration stream root → shoot and accumulate in transpiring leaves — the basis of soil-applied systemic fungicides and neonicotinoids. Phloem-mobile ones go source → sink to roots and fruits (A); D would block systemic action altogether.
