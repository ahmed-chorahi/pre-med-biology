# Plant Reproduction and Alternation of Generations

## Why it matters

The first time you meet a plant life cycle it feels wrong: in animals meiosis makes gametes, in plants **meiosis makes spores**. That one swap is why botany sounds like a foreign language at first, and it is the only idea you must get exactly right — once **meiosis produces spores, not gametes**, the rest follows as consequence.

**Alternation of generations** is the concept: a plant body cycles between two independent multicellular forms, a **diploid sporophyte (2n)** and a **haploid gametophyte (n)**. Because both phases are multicellular, the divisions that build them have different jobs — **meiosis builds spores from the sporophyte; mitosis builds everything else, including the gametes**. Get that right and pollen, double fertilisation, seeds, fruit, and dormancy become predictions rather than facts. Tissue origins were set up in [01 — Plant cells and tissues](01-plant-cells-and-tissues.md) and [02 — Roots, stems, and leaves](02-roots-stems-and-leaves.md); the hormones acting on flowering, fruit set, ripening, and germination are in [05 — Plant hormones](05-plant-hormones.md).
## Alternation of generations

### The full life-cycle chain

```
SPOROPHYTE (2n, multicellular)
      │  MEIOSIS in sporangia   ← the ONLY reduction step
      ▼
SPORES (n, single-celled)
      │  MITOSIS
      ▼
GAMETOPHYTE (n, multicellular)
      │  MITOSIS  ← gametes are made mitotically, not by meiosis
      ▼
GAMETES (n): egg + sperm
      │  FERTILISATION (two n cells fuse)
      ▼
ZYGOTE (2n)  ──MITOSIS──▶  EMBRYO ──▶ SPOROPHYTE (2n)   cycle repeats
```

Three rules follow, and they are worth memorising as rules rather than facts:

1. **Meiosis produces spores**, inside structures called **sporangia** on the sporophyte.
2. **Mitosis produces gametes** — in plants only. The gametophyte is already haploid, so mitosis gives a haploid gamete with no change of chromosome number.
3. **Fertilisation restores diploidy**, and only fertilisation does — the zygote is the first cell of the new sporophyte.

### How this differs from animals

```
ANIMAL:  germ cell (2n) ──MEIOSIS──▶ gametes (n) ──fertilisation──▶ zygote (2n)
PLANT:   sporophyte (2n) ──MEIOSIS──▶ spores (n) ──mitosis──▶ gametophyte (n)
                                        ──mitosis──▶ gametes (n) ──▶ zygote (2n)
```

The animal route reduces and fuses in two steps. The plant route inserts **an entire multicellular haploid organism** between meiosis and fertilisation — that insertion is the whole conceptual difficulty, and the whole explanatory power. Meiosis still performs exactly the two jobs set out in [12 — Meiosis](../03-cellular-processes/12-meiosis.md): halving the genome so fusion does not double it forever, and generating variation by crossing over and independent assortment; plants simply delay fusion by a generation of growth.

| Stage | Ploidy | How it was produced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sporophyte | **2n** | Mitosis of the zygote |
| Spores | **n** | **Meiosis** of sporophyte cells in the sporangium |
| Gametophyte | **n** | Mitosis of the spore |
| Gametes | **n** | **Mitosis** inside the gametophyte |
| Zygote | **2n** | Fusion of two n gametes |

Whenever you are unsure which division is happening, ask: *did the chromosome number just change?* If it fell from 2n to n it was meiosis and the product is a spore; if it stayed the same it was mitosis.

A haploid phase is **directly exposed to selection** — no second copy masks a deleterious recessive, so harmful alleles are purged rather than hidden — but it carries no heterozygous buffer. Evolution has therefore **shifted the dominant, long-lived phase to the diploid sporophyte**, keeping the haploid phase short, protected, and dispersal-oriented.

## The trend across the plant groups

The lineages differ less in the presence of the two generations than in **which one dominates** and **how sperm reach the egg**:

| Group | Dominant generation | Gametophyte | Vascular tissue | Sperm delivery | Seeds / pollen | Flower / fruit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bryophytes** (mosses, liverworts) | **Gametophyte (n)** — the green plant you see | Large, free-living, photosynthetic | **Absent** | **Free-swimming flagellated sperm** — needs a film of water | **None** | **None** |
| **Ferns** | **Sporophyte (2n)** — the frond | Tiny heart-shaped **prothallus**, free-living and independent | Present | **Free-swimming flagellated sperm** — needs water | **None** (spores only) | **None** |
| **Gymnosperms** (conifers, cycads, ginkgo) | Sporophyte | **Reduced**, retained on and fed by the sporophyte | Present | **Pollen tube**; cycads and ginkgo still have flagellated sperm | **Pollen + ovules**, seeds "naked" on cone scales | **No flower, no true fruit** |
| **Angiosperms** | Sporophyte | **Reduced to the embryo sac** (7 cells, 8 nuclei) in the ovule | Present | **Pollen tube**; no swimming sperm at all | Pollen + ovules enclosed in an ovary | **Flower**, **double fertilisation**, **fruit** |

**Bryophytes.** The visible moss is the gametophyte; the sporophyte grows *out of* the fertilised archegonium as a dependent capsule on a stalk, and sperm must **swim** from antheridium to archegonium, tying the group to damp habitats. **Ferns — the flip.** The frond is the diploid sporophyte, bearing **sori** (sporangium clusters); spores germinate into the photosynthetic prothallus, still so small that swimming sperm need a film of water. **Gymnosperms — pollen, no flower.** **Pollen** carries the male gametophyte to the ovule instead of swimming, and the seed develops exposed on a cone scale — hence **"naked" seed**; cycads and ginkgo still make **flagellated sperm**. **Angiosperms.** The flower advertises the organs, **double fertilisation** builds embryo and food supply in one event, the **ovary ripens into a fruit**, and the gametophyte shrinks to a handful of cells inside the ovule.

### The two evolutionary trends

```
TREND 1: GAMETOPHYTE REDUCTION
  moss: dominant, free-living, photosynthetic
  fern: free-living, tiny but independent
  gymnosperm: retained on the sporophyte, dependent on it
  angiosperm: reduced to the embryo sac inside the ovule
      → the sporophyte increasingly houses and supports the gametophyte

TREND 2: FREEDOM FROM FREE WATER + EMBRYO PROTECTION
  bryophyte/fern: swimming sperm → habitat tied to moisture
  gymnosperm:     pollen tube to the ovule; seed with a coat
  angiosperm:     pollen tube + enclosed ovary → fruit; no water needed
```

These are the plant answers to the problems animals solved with internal fertilisation and the amniotic egg: **how to reproduce without environmental water, and how to protect the offspring while doing it**. They also explain the dominance of angiosperms — they occupy the driest ground because nothing in their cycle needs a film of water.

## The flower as the reproductive organ

A flower is a **modified shoot**: a short axis bearing four whorls of specialised leaves — so every part below is a leaf, transformed.

| Whorl | Part | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outer | **Sepals** (calyx) | Usually green, leaf-like | Protect the bud before opening |
| Second | **Petals** (corolla) | Coloured, scented, nectar-bearing | Attract pollinators |
| Third | **Stamens** (androecium) | **Anther** + **filament** | Male reproductive organ |
| Inner | **Carpels / pistils** (gynoecium) | **Stigma** + **style** + **ovary** | Female reproductive organ |

**The stamen.** The **anther** is the **microsporangium**: microsporocytes undergo **meiosis** to give **microspores**, each dividing mitotically into a **pollen grain** — the immature male gametophyte. The **filament** positions the anther where a pollinator or the wind will contact it. Chain: anther (2n) →meiosis→ microspores (n) →mitosis→ pollen (n).

**The carpel.** The **stigma** is the receptive landing surface, the **style** the tube the pollen tube must traverse, and the **ovary** the swollen base holding one or more **ovules**. Each ovule houses the **megasporangium** (nucellus) and, inside it, the **female gametophyte** — the **embryo sac** — formed like the pollen: a megaspore mother cell (2n) undergoes meiosis to give megaspores (n), one of which divides mitotically into an embryo sac of typically an **egg cell**, two synergids, three antipodals, and a **central cell with two polar nuclei**.

The location logic links back to [01 — Plant cells and tissues](01-plant-cells-and-tissues.md) and [02 — Roots, stems, and leaves](02-roots-stems-and-leaves.md): ovules sit inside the ovary at the centre of the floral axis, and all reproductive structures derive from **meristematic** tissue that can still be redirected towards a reproductive fate.

| Term | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Perfect (hermaphrodite)** | Has **both** stamens and carpels | Most fruit-tree blossoms |
| **Imperfect** | Lacks stamens **or** carpels | Hazel, birch, maize |
| **Complete** | All four whorls present | A wild rose |
| **Incomplete** | Missing at least one whorl | Grass florets |
| **Monoecious** ("one house") | Separate male and female flowers **on one plant** | Maize, hazel, squash |
| **Dioecious** ("two houses") | Male flowers on one plant, female on another | Holly, kiwifruit, asparagus |

The consequence is genetic: **dioecious species cannot self-fertilise at all**, so every seed comes from outcrossing — a built-in guarantee of heterozygosity. Monoecious species can self if pollen reaches a female flower on the same plant, though many stagger anther and stigma maturation (**dichogamy**) to favour crossing.

## Pollination and fertilisation

### Two words, two events

```
POLLINATION   = transfer of pollen from anther to a receptive stigma
                (a PHYSICAL transport event — no fusion, no chromosome change)
                       │
                       ▼
                pollen tube germinates, grows down the style
                       │
                       ▼
FERTILISATION = fusion of a sperm nucleus with the egg nucleus
                (n + n → 2n zygote)
```

**Pollination can occur with zero chance of fertilisation** — the wrong species' pollen, a sterile grain, a stigma that rejects it — and fertilisation can be studied independently of how the pollen arrived. Pollination is ecology; fertilisation is cell biology (recognition, tube growth, membrane fusion).

| Mode | Agent | Floral signals | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Abiotic — wind** | Air currents | No scent, nectar, or colour; **large exposed anthers**, feathery stigmas, huge pollen counts | Grasses, maize, oaks, birches, conifers |
| **Abiotic — water** | Water flow | Buoyant, smooth pollen | *Vallisneria*, seagrasses |
| **Biotic — insects** | Bees, moths, butterflies, flies, beetles | Colour, scent, **nectar** guides, landing platforms | Most showy flowers |
| **Biotic — birds** | Sunbirds, hummingbirds | Red/orange, tubular, dilute copious nectar, no scent | *Fuchsia*, *Aloe*, proteas |
| **Biotic — bats** | Bats | Pale, night-opening, strong musty odour | Baobab, saguaro cactus |

Wind pollination is wasteful — astronomical pollen counts, most grains lost — but needs no partner, so it is reliable in open habitats; biotic pollination trades that waste for **precision**. The animal relationship is **coevolutionary** in the sense used in [06 — Speciation](../06-evolution/04-speciation.md): nectar is a **sugar solution paid in energy** for pollen transport, and because the reward costs resources, selection favours **pollinator constancy** — signals that make a visitor deposit pollen on the same species. Some flowers deceive instead of paying, because the transport still happens.

### Pollen tube growth: sperm without swimming

A compatible grain on a stigma takes up water and extends a **pollen tube** down the style, driven by tip growth and guided chemically by the synergid cells and the egg. The tube carries the male gametes and bursts in the embryo sac:

```
pollen grain on stigma
   → germination and hydration
      → pollen tube grows down the style (chemically guided)
         → tube enters the embryo sac through the filiform apparatus
            → releases TWO sperm beside the egg and the central cell
               → fertilisation WITHOUT free-swimming flagellated sperm
```

Bryophyte and fern sperm retain **flagella** and must swim; cycads and ginkgo also make **flagellated sperm**, but even they are delivered by a pollen tube — a transitional state showing how swimming was replaced. In conifers and angiosperms the flagellum is lost: **the pollen tube is both vehicle and substitute for motility**, removing external water from the life cycle entirely.

### Double fertilisation in angiosperms

```
EMBRYO SAC:  egg cell  +  CENTRAL CELL (two polar nuclei, n + n)

SPERM 1 + EGG (n)           ──▶ ZYGOTE (2n)      ──mitosis──▶ EMBRYO
SPERM 2 + CENTRAL CELL (n+n) ──▶ ENDOSPERM (3n)   ──mitosis──▶ food tissue
```

Both sperm arrive in one pollen tube and both fusions happen within minutes. The primary endosperm nucleus is **triploid** because it fuses with a central cell already holding two haploid nuclei.

**Why triploid endosperm is an adaptation, not an accident.** Building endosperm costs the mother plant carbon and nitrogen. Triploidy creates a **genomic dosage conflict that acts as a cross-validity filter**: endosperm growth depends on the balance of maternal-to-paternal allele expression (imprinted genes, as in mammals). If the cross is aberrant — selfed in an outcrossing species, or interspecific — the balance shifts, endosperm development fails, and the seed aborts *before* full resources are committed. The seed effectively **tests the cross by attempting triploid tissue first**, and the food supply stays tied to a genuine two-parent event.

## Seeds

A seed is the product of a fertilised ovule — three tissues, all consequences of the double event above:

| Seed component | Origin | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Embryo** | Zygote (2n) after mitosis | The immature sporophyte of the next generation |
| **Food store** | Endosperm (3n), often absorbed into the **cotyledons** before the seed matures | Energy and building blocks before photosynthesis begins |
| **Seed coat (testa)** | **Ovule integuments** (maternal tissue, 2n) | Physical protection; control of water and gas exchange |

Because the coat is maternal tissue while the embryo is a two-parent organism, a seed is a **dormant, protected, packaged embryo with a packed lunch** — the plant analogue of a shelled egg, plus dormancy.

### Dormancy and germination

**Dormancy** is an enforced pause: the seed will not germinate even in favourable conditions until an internal block is lifted, which adapts plants to seasonality — a seed shed in autumn must not sprout into the first frost. Cues that break it include **chilling, light (phytochrome reads the red:far-red ratio, so seeds under a canopy wait), after-ripening, and scarification** of a tough coat.

The hormone that executes germination is **gibberellin (GA)**, whose wider roles are in [05 — Plant hormones](05-plant-hormones.md):

```
imbibition (water uptake)
   → GA synthesised and released
      → GA induces the embryo to secrete hydrolytic enzymes (amylase, protease)
         → enzymes digest stored starch and protein in endosperm/cotyledons
            → sugars and amino acids released → fuel for the growing axis
               → radicle emerges → GERMINATION
```

**Abscisic acid (ABA)** enforces dormancy and opposes GA; the **GA:ABA ratio**, not the absolute level of either, decides whether the seed waits or grows.

### Seed versus spore

Students routinely treat a spore as a baby seed. It is not, and the difference *is* the alternation-of-generations distinction:

| Feature | **Spore** | **Seed** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A **single haploid cell** (n) | A **multicellular diploid embryo** (2n) + food store + coat |
| Produced by | **Meiosis** of the sporophyte | Development of the **fertilised ovule** — needs fertilisation |
| Grows into | A **gametophyte** by mitosis | A **sporophyte** by mitosis |
| Food supply | **None** — must photosynthesise at once or die | **Present** (endosperm or cotyledons) |
| Protection | Resistant wall (sporopollenin), no coat | **Seed coat** from ovule integuments |

A spore fends for itself as the first cell of the haploid generation; a seed is the diploid generation **already multicellular, provisioned, and armoured**, waiting for the right moment — embryo, food, coat, and dormancy arriving as one package.

## Fruits

After fertilisation the **ovary wall (pericarp)** grows into the **fruit**, the ovules become seeds, and the rest of the flower withers. Some fruits develop **without fertilisation** (**parthenocarpy** — seedless bananas and grapes), showing that fruit and embryo development are separable, hormone-controlled processes.

**Ripening** is a genuine developmental programme, not decay: starch → sugar conversion, acid breakdown, pigment change, volatile production, and cell-wall softening by pectinase. The trigger is **ethylene**, a gaseous hormone — which is why one ripening apple spoils a bowl: the gas diffuses and induces autocatalytic ethylene in its neighbours. Physiology is in [05 — Plant hormones](05-plant-hormones.md); the reproductive point is that **ripening is the stage that makes dispersal pay**.

| Dispersal mechanism | Fruit / seed adaptation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wind** | Dry, lightweight fruit; **wings** or **plumes**; dust-like seeds; or dried walls that build tension | Maple and ash **samaras**, dandelion **pappus**, orchid dust seeds |
| **Water** | Fibrous, buoyant, waterproof tissue with air spaces | Coconut, mangrove |
| **Animal ingestion** | **Fleshy, sweet, coloured pericarp**; a seed coat that survives gut passage and often germinates *better* after it | Berry, apple, cherry |
| **Explosive dehiscence** | Dry fruit drying unevenly and **flinging** seeds by mechanical tension | *Impatiens*, wood sorrel |
| **Ants (myrmecochory)** | **Elaiosome** — lipid-rich attachment ants carry home, discarding the seed | Woodland herbs, acacias |

Fleshy fruits are the angiosperm's contract with animals: sugar and pigment in exchange for transport, often with a scarified seed coat thrown in; dry fruits take the mechanical route. Both replace the bryophyte's reliance on water for spore dispersal with strategies that work in open, dry landscapes.

## Asexual and vegetative reproduction

Vegetative reproduction uses **somatic (2n) tissue** to make a new individual without meiosis or fertilisation. Because neither reduction nor fusion occurs, offspring are **genetically identical clones**.

| Method | Structure | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Runners (stolons)** | Horizontal above-ground stems | Nodes root into plantlets; clones stay linked until the runner senesces | Strawberry, couch grass |
| **Tubers** | Swollen underground stem | Stores starch; **eyes** are axillary buds that sprout | Potato |
| **Bulbs** | Reduced underground shoot, fleshy leaf bases | Scales store food; lateral buds form **bulblets** | Onion, garlic, tulip |
| **Cuttings** | Detached stem, leaf, or root | Wound-induced **adventitious root** formation from meristematic cells | Herbs, ornamentals |
| **Apomixis** | Seed-like structure | **A seed produced without fertilisation** — an embryo from an unreduced (2n) egg or ovule tissue: *dispersed as a seed, but cloned genetically* | Dandelion, some citrus, some grasses |

**Apomixis** is the conceptually interesting case: it decouples the seed from sexual reproduction, so the plant keeps dispersal, dormancy, and protection — the whole seed package — while producing clones. Cloning with a delivery system.

### The trade-off

```
SEXUAL (meiosis + fertilisation)
   cost:  gametes, pollinator or other expense, only some offspring survive
   gain:  RECOMBINATION — new allele combinations each generation
          → variation → raw material for natural selection (06 — Evolution)
          → heterozygosity masks deleterious recessives

ASEXUAL / VEGETATIVE
   cost:  NO variation — every ramet is a copy
   gain:  speed, no mate or pollinator needed, exact preservation of a proven genotype
          → one successful genotype colonises a whole field quickly
```

The value of recombination is set out in [01 — Variation, fitness, and adaptation](../06-evolution/01-variation-fitness-adaptation.md): selection can only act on variation that exists, and sexual populations generate fresh variation every generation while asexual ones do not. Clonal reproduction is a **short-term win and a long-term trap** — efficient while the environment is stable, catastrophic when it changes or a pathogen arrives.

### Genetic uniformity as a disease risk

A clone has no internal variation, so a pathogen genotype that infects one individual can infect **every** individual — there is no resistant variant in the field to slow the epidemic, which is why sexual reproduction persists despite its cost:

- **Potato** is propagated from tubers, so each cultivar is a clone. The **Irish potato famine (1845–1852)** spread through a genetically near-uniform crop: no resistant genotype existed, and failure of the single staple food had devastating demographic and political consequences.
- **Cavendish bananas** are grown worldwide from suckers — every commercial banana is a clone — which is why *Fusarium oxysporum* f.sp. *cubense* (Tropical Race 4) threatens the crop rather than being a manageable pest: there is no resistance to select for.
- Seedless fruits, grapes, and potatoes are kept clonally for the very reason that makes them vulnerable: the desirable genotype is preserved perfectly, along with everything else.

## Relevance to medicine and agriculture

**Pollination services are infrastructure.** Many food crops depend on animal pollinators — bees, hoverflies, moths, bats, birds — for fruit and seed set, worth hundreds of billions of dollars annually. **Pollinator decline** (habitat loss, pesticide exposure, pathogens such as *Varroa* mites carrying deformed wing virus in honeybees, floral-resource starvation) translates directly into yield loss and shortfalls in fruit- and nut-dependent diets; spread between managed and wild pollinators is studied with the tools of [07 — Microbiology](../07-microbiology/). Wind-pollinated staples (wheat, rice, maize) are exempt — part of why they are staples.

**Hybrid vigour, hybrid seed, and apomixis.** Crossing two inbred lines can yield an F1 with greater yield, uniformity, and stress tolerance than either parent — **heterosis**, which requires **heterozygosity** and therefore a sexual cross; proposed mechanisms include dominance of favourable alleles and overdominance. Commercial production exploits **incompatibilities**: male-sterile lines prevent selfing so the desired cross is guaranteed, and F1 seed is sold with an F2 that segregates, so farmers repurchase each season. If a hybrid's genotype could be set into seed without meiosis, farmers could replant seed and still get uniform plants — **fixing heterosis permanently** — which is why programmes are engineering **apomixis** into crops.

**Seed banks, cereal endosperm, and clonal crops.** Because a seed is a dormant, protected embryo, it can be stored cold and dry for decades: seed banks are **biodiversity insurance**, preserving landraces and wild relatives whose variation breeding needs against new pathogens and climates — the agricultural form of the argument in [06 — Evolution](../06-evolution/01-variation-fitness-adaptation.md). Wheat, rice, and maize are eaten as **endosperm** — white flour is milled endosperm, polished rice is endosperm — so cereal calories rest on double fertilisation: no legitimate fertilisation event, no grain. Potatoes, bananas, cassava, grapes, and most tree fruits are propagated vegetatively because the cultivar's genotype *is* the product.

**Pollen as an allergen.** Pollen proteins (such as **Bet v 1** from birch and **Lol p 1** from rye grass) are misidentified as dangerous by the immune system, so in sensitised individuals **IgE-mediated mast-cell degranulation** produces hay fever, allergic rhinitis, asthma, and conjunctivitis — a substantial burden in industrialised countries. The pattern follows the biology: symptoms track the **pollination season**, wind-pollinated species dominate exposure through sheer pollen numbers, and **cross-reactivity** runs between related species (birch–alder–hazel) and between pollen and food proteins (**oral allergy syndrome**: apple, celery, carrot in birch-sensitised patients). Management reduces exposure (filters, avoiding high-count days), blocks the IgE pathway (antihistamines), or desensitises it (allergen immunotherapy).

## Common confusions

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Meiosis makes gametes in plants as in animals" | **No.** In plants meiosis makes **spores**; gametes are made by **mitosis** in the haploid gametophyte. |
| "The green moss plant is the sporophyte" | The visible moss is the **gametophyte (n)**; the sporophyte is the dependent capsule-and-stalk growing out of it. |
| "Pollination and fertilisation are the same event" | **Pollination = transfer of pollen to the stigma**; **fertilisation = fusion of sperm and egg nuclei**. Pollination can occur with no fertilisation following. |
| "A spore is a baby seed" | A spore is a **single haploid cell** with no store that grows into a gametophyte; a seed is a **multicellular diploid embryo** with food and a coat that grows into a sporophyte. |
| "Endosperm is diploid" | In angiosperms the primary endosperm nucleus is **triploid (3n)**: sperm 2 (n) plus two polar nuclei (n + n). |
| "All plants need water for sperm to reach the egg" | Only bryophytes and ferns have swimming sperm; a **pollen tube** serves gymnosperms and angiosperms, though cycads and ginkgo retain flagellated sperm. |
| "Fruit is just the seed" | The **fruit develops from the ovary wall**; seeds develop from ovules — distinct structures, distinct origins. |
| "Vegetative reproduction is just slower sex" | It involves **no meiosis and no fertilisation** — offspring are clones, so there is no recombination and no new variation. |
| "Cloning a good genotype is always best" | It works while the environment is stable, but **genetic uniformity removes resistance variation** — the Irish potato famine and the threat to Cavendish bananas are the cautionary cases. |
| "Pollen tubes and swimming are alternative pollination methods" | Pollination (transport to the stigma) and sperm delivery (tube growth to the embryo sac) are separate steps; the tube replaces swimming *after* pollination. |

## Key facts

- **Alternation of generations**: plants cycle between a **diploid sporophyte** and a **haploid gametophyte**, both multicellular phases.
- **Meiosis makes spores; mitosis makes gametes** in plants — the reverse of animals, where meiosis makes gametes directly.
- The chain: sporophyte (2n) →meiosis in sporangia→ spores (n) →mitosis→ gametophyte (n) →mitosis→ gametes (n) →fertilisation→ zygote (2n) →mitosis→ sporophyte.
- Trend 1: the **gametophyte becomes reduced and dependent on the sporophyte** (moss dominant → fern free-living but tiny → gymnosperm retained → angiosperm embryo sac).
- Trend 2: **freedom from free water for sperm transfer, with increasing embryo protection** — swimming sperm (bryophytes, ferns, cycads, ginkgo) → pollen tube (conifers, angiosperms) → ovary and fruit.
- The **flower** bears sepals, petals, **stamens** (anther = microsporangium producing pollen; filament), and **carpels** (stigma, style, ovary; the ovule holds the megasporangium and embryo sac).
- **Perfect** flowers bear both sexes, **imperfect** lack one; **complete** have all four whorls; **monoecious** = both flower sexes on one plant, **dioecious** = on separate plants.
- **Pollination ≠ fertilisation**: transfer of pollen (wind, water, insects, birds, bats) versus fusion of gamete nuclei, delivered by a **pollen tube** with no free-swimming sperm in flowering plants.
- **Double fertilisation**: sperm 1 + egg → zygote (2n); sperm 2 + central cell → **endosperm (3n)** — a resource-allocation and cross-validity check before provisioning.
- A **seed = embryo + food store + protective coat** from the fertilised ovule; a **spore = one haploid cell** with no store that grows straight into a gametophyte.
- Germination is executed by **gibberellin** (induces amylase to digest stored starch) and opposed by **abscisic acid**; dormancy breaks on cold, light, or scarification cues.
- **Fruit develops from the ovary wall**; ripening is ethylene-triggered; fruit type matches the dispersal vector (wind, water, ingestion, explosion).
- **Vegetative reproduction** and **apomixis** yield clones — speed and exact copying at the cost of recombination, so **genetic uniformity is a disease risk** (Irish potato famine, Cavendish banana); agriculture rests on pollination services, hybrid seed, seed banks, cereal endosperm, and clonal propagation, while pollen is a major allergen.

## Practice questions

**1. In a fern, meiosis in the sporangium produces**

A. Spores
B. Gametes
C. Zygotes
D. Embryo sacs

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Meiosis halves the chromosome number, and in plants its product is the haploid **spore** — the first cell of the gametophyte. Gametes come later by mitosis (B); zygotes form only at fertilisation (C); the embryo sac is built mitotically from a megaspore (D).

---

**2. Which arrow chain correctly describes the plant life cycle?**

A. Sporophyte → mitosis → spores → meiosis → gametophyte → mitosis → gametes
B. Gametophyte → meiosis → spores → mitosis → sporophyte → mitosis → gametes
C. Sporophyte → meiosis → spores → mitosis → gametophyte → mitosis → gametes → fertilisation → zygote → mitosis → sporophyte
D. Sporophyte → mitosis → gametes → fertilisation → zygote → meiosis → spores

**Answer: C**

Explanation: The sporophyte makes spores by meiosis, spores grow into the gametophyte by mitosis, gametes are made mitotically, and fertilisation restores 2n before mitosis builds the next sporophyte. A puts meiosis at the wrong step, B reverses the generations, D is the animal pattern.

**3. The anther of a flowering plant is best described as**

A. A megasporangium producing the female gametophyte
B. A microsporangium in which meiosis produces microspores that become pollen
C. The ovary wall that ripens into fruit
D. The structure that receives pollen at its stigma

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The anther is the **microsporangium**, where microsporocytes undergo meiosis and each microspore divides mitotically into a pollen grain. The megasporangium lies in the ovule (A), the ovary wall becomes fruit (C), and the stigma sits atop the style (D).

**4. Which statement correctly corrects the claim that pollen grains swim to the ovule?**

A. Pollen grains are flagellated and swim down the style
B. Fertilisation occurs on the stigma, so no tube is needed
C. Only gymnosperms use pollen tubes
D. Pollination is transfer to the stigma; a pollen tube then grows down the style and delivers sperm without free-swimming cells

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Pollination ends at the stigma; the grain then germinates a **pollen tube** that releases sperm in the embryo sac, so no swimming stage exists in flowering plants. Pollen lacks flagella (A), fertilisation happens only inside the ovule (B), and gymnosperms use tubes too (C).

**5. Double fertilisation in angiosperms produces a zygote and**

A. A diploid endosperm
B. A second zygote
C. A triploid endosperm
D. A haploid embryo sac

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Sperm 1 (n) + egg (n) gives the **2n zygote**; sperm 2 (n) + central cell (n + n) gives **3n endosperm**. Endosperm is food tissue rather than an embryo (B), it is not diploid (A), and the embryo sac already exists as the female gametophyte (D).

**6. Triploid endosperm is considered a resource-allocation adaptation because it**

A. Lets the plant test a cross before committing full resources, since abnormal crosses disrupt the endosperm's allele balance and the seed aborts early
B. Doubles the embryo's chromosome number
C. Physically prevents self-fertilisation
D. Is found only in gymnosperms

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Endosperm growth depends on balanced maternal:paternal allele expression (imprinting), so an illegitimate or incompatible cross skews the balance, development fails, and the seed aborts before heavy investment. It does not change embryo ploidy (B), block selfing (C), or occur in gymnosperms (D).

**7. Which correctly contrasts a spore with a seed?**

A. A spore is diploid and multicellular; a seed is haploid and single-celled
B. A seed is a multicellular diploid embryo with a food supply and a coat; a spore is a single haploid cell that grows directly into a gametophyte
C. Both are produced by meiosis
D. A seed contains no food store, whereas a spore always does

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The seed holds a **2n embryo** made after fertilisation plus stored food and a coat; the spore is one **haploid cell** made by meiosis with no reserve, which grows into the gametophyte. A reverses the ploidies, C is wrong because seeds follow fertilisation, D inverts the food facts.

**8. In a fleshy fruit, the edible part develops from the**

A. Ovule integuments
B. Endosperm
C. Zygote
D. Ovary wall (pericarp)

**Answer: D**

Explanation: The **fruit develops from the ovary wall**, ripening (often under ethylene) to attract dispersers. The ovule integuments become the **seed coat** (A), endosperm is the seed's food tissue (B), and the zygote becomes the embryo (C).

**9. Gibberellin promotes seed germination chiefly by**

A. Thickening the seed coat
B. Halting water uptake during imbibition
C. Causing the embryo to secrete hydrolytic enzymes such as amylase that digest stored starch
D. Inducing abscisic acid synthesis

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Gibberellin induces **amylase and protease** in the endosperm or cotyledons, releasing sugars and amino acids that fuel the growing axis. Abscisic acid enforces dormancy (D), and GA promotes rather than blocks growth (A, B).

**10. A crop propagated entirely from tubers is devastated by a new fungal pathogen. This is best explained by**

A. Genetic uniformity of clones removing the resistant variants that selection would otherwise favour
B. Apomixis producing triploid embryos
C. Failure of double fertilisation in the tubers
D. Excessive meiosis creating harmful mutations

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Clones are genetically identical, so a pathogen that infects one plant infects all — no resistant individual limits spread, exactly the mechanism behind the Irish potato famine. Clonal propagation involves no meiosis (D), tubers bypass fertilisation (C), and apomixis is fertilisation-free seeding (B).
