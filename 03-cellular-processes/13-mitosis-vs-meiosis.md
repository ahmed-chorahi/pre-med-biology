# Mitosis vs Meiosis

## Why it matters

Two division processes, one cell, one set of shared machinery — and every exam in this area ultimately asks you to **tell them apart and say why the difference exists**. This chapter is the consolidation of the previous two: the comparisons stated side by side, the reasoning behind each difference, and the confusions retired.

Nothing new is introduced. If you cannot rebuild these tables from blank paper, reread chapters 11 and 12 rather than re-reading the tables.

## The one-sentence difference

**Mitosis preserves; meiosis reshuffles.** Mitosis makes two daughters genetically identical to the parent and to each other (growth, repair, asexual reproduction); meiosis makes four genetically unique haploid cells (gametes).

## The master comparison

| Feature | **Mitosis** | **Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Growth, tissue renewal, repair | Production of gametes |
| **Number of divisions** | **One** | **Two** (I and II) |
| **DNA replication** | Before division (S phase) | **Once**, before meiosis I — none between I and II |
| **Homologues pair?** | **No** — homologues behave independently | **Yes** — synapsis, bivalents/tetrads |
| **Crossing over?** | **No** (rarely, and pathologically) | **Yes — prophase I, between non-sister chromatids** |
| **Metaphase alignment** | Individual chromosomes on the plate | **Bivalents (pairs) on the plate** (meiosis I); single chromosomes (meiosis II) |
| **What separates in the first anaphase** | **Sister chromatids** | **Homologues** (meiosis I); sisters only in meiosis II |
| **Number of daughter cells** | **2** | **4** |
| **Ploidy of daughters** | **2n** (same as parent) | **n** (half) |
| **Genetically identical to parent?** | **Yes** (barring mutation) | **No** — recombinant and independently assorted |
| **Occurs in** | Somatic cells (and germ line for proliferation) | **Germ line only** |
| **Cytokinesis** | Once | Twice |
| **Typical duration** | Short (30–60 min for M phase) | Long — **prophase I alone can take days/years of arrest** |
| **Variation produced** | None (clones) | **Independent assortment + crossing over + random fertilisation** |
| **Key enzyme/motor event** | Cohesin cleaved by separase (APC/C) | Same, plus **Spo11** recombination in prophase I |

## Why each difference exists

Comparison tables are only durable when each row has a *reason*. Here is the reasoning, row by row:

| Difference | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Two divisions** | One replication + two divisions = halve without halving the DNA twice. Replicate once, split twice: 2n → n. |
| **Homologue pairing (synapsis)** | Crossing over requires physical juxtaposition; mitosis has no reason to compare maternal and paternal copies and many reasons not to (damage to one could spread). |
| **Crossing over** | Generates recombinant chromosomes — new allele combinations for natural selection to act on (06 — Evolution). |
| **Independent assortment** | Which homologue goes where is *random* — maximises combinatorial output; mitosis needs *exact copies*, so randomness would be a defect. |
| **Sisters stay joined in anaphase I** | If sisters separated in I, the reduction would never happen — homologues and sisters would all scatter. Holding sisters keeps each homologue intact as a unit until division II. |
| **Four haploid cells** | A gamete must be n so that fusion restores 2n — the entire basis of sexual life cycles. |
| **Germ-line restriction** | A somatic cell with half a genome cannot function; reduction is only meaningful for cells destined to fuse. |

**The unifying statement:** *every difference between the two divisions follows from whether the goal is identical copies or novel combinations.*

## Chromosome counting — the skill exams test

Count **centromeres**, not chromatids:

```
CELL AT METAPHASE OF MITOSIS (2n = 4)

   ──╂──   ╫──   ──╫──   ╫──
              4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids

CELL AT METAPHASE I (2n = 4)

   ══╤══ ══╤══      ══╤══ ══╤══
      bivalent            bivalent
   4 chromosomes (as 2 bivalents), 8 chromatids, 2n = 4

CELL AT METAPHASE II (n = 2)

   ──╫──   ╫──
   2 chromosomes, 4 chromatids, n = 2

AFTER MEIOSIS II

   ──╂──  ──╂──  ──╂──  ──╂──
   4 cells × 1 chromosome, 1 chromatid each, n = 2
```

**Rules:**

1. A chromosome = one centromere, regardless of how many chromatids hang off it.
2. Two chromatids joined = still **one** chromosome (a *duplicated* one).
3. After sister separation, each chromatid is its own chromosome — **the count doubles at anaphase of mitosis/meiosis II**, and this is correct, not an error.

**Common exam traps:**

- "How many chromosomes are in a cell after S phase?" → **Still 2n** — DNA doubled, chromosome count did not.
- "How many DNA molecules?" → doubles with replication; count chromatids for DNA molecules.
- "How many chromosomes in a gamete?" → n, one chromatid each.

## The errors compared

| | Mitotic error | Meiotic error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What fails** | Chromatid separation, spindle checkpoint, cytokinesis | Homologue separation, synapsis, chromatid separation |
| **Result** | **Mosaic aneuploidy** — only the daughter lineage affected | **Full aneuploidy in every cell** of the conceptus (trisomy/monosomy) |
| **Typical outcome** | Local tissue abnormality; cancer | Systemic chromosome disorder or early miscarriage |
| **Example** | Binucleate tumour cell, tumour with variable chromosome numbers | Down syndrome, Turner syndrome |

**Timing decides the blast radius:** a mitotic error is *private* to a cell lineage; a meiotic error is *constitutional* — present from conception in every cell, which is why chromosomal disorders are systemic and typically present at birth.

## Where they intersect

- **Both use the same core machinery** — spindle microtubules, kinetochores, cohesin/separase, APC/C, actin–myosin cytokinesis. Meiosis did not invent a new machine; it *modified the control logic* of the existing one (add synapsis, add recombination, change what separates first, run twice).
- **Meiosis II is structurally a mitosis** in haploid cells — the closest relative of the two processes.
- **Errors in either** feed aneuploidy, but only meiotic ones are inherited constitutionally.
- **Germ-line cells first proliferate by mitosis** (spermatogonia dividing to maintain the pool), then switch to meiosis — one cell type, both processes, in sequence.

## Common confusions — final list

| Confusion | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| "Meiosis I is like mitosis" | **They are the opposite**: mitosis separates sisters; meiosis I separates homologues. Meiosis **II** resembles mitosis. |
| "A cell doubles its chromosome number in S phase" | DNA doubles; **chromosome count (centromeres) does not**. |
| "Crossing over creates new genes" | It creates new **combinations** of existing alleles — no new alleles arise (that is mutation, 05 — Molecular Biology). |
| "Mitosis produces variation" | It produces **clones**; variation enters through mutation and meiosis only. |
| "Four products means four sperm in oogenesis" | Oogenesis yields **one ovum + three polar bodies**. |
| "Homologues pair in mitosis" | They do not — mitotic cells ignore homology entirely. |
| "Meiosis halves the genome twice" | Replication happens once; **two divisions** undo it once. Net: halved. |

## Medical relevance, consolidated

- **Chromosomal disorders** (Down, Edwards, Patau, Turner, Klinefelter) are **meiotic nondisjunction** — screened in pregnancy, confirmed by karyotype.
- **Infertility** often reflects meiotic failure — failed synapsis, failed recombination, meiotic arrest; assessed directly in spermatocyte preparations and sperm FISH.
- **Miscarriage** is dominated by aneuploid conceptions — most chromosomal errors never reach live birth.
- **Cancer** is dominated by **mitotic errors** — checkpoint failure, multipolar spindles, binucleation → variable aneuploid clones that evolve under therapy.
- **Mosaicism** — a mitotic error early in development → two cell lines in one person, with features depending on which tissues carry which line.

## Key facts

- **Mitosis: 1 division, 2 identical diploid cells, no pairing, no crossing over, sisters separate first.**
- **Meiosis: 1 replication + 2 divisions, 4 unique haploid cells, synapsis and crossing over, homologues separate first.**
- **Meiosis I = reductional** (homologues part); **meiosis II = equational** (sisters part, like mitosis).
- Variation = **independent assortment (2²³)** + **crossing over** + **random fertilisation**.
- Count chromosomes by **centromeres**; S phase doubles DNA, not chromosomes.
- Mitotic errors → mosaicism and cancer; meiotic errors → constitutional aneuploidy, miscarriage, and named chromosome disorders.
- Meiosis reuses mitotic machinery with altered control logic — **add pairing, add recombination, delay sister separation, run twice.**

## Practice questions

**1. The most fundamental difference between mitosis and meiosis is that**

A. Meiosis requires more ATP
B. Meiosis separates homologous chromosomes and produces haploid cells with new combinations of alleles; mitosis separates sister chromatids and produces identical diploid cells
C. Mitosis occurs only in plants
D. Meiosis involves three divisions

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Everything else follows from this: homologue pairing and crossing over (meiosis only), one versus two divisions, two versus four cells, diploid versus haploid output, clones versus variants. Energy use (A), organism (C), and division count (D) are incidental details, not the defining distinction.

---

**2. A cell with 2n = 46 chromosomes at the start of meiosis will contain how many chromosomes after meiosis I?**

A. 46
B. 23, each with two chromatids
C. 23, each with one chromatid
D. 92

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Meiosis I separates homologues, halving the chromosome number to 23 — but sister chromatids are still joined, so each chromosome is still duplicated. Option A would be mitosis's outcome for chromosome count; C is the state after meiosis II; D counts chromatids, not chromosomes.

---

**3. Crossing over contributes to genetic variation by**

A. Increasing chromosome number
B. Exchanging segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, creating recombinant chromosomes
C. Mutating DNA sequences randomly during replication
D. Occurring between sister chromatids during mitosis

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Physical exchange between maternal and paternal chromatids in prophase I produces chromosomes carrying allele combinations found in neither parent — the shuffling step of meiosis. It does not change chromosome number (A), creates combinations rather than new mutations (C), and does not occur in mitosis (D).

---

**4. During anaphase of mitosis versus anaphase I of meiosis, what separates?**

A. Mitosis separates homologues; meiosis I separates sisters
B. Both separate sister chromatids
C. Mitosis separates sister chromatids; meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes
D. Neither separates anything — both are arrested

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Mitosis pulls sisters apart (each becomes a chromosome in a daughter cell). Meiosis I pulls homologues apart while sisters remain attached — the reductional step. Meiosis II, not meiosis I, separates sisters — which is why "meiosis is like mitosis" is true only of its second division.

---

**5. Which feature is present in meiosis but absent in mitosis?**

A. Spindle formation
B. Synapsis of homologous chromosomes and crossing over
C. Cytokinesis
D. Cohesin cleavage by separase

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Pairing into bivalents and exchanging segments are unique to prophase I — they exist to generate variation and to allow reductional segregation. Spindle formation, cytokinesis, and cohesin cleavage are shared machinery used by both processes (though timing differs).

---

**6. Why must gametes be haploid?**

A. Haploid cells are larger
B. So that fusion of two gametes at fertilisation restores the diploid number, maintaining chromosome constancy across generations
C. Because diploid cells cannot swim
D. So that crossing over can occur after fertilisation

**Answer: B**

Explanation: If gametes were diploid, each generation would double the genome — 2n → 4n → 8n. Halving at each generation and restoring at fertilisation keeps the species' chromosome number constant. Cell size and motility are irrelevant, and crossing over precedes fertilisation, in meiosis I.

---

**7. A zygote is found with 47,XX,+21. Which process produced the abnormal gamete?**

A. Mitotic error in the embryo
B. Meiotic nondisjunction producing a gamete with 24 chromosomes
C. Failure of DNA replication
D. Crossing over between chromosome 21 and a sex chromosome

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Trisomy requires a gamete carrying an extra chromosome 21 (n+1 = 24) fusing with a normal gamete — the product of nondisjunction in meiosis I or II. A mitotic error after fertilisation produces mosaicism (two cell lines), not a uniform constitutional trisomy; replication failure and crossing over do not add whole chromosomes.

---

**8. Compared with mitosis, meiosis produces genetic variation through all of the following EXCEPT:**

A. Independent assortment of homologues
B. Crossing over in prophase I
C. Random fusion of gametes at fertilisation
D. Exact copying of the parental genome

**Answer: D**

Explanation: Exact copying is mitosis's output — clonal propagation. Independent assortment, crossing over, and random fertilisation are the three combinatorial engines of sexual variation. The exception format asks you to recognise that D is the mitotic, not meiotic, product.

---

**9. Meiosis II is most similar to mitosis because it**

A. Separates sister chromatids and requires no DNA replication beforehand
B. Separates homologous chromosomes
C. Occurs only in germ cells
D. Produces four cells

**Answer: A**

Explanation: Both mitosis and meiosis II split sister chromatids using the same cohesin/separase machinery, and meiosis II proceeds without an intervening S phase (as mitosis does after its own pre-cycle replication). Homologue separation is meiosis I's distinction; both B and C are true statements about meiosis generally but do not explain the similarity to mitosis; D describes meiosis as a whole.

---

**10. Which statement best explains why mitotic errors tend to cause cancer while meiotic errors tend to cause chromosome disorders at birth?**

A. Mitosis occurs more slowly
B. Mitotic errors affect a single cell lineage and can spread clonally within a tissue; meiotic errors are present from conception in every cell of the organism
C. Meiotic errors cannot survive at all
D. Cancer is always inherited

**Answer: B**

Explanation: A mitotic mistake is initially private — the aberrant daughter and its descendants populate one tissue, and further mutations can build a tumour clone. A meiotic mistake creates the zygote's genotype itself, so every cell carries it from the start — a constitutional disorder visible at birth (or a lethal loss causing miscarriage). Both are chromosome-level errors; timing and scope differ.
