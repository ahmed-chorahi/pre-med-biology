# ATP and Metabolism

## Why it matters

Every pump, motor, and biosynthetic reaction in this course spends **ATP**. This chapter explains what ATP is, why it works as an energy currency, and how the cell's two great metabolic directions — **catabolism** breaking things down and **anabolism** building things up — are coupled through it. The five chapters that follow walk through the pathways that *make* ATP; this one gives you the rules those pathways obey.

## The energy problem

Life runs on work that is energetically unfavourable: building proteins, pumping ions, moving muscles. The cell cannot use heat for any of it — heat does not push reactions in a chosen direction. What it needs is a molecule that can:

1. **Store energy in a usable chemical form**
2. **Release that energy in controlled, enzyme-catalysed steps**
3. **Transfer energy between reactions** — spending it here, earning it there

**ATP does all three**, which is why it is the universal energy currency.

## ATP structure and the source of its energy

**Adenosine triphosphate** = adenine + ribose + **three linked phosphates**.

```
        adenine ── ribose ── P ~ P ~ P
                              │      │
                          γ-phosphate (terminal, the one usually spent)
                              β
                              α
```

The energy is in the **phosphoanhydride bonds** between phosphates — often called "high-energy bonds." That phrase is misleading and worth retiring: **the bonds are not unusually strong; they are unusually *unstable*.** Several factors make their hydrolysis strongly exergonic:

- **Electrostatic repulsion** — the three phosphate groups are all negatively charged and crowded; releasing one relieves that strain.
- **Resonance stabilisation of the products** — free phosphate (Pᵢ) and ADP are better stabilised than the intact triphosphate.
- **Product hydration** — the released products are better hydrated than the reactant.
- **Entropy** — one molecule becomes two, increasing disorder.

**The correct mental statement:** *ATP hydrolysis releases energy because the products are more stable than the reactant — not because the bond is a loaded spring.* The same chemistry applies to all phosphoanhydride bonds: phosphocreatine, 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, and the others all work for the same reasons.

### The numbers

```
ATP + H₂O ──▶ ADP + Pᵢ        ΔG ≈ −30.5 kJ/mol  (under standard conditions)
```

In a cell (non-standard conditions — concentrations, Mg²⁺, pH), the actual figure is nearer **−50 to −54 kJ/mol**, and it is that *larger* value the cell exploits.

**Why this particular molecule?** A currency must be **intermediate** in energy — high enough to drive most reactions when spent, not so high that it cannot be regenerated by ordinary metabolism. ATP sits exactly in the middle of the phosphorylated-molecule hierarchy:

```
more energy stored        phosphoenolpyruvate
     ▲                     1,3-bisphosphoglycerate
     │                     phosphocreatine
     │                     ATP  ◀── the currency: mid-range
     │                     glucose-6-phosphate
     │                     glycerol-3-phosphate
less energy stored        glucose
```

**Transfer runs downhill through this ladder.** A molecule above ATP can phosphorylate ADP to ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation); ATP can phosphorylate anything below it. **ATP is the middle of the road, so traffic flows through it in both directions.**

## Energy coupling

Exergonic reactions pay for endergonic ones **by sharing an intermediate**, not by magic:

```
A ──▶ B          ΔG > 0   (unfavourable, will not run)

coupled:
A + ATP ──▶ B + ADP + Pᵢ      (overall ΔG < 0 — runs)
```

The coupling is physical: ATP transfers a phosphate to the substrate, making a new intermediate that is now energetically downhill. **The enzyme is what couples them** — by holding both reactions in one active site sequence.

This is exactly how the **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** of the last chapter works: ATP phosphorylates the pump, the phosphate changes the pump's shape, and the shape change moves the ions. **Energy → covalent intermediate → mechanical work.** The same three-step pattern appears in muscle contraction, vesicle movement, and biosynthesis.

**Metabolism, defined:** the totality of a cell's chemical reactions, divided into

| | **Catabolism** | **Anabolism** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | Break down | Build up |
| Energy | **Releases** (exergonic) | **Requires** (endergonic) |
| Examples | Glucose → CO₂; fatty acid → acetyl-CoA | Amino acid → protein; glucose → glycogen |
| O₂ | Usually consumes (when aerobic) | Usually consumes reducing power |
| Coupling | **Produces ATP, NADH** | **Spends ATP, NADPH** |

**The two are coupled through ATP** (and NADPH for biosynthesis): catabolism generates the currency, anabolism spends it. That is the entire logic of metabolism in two lines.

## ATP yield and the bookkeeping

One molecule of glucose fully oxidised:

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ ──▶ 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O     ΔG ≈ −2,870 kJ/mol
```

But the cell does not burn glucose in one step — that would release the energy as heat and destroy the cell. Instead it harvests it **in ~30 transfers**, capturing roughly **30–32 ATP per glucose** (the modern figure; older texts say 36–38, which ignored the cost of transporting NADH and ATP across membranes).

| Stage | Location | ATP (direct) | ATP (from NADH/FADH₂) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Glycolysis | Cytoplasm | 2 net | 2 (or 3, depending on shuttle) |
| Pyruvate oxidation | Mitochondrial matrix | 0 | 2 |
| Citric acid cycle | Mitochondrial matrix | 2 (GTP) | 22 |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Inner membrane | — | (carried here) |
| **Total** | | **~30–32 ATP per glucose** | |

**Why not capture 100%?** Each transfer is imperfect and some energy is inevitably released as heat — and a small amount of heat is not waste in a warm-blooded animal, it is **thermoregulation**. Metabolism is not a machine designed for efficiency alone; it is designed for *control*.

## Free energy, stated once, properly

Every pathway in the next four chapters obeys the same statement:

- **Exergonic (ΔG < 0):** releases free energy; can run spontaneously *if a kinetic pathway exists* — which is what enzymes provide.
- **Endergonic (ΔG > 0):** requires energy input; will not run alone.

**ΔG tells you whether a reaction *can* run; it says nothing about *how fast.*** A reaction can be thermodynamically favourable and kinetically frozen without an enzyme — the distinction that makes enzymes necessary at all ([05 — Enzymes](../01-biochemistry/05-enzymes.md)).

**Pathway direction in practice** is controlled by three levers:

1. **Mass action** — product removal pulls a reaction forward.
2. **Irreversible enzymes** — the committed steps, regulated.
3. **Coupling** — a favourable reaction linked to an unfavourable one.

## Metabolic states: when the body switches fuels

The pathways ahead are always running in some proportion, and the proportions shift with feeding state:

| State | Dominant fuel | Key events |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fed (absorbed)** | Blood glucose | Insulin up; glucose used immediately, excess stored as **glycogen** then **fat** |
| **Post-absorptive (early fasting)** | Glycogen + some fat | **Glycogenolysis** maintains blood glucose; glucagon rises |
| **Fasting / starvation** | **Fat, then ketones** | Glycogen stores last ~24 h; fat oxidation takes over; brain adapts to **ketone bodies** |
| **Prolonged starvation** | Fat + ketones; some protein early only | Protein sparing — continued muscle breakdown would be fatal |

**Why this table belongs before the pathways:** each pathway you learn has a *moment when it matters*. Glycolysis dominates in the fed state; β-oxidation dominates in fasting; gluconeogenesis runs when glycogen is gone. Regulation of the pathways (which this section repeatedly returns to) is really the question of *which state the cell thinks it is in*.

## Where the pathways live

```
CYTOPLASM          glycolysis · gluconeogenesis · fatty acid synthesis · pentose pathway

MITOCHONDRIUM      pyruvate oxidation · citric acid cycle · β-oxidation ·
                   oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane) · ketogenesis

ER (smooth)        lipid and steroid synthesis · detoxification
CHLOROPLAST        photosynthesis

PEROXISOME         very-long-chain fatty acid oxidation · H₂O₂ handling
```

**Compartmentalisation is regulation.** Separating pathways lets the cell run opposing reactions simultaneously — fatty acid synthesis in the cytoplasm while β-oxidation runs in the mitochondrion — without the two cancelling each other. This is the endomembrane logic from 02 — Cell Biology applied to chemistry.

## The carriers you will meet in every pathway

| Carrier | Carries | Reduced form | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NAD⁺** | 2 electrons + H⁺ (as hydride) | **NADH** | Catabolism → ETC (complex I) |
| **FAD** | 2 electrons (one at a time) | **FADH₂** | Catabolism → ETC (complex II) |
| **NADP⁺** | 2 electrons | **NADPH** | **Anabolism** — reductive biosynthesis; also glutathione defence |
| **CoA** | Acyl (2-carbon) groups | Acetyl-CoA | Fuel entry to the cycle |

**The NADH vs NADPH division of labour is a favourite exam point:** catabolic pathways make **NADH** for energy; anabolic pathways spend **NADPH** for reducing power. Nearly identical molecules, deliberately separate pools, so that oxidation for energy does not get confused with reduction for building.

## Medical relevance

**ATP failure is the final common pathway of cell injury.** Ischaemia, cyanide, and mitochondrial disease all converge on the same result: pumps stop (cell swells), Ca²⁺ rises (enzymes activate), and the cell dies. Every subsequent chapter of pathology runs through this sentence.

**Heat from metabolism is measurable.** Thermogenesis — including **uncoupled respiration in brown fat** (chapter 07) — is deliberate ATP hydrolysis-as-heat, and fever increases metabolic rate roughly 10% per °C. The inefficiency of the coupling is, in places, the point.

**Metabolic states explain clinical presentations.** Diabetic ketoacidosis is a starvation state in the *fed* patient — no insulin means the body behaves as though fasting despite high glucose. Fasting hypoglycaemia in a child after a night without food is simply the glycogen store running out. Each is a row of the metabolic-state table failing.

**Why tumours glucose-hungry:** many cancers run glycolysis hard even with oxygen available (**Warburg effect**) — a shift you will recognise fully after chapter 05 — and that reliance is what makes **FDG-PET scanning** work: radioactive glucose analogue accumulates wherever glycolysis is extreme.

## Key facts

- ATP = adenine + ribose + triphosphate; energy comes from **electrostatic repulsion and product stability**, not from a "stretched" bond.
- ΔG°′ of ATP hydrolysis ≈ **−30.5 kJ/mol** (≈ −50 in cells); ATP is deliberately **mid-range** on the phosphorylated-molecule ladder so it can accept from high-energy donors and donate to low-energy acceptors.
- **Energy coupling** works by transferring a phosphate through an enzyme-bound intermediate — ATP + substrate → phosphorylated substrate + ADP.
- **Catabolism** breaks down and releases energy (makes ATP/NADH); **anabolism** builds and spends energy (uses ATP/NADPH); ATP links them.
- Glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O releases ≈ −2,870 kJ/mol; the cell captures it as **~30–32 ATP** through many controlled steps.
- **ΔG predicts direction, not rate** — enzymes supply the rate.
- Metabolic states: fed (glucose, storage) → post-absorptive (glycogen) → fasting (fat, ketones) → starvation (fat + ketones, protein spared).
- **NADH = catabolism/energy; NADPH = anabolism/reduction.** Separate pools on purpose.
- Pathways are separated by compartment; opposing pathways can then run at once.

## Practice questions

**1. The energy released by ATP hydrolysis is best explained by**

A. The phosphoanhydride bonds being unusually strong
B. Greater stability of the products (ADP + Pᵢ) through charge repulsion relief, resonance, and hydration
C. The adenine ring storing energy
D. Hydrolysis being endergonic

**Answer: B**

Explanation: The "high-energy bond" label is misleading — the bonds are not unusually strong, they are unusually *unfavourable to keep*. Three crowded negative charges repel; products are resonance-stabilised and better hydrated; entropy increases as one molecule becomes two. D is backwards: hydrolysis is exergonic.

---

**2. Why is ATP described as the universal energy currency rather than glucose?**

A. Glucose cannot be broken down
B. ATP is mid-range in transfer potential, so it can both accept energy from higher-energy donors and donate it to lower-energy reactions in controlled steps
C. ATP stores more total energy per gram than glucose
D. Glucose is not found inside cells

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Glucose stores more energy per molecule but cannot donate that energy to an arbitrary reaction — it must be oxidised in steps. ATP sits in the middle of the phosphorylated-molecule hierarchy, so group-transfer reactions flow through it in both directions. Per gram, fat and glucose store far more energy than ATP; the cell keeps only a small, rapidly turned-over ATP pool.

---

**3. Catabolism and anabolism are coupled in the cell principally through**

A. Water
B. ATP (and NADH/NADPH)
C. Oxygen only
D. DNA

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Catabolism releases energy captured as ATP and reduced carriers; anabolism spends ATP and NADPH. The currency links the two so that breaking down fuels pays for building macromolecules. Water and oxygen participate in reactions but do not transfer usable energy between pathways, and DNA stores information, not metabolic energy.

---

**4. Which statement about ΔG is correct?**

A. A negative ΔG means the reaction will proceed quickly
B. A positive ΔG reaction can occur if coupled to a sufficiently exergonic one
C. ΔG predicts the rate of a reaction
D. Endergonic reactions do not occur in cells

**Answer: B**

Explanation: ΔG describes thermodynamic feasibility; rate is a kinetic question answered by enzymes. Coupling can make the *combined* ΔG negative so an otherwise unfavourable reaction proceeds — the basis of ATP-driven pumps and biosynthesis. A and C confuse thermodynamics with kinetics; D is false since biosynthesis is largely endergonic.

---

**5. Which pairing of carrier with metabolic role is correct?**

A. NADPH — oxidative phosphorylation
B. NADH — reductive biosynthesis
C. NADPH — reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defence
D. FAD — fatty acid synthesis only

**Answer: C**

Explanation: NADPH supplies reducing power for biosynthesis (fatty acid, cholesterol synthesis) and for regenerating glutathione against oxidative damage. NADH carries electrons from catabolism to the electron transport chain. FAD is used in catabolic oxidation (acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, Krebs cycle), not biosynthesis.

---

**6. After a 24-hour fast, which fuel source is most important for maintaining blood glucose?**

A. Continued intestinal absorption
B. Glycogenolysis, followed by gluconeogenesis as stores deplete
C. Protein breakdown exclusively
D. Blood glucose is maintained by fat directly

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Glycogen stores are the first line — liver glycogenolysis releases glucose — and last roughly a day; after that, gluconeogenesis (from glycerol, lactate, and amino acids) takes over. Fat cannot become glucose directly (its glycerol portion only, and acetyl-CoA cannot be net-converted to oxaloacetate in animals), so fat supports the brain indirectly via ketones.

---

**7. Why are opposing pathways placed in different compartments?**

A. To increase temperature inside organelles
B. So they cannot operate simultaneously
C. So they can run simultaneously without cancelling each other, and be regulated independently
D. Because all reactions require a membrane

**Answer: C**

Explanation: Fatty acid synthesis (cytoplasm) and β-oxidation (mitochondria) both run in a well-fed cell; separating them spatially means each can be regulated by its own enzymes and conditions rather than by mass action across a single pool. Compartmentalisation is a regulatory strategy, not a barrier to activity.

---

**8. The Warburg effect refers to tumour cells that**

A. Stop using glucose entirely
B. Take up large amounts of glucose and ferment it to lactate even when oxygen is plentiful
C. Cannot perform glycolysis
D. Oxidise only fat

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Many cancer cells run high glycolytic rates irrespective of oxygen availability — aerobic glycolysis — which is exploited diagnostically by FDG-PET (radioactive glucose analogue accumulates in glycolysis-hungry tumours). They certainly use glucose (A, C wrong) and rely on it heavily.

---

**9. Which is the correct overall equation for complete aerobic oxidation of glucose?**

A. Glucose → 2 lactate + 2 ATP
B. Glucose + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O, releasing energy captured as ~30–32 ATP
C. Glucose + 6 CO₂ → 6 O₂ + 6 H₂O
D. Glucose → 2 ethanol + 2 CO₂

**Answer: B**

Explanation: Complete oxidation consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water, with the released free energy captured in ~30–32 ATP. A is anaerobic glycolysis (fermentation to lactate), D is alcoholic fermentation, and C is the overall equation of photosynthesis reversed.

---

**10. Why does the cell keep only a small pool of ATP rather than storing energy as ATP?**

A. ATP is too unstable to store in granules
B. ATP is a currency with rapid turnover — it is made and spent continuously; long-term energy is stored as glycogen and fat
C. Cells cannot synthesise ATP
D. ATP would crystallise at high concentrations

**Answer: B**

Explanation: A currency circulates; it is not a warehouse. The ATP pool turns over many times per minute, so the cell needs only enough for seconds of demand, with pathways regenerating it continuously. Long-term reserves are glycogen (fast, small) and fat (slow, large). Instability (A) is a practical feature, not the reason — the reason is the distinction between currency and storage.
